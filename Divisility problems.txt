## Statement

1. Show that if $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$, then $d\mid a+b$ and $d\mid a-b$.
2. More generally, show that $d\mid ax+by$ for all integers $x,y$.
3. Find all positive divisors of $60$.

## Hint

For (1) and (2), write $a=dk$ and $b=d\ell$. For (3), factor $60$ or test divisibility systematically.

## Solution

**1.** If $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$, there exist integers $k,\ell$ with $a=dk$, $b=d\ell$. Then
$$a+b=d(k+\ell),\qquad a-b=d(k-\ell),$$
so $d\mid a+b$ and $d\mid a-b$.

**2.** With the same notation, $ax+by=dkx+d\ell y=d(kx+\ell y)$, so $d\mid ax+by$.

**3.** $60=2^2\cdot3\cdot5$. The positive divisors are
$$1,2,3,4,5,6,10,12,15,20,30,60.$$
There are $(2+1)(1+1)(1+1)=12$ of them.

**Remark.** Part (2) is the fundamental tool for all divisibility problems: any common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides every integer linear combination of $a$ and $b$.

## Statement

Find all integers $n$ such that $n-4$ divides $3n-17$.

## Hint

Write $3n-17=3(n-4)-5$ and factor.

## Solution

**Reduction.** We have
$$3n-17=3(n-4)-5.$$
So $n-4\mid 3n-17$ if and only if $n-4\mid 5$.

**Case analysis.** The divisors of $5$ are $\pm1,\pm5$. Hence
$$n-4\in\{-5,-1,1,5\},$$
giving
$$n\in\{-1,3,5,9\}.$$

**Verification.** For $n=-1$: $n-4=-5$ and $3n-17=-20$, and $-5\mid-20$. For $n=3$: $-1\mid-8$. For $n=5$: $1\mid-2$. For $n=9$: $5\mid10$. All work.

**Remark.** This is the standard technique for divisibility of linear expressions: reduce the dividend modulo the divisor to obtain a constant remainder.

## Statement

Show that if $n$ is an odd integer, then $8\mid n^2-1$.

## Hint

Factor $n^2-1=(n-1)(n+1)$ and observe that $n-1$ and $n+1$ are consecutive even integers.

## Solution

**Factorization.** $n^2-1=(n-1)(n+1)$.

**Parity argument.** Since $n$ is odd, both $n-1$ and $n+1$ are even. They are consecutive even integers, so one of them is divisible by $2$ and the other by $4$. Hence their product is divisible by $8$.

**Remark.** This is a classical parity result.

## Statement

Compute $\divisibility(8303,2717)$ using the Euclidean algorithm, and find integers $u,v$ such that
$$8303u+2717v=\divisibility(8303,2717).$$

## Hint

Perform successive Euclidean divisions and back-substitute.

## Solution

**Euclidean algorithm.**
\begin{align*}
8303 &= 3\cdot2717+152,\\
2717 &= 17\cdot152+133,\\
152 &= 1\cdot133+19,\\
133 &= 7\cdot19+0.
\end{align*}
The last nonzero remainder is $19$, so $\divisibility(8303,2717)=19$.

**Back-substitution.**
\begin{align*}
19 &= 152-133\\
&=152-(2717-17\cdot152)=18\cdot152-2717\\
&=18(8303-3\cdot2717)-2717\\
&=18\cdot8303-55\cdot2717.
\end{align*}
So $u=18$, $v=-55$.

**Remark.** This is the corrected exercise from Université Lyon 1.

## Statement

Compute $\divisibility(1001,315)$ and find a Bézout identity.

## Hint

Apply the Euclidean algorithm and back-substitute.

## Solution

**Euclidean algorithm.**
\begin{align*}
1001 &= 3\cdot315+56,\\
315 &= 5\cdot56+35,\\
56 &= 1\cdot35+21,\\
35 &= 1\cdot21+14,\\
21 &= 1\cdot14+7,\\
14 &= 2\cdot7+0.
\end{align*}
So $\divisibility(1001,315)=7$.

**Back-substitution.**
\begin{align*}
7 &= 21-14\\
&=21-(35-21)=2\cdot21-35\\
&=2(56-35)-35=2\cdot56-3\cdot35\\
&=2\cdot56-3(315-5\cdot56)=17\cdot56-3\cdot315\\
&=17(1001-3\cdot315)-3\cdot315\\
&=17\cdot1001-54\cdot315.
\end{align*}
So $17\cdot1001-54\cdot315=7$.

title: The relation between divisibility and lcm
category: number/divisibility
level: lycee
difficulty: 2
tags: divisibility, lcm, product relation
date: 2026-11-02

## Statement

Let $a,b$ be positive integers. Show that
$$\divisibility(a,b)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab.$$

## Hint

Write $a=\delta a'$ and $b=\delta b'$ with $\divisibility(a',b')=1$, where $\delta=\divisibility(a,b)$.

## Solution

**Setup.** Let $\delta=\divisibility(a,b)$, $a=\delta a'$, $b=\delta b'$ with $\divisibility(a',b')=1$.

**LCM.** Any common multiple $M$ of $a$ and $b$ satisfies $M=\alpha a=\beta b$, so $\alpha\delta a'=\beta\delta b'$, i.e. $\alpha a'=\beta b'$. Since $\divisibility(a',b')=1$, $a'\mid\beta$, say $\beta=ka'$. Then
$$M=k b'\delta a'=k\,ab/\delta.$$
The smallest positive such $M$ is for $k=1$, so
$$\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=\frac{ab}{\delta}.$$

**Conclusion.** Multiplying by $\delta$:
$$\divisibility(a,b)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab.$$

**Remark.** If $\divisibility(a,b)=1$, then $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab$.

## Statement

Find all positive integers $a,b$ such that
$$\divisibility(a,b)+\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=9+b.$$

## Hint

Let $\delta=\divisibility(a,b)$, $a=\delta a'$, $b=\delta b'$ with $\divisibility(a',b')=1$. Show that $\delta\mid9$.

## Solution

**Reduction.** Let $\delta=\divisibility(a,b)$, $a=\delta a'$, $b=\delta b'$, with $\divisibility(a',b')=1$. Then $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=\delta a'b'$, and the equation becomes
$$\delta+\delta a'b'=9+\delta b',$$
i.e.
$$\delta(1+a'b'-b')=9.$$
So $\delta\mid9$.

**Case analysis.** $\delta\in\{1,3,9\}$.

- $\delta=1$: $1+a'b'-b'=9$, so $a'b'-b'=8$, i.e. $b'(a'-1)=8$. With $\divisibility(a',b')=1$, solve the divisor cases.
- $\delta=3$: $3(1+a'b'-b')=9$, so $a'b'-b'=2$, i.e. $b'(a'-1)=2$.
- $\delta=9$: $1+a'b'-b'=1$, so $a'b'=b'$, i.e. $b'(a'-1)=0$, hence $a'=1$ (since $b'>0$). Then $a=\delta=9$ and $b=9b'$ arbitrary.

**Conclusion.** The full solution set is obtained by checking the finitely many divisor cases for each $\delta$. For $\delta=9$, the solutions are $a=9$, $b\equiv0\pmod9$.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c$ be integers with $\divisibility(a,b)=1$. Show that if $a\mid bc$, then $a\mid c$.

@@@ divisibility-coprime-powers.md
---
title: Coprime powers remain coprime
category: number/divisibility
level: l1
difficulty: 3
tags: coprime, powers, Bézout
date: 2026-11-02
---

## Statement

Let $a,b$ be coprime positive integers. Show that $a^n$ and $b^n$ are coprime for every $n\ge1$.

## Hint

Use Bézout and raise to a suitable power, or use prime factorizations.

## Solution

**Prime factorization approach.** Since $\divisibility(a,b)=1$, no prime divides both $a$ and $b$. The prime factors of $a^n$ are exactly those of $a$, and similarly for $b^n$. Hence no prime divides both $a^n$ and $b^n$, so $\divisibility(a^n,b^n)=1$.

**Bézout approach.** From $au+bv=1$, raise to the power $2n-1$:
$$1=(au+bv)^{2n-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{2n-1}\binom{2n-1}{k}(au)^k(bv)^{2n-1-k}.$$
Every term contains either $a^n$ or $b^n$ as a factor (since $k\ge n$ or $2n-1-k\ge n$). So $1$ is a linear combination of $a^n$ and $b^n$, hence they are coprime.

title: Why the Euclidean algorithm works
category: number/divisibility
level: l1
difficulty: 3
tags: Euclidean algorithm, proof, division
date: 2026-11-02
---

## Statement

Let $a=bq+r$ with $0\le r<b$. Prove that $\divisibility(a,b)=\divisibility(b,r)$.

## Hint

Show that the set of common divisors of $(a,b)$ equals the set of common divisors of $(b,r)$.

## Solution

**Inclusion 1.** If $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$, then $d\mid a-bq=r$. So $d$ is a common divisor of $b$ and $r$.

**Inclusion 2.** If $d\mid b$ and $d\mid r$, then $d\mid bq+r=a$. So $d$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$.

**Conclusion.** The two pairs have the same common divisors, hence the same greatest one. The Euclidean algorithm terminates because the remainders strictly decrease: $r<b$, and each new remainder is strictly smaller than the previous one. It stops when the remainder is $0$, and the last nonzero remainder is the divisibility.

title: Existence of a Bézout identity
category: number/divisibility
level: l1
difficulty: 4
tags: Bézout, existence, well-ordering
date: 2026-11-02

## Statement

Let $a,b$ be nonzero integers, and $d=\divisibility(a,b)$. Show that there exist integers $u,v$ such that $au+bv=d$.

## Hint

Consider the set $S=\{ax+by:x,y\in\mathbb{Z},\,ax+by>0\}$ and let $d'$ be its minimum.

## Solution

**Well-ordering.** $S$ is a nonempty set of positive integers (for example, $a^2+b^2\in S$). Let $d'$ be its smallest element, written $d'=au+bv$.

**Division.** Divide $a$ by $d'$: $a=d'q+r$ with $0\le r<d'$. Then
$$r=a-d'q=a-(au+bv)q=a(1-uq)+b(-vq).$$
So $r$ is a linear combination of $a$ and $b$. If $r>0$, then $r\in S$ and $r<d'$, contradicting the minimality of $d'$. Hence $r=0$, so $d'\mid a$. Similarly $d'\mid b$.

**Conclusion.** $d'$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$, so $d'\le d$. On the other hand, $d$ divides every linear combination of $a$ and $b$, so $d\mid d'$, hence $d\le d'$. Therefore $d'=d$, and $d$ has a Bézout representation.

## Statement

Show that $\divisibility(a+b,\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))=\divisibility(a,b)$ for all positive integers $a,b$.

## Hint

Write $a=\delta a'$, $b=\delta b'$ with $\divisibility(a',b')=1$. Then $\operatorname{lcm}=\delta a'b'$ and $a+b=\delta(a'+b')$.

## Solution

**Reduction.** Let $\delta=\divisibility(a,b)$, $a=\delta a'$, $b=\delta b'$, $\divisibility(a',b')=1$. Then
$$a+b=\delta(a'+b'),\qquad \operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=\delta a'b'.$$

**Conclusion.** $\divisibility(a+b,\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))=\delta=\divisibility(a,b)$.

Let $p$ be a prime dividing both $a+b$ and $ab$. Use Euclid's lemma.

## Solution

**Prime argument.** Suppose a prime $p$ divides $a+b$ and $ab$. Since $p\mid ab$ and $p$ is prime, $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$.

If $p\mid a$, then from $p\mid a+b$ we get $p\mid b$, contradicting $\divisibility(a,b)=1$. Similarly if $p\mid b$.

**Conclusion.** No prime divides both $a+b$ and $ab$, so $\divisibility(a+b,ab)=1$.

## Statement

## Hint

For the forward direction, note that $\divisibility(a,b)$ divides every linear combination. For the converse, use Bézout.

## Solution

**Necessity.** If $ax+by=c$ and $d=\divisibility(a,b)$, then $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$, so $d\mid ax+by=c$.

**Sufficiency.** Suppose $d\mid c$, say $c=dq$. By Bézout, there exist $u,v$ with $au+bv=d$. Multiplying by $q$:
$$a(uq)+b(vq)=dq=c.$$
So $(x,y)=(uq,vq)$ is a solution.

## Statement

Let $p$ be prime and $a,b$ integers. Show that if $p\mid ab$, then $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$.

## Hint

If $p\nmid a$, then $\divisibility(p,a)=1$. Apply Gauss's lemma.

## Solution

**Case 1.** If $p\mid a$, done.

**Case 2.** If $p\nmid a$, then since $p$ is prime, $\divisibility(p,a)=1$. By Gauss's lemma (if $\divisibility(p,a)=1$ and $p\mid ab$, then $p\mid b$), we get $p\mid b$.

**Remark.** This property characterizes prime numbers in $\mathbb{Z}$ and is the key to unique factorization.

## Statement

Show that a positive integer $n$ has an odd number of positive divisors if and only if $n$ is a perfect square.

## Hint

Pair each divisor $d$ with $n/d$. The pairing is perfect except when $d=n/d$.

## Solution

**Pairing.** For each divisor $d$ of $n$, $n/d$ is also a divisor. The map $d\mapsto n/d$ is an involution. Fixed points satisfy $d=n/d$, i.e. $d^2=n$.

**Conclusion.** If $n$ is not a square, all divisors pair up, so the number of divisors is even. If $n=k^2$, all divisors pair up except $d=k$, so the number of divisors is odd.
