## Statement

Prove that $n^5-n$ is divisible by $30$ for every integer $n$.

## Hint

Factor $n^5-n=n(n-1)(n+1)(n^2+1)$ and check divisibility by $2$, $3$ and $5$ separately.

## Solution

We have $n^5-n=(n-1)\,n\,(n+1)\,(n^2+1)$.

- **By 2 and 3.** The product $(n-1)n(n+1)$ of three consecutive integers is divisible by $2$ and by $3$.
- **By 5.** Look at $n$ modulo $5$. If $n\equiv0,1$ or $4$, one of $n$, $n-1$, $n+1$ is divisible by $5$. If $n\equiv2$ or $3$, then $n^2\equiv4$, so $n^2+1\equiv0\pmod5$.

The numbers $2,3,5$ are pairwise coprime, so $n^5-n$ is divisible by $2\cdot3\cdot5=30$.

## Statement

Compute $\gcd(252,198)$ with the Euclidean algorithm, then find integers $u,v$ with $252u+198v=\gcd(252,198)$.

## Hint

Run the divisions, then substitute backwards from the last non-zero remainder.

## Solution

**Algorithm.**
$$252=1\cdot198+54,\qquad198=3\cdot54+36,\qquad54=1\cdot36+18,\qquad36=2\cdot18+0.$$
The last non-zero remainder is $18$, so $\gcd(252,198)=18$.

**Bézout.** Working backwards,
$$18=54-36=54-(198-3\cdot54)=4\cdot54-198=4(252-198)-198=4\cdot252-5\cdot198.$$

So $u=4$ and $v=-5$. Check: $4\cdot252-5\cdot198=1008-990=18$.

## Statement

State and justify the divisibility rules for $2$, $3$, $5$, $9$, and $11$.

## Hint

Write $n=\sum a_k10^k$ and reduce $10$ modulo the divisor.

## Solution

**Divisibility by $2$ and $5$.** Since $10\equiv0\pmod2$ and $\pmod5$, only the last digit matters: $n\equiv a_0\pmod2$ and $\pmod5$. So $2\mid n$ iff the last digit is even; $5\mid n$ iff the last digit is $0$ or $5$.

**Divisibility by $3$ and $9$.** Since $10\equiv1\pmod3$ and $\pmod9$, $n\equiv\sum a_k\pmod3$ (and mod $9$). So $3\mid n$ (resp. $9\mid n$) iff the digit sum is divisible by $3$ (resp. $9$).

**Divisibility by $11$.** Since $10\equiv-1\pmod{11}$, $n\equiv\sum(-1)^ka_k\pmod{11}$. So $11\mid n$ iff the alternating digit sum is divisible by $11$.

**Conclusion.** These rules follow directly from $10\equiv0,1,-1$ modulo the respective divisors.

## Statement

Show that $a$ and $b$ are coprime iff there exist $u,v\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $au+bv=1$.

## Hint

Forward: Bézout. Converse: any common divisor divides $au+bv$.

## Solution

**Forward.** If $\gcd(a,b)=1$, Bézout gives $u,v$ with $au+bv=1$.

**Converse.** If $au+bv=1$ and $d\mid a$, $d\mid b$, then $d\mid au+bv=1$, so $d=1$. Hence $\gcd(a,b)=1$.

**Conclusion.** $\gcd(a,b)=1$ iff $a$ and $b$ have a Bézout relation equal to $1$.

## Statement

Show that $\gcd(a,b)$ is the smallest positive integer of the form $au+bv$ with $u,v\in\mathbb{Z}$.

## Hint

Let $d=\gcd(a,b)$ and $m$ the smallest positive $au+bv$. Show $m\mid a$ and $m\mid b$, then $m\le d$; and $d\mid m$, so $d\le m$.

## Solution

**Setup.** Let $m=\min\{au+bv>0:u,v\in\mathbb{Z}\}$, achieved at $(u_0,v_0)$.

**$m\mid a$.** Euclidean division: $a=qm+r$ with $0\le r<m$. Then $r=a-qm=a-q(au_0+bv_0)=a(1-qu_0)+b(-qv_0)$ is a positive combination if $r>0$, contradicting minimality. So $r=0$.

**$m\mid b$.** Same argument.

**Conclusion.** $m$ is a common divisor, so $m\le d$. Conversely, $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$ imply $d\mid m$, so $d\le m$. Hence $m=d$.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Show that if $a\mid bc$, then $a\mid c$.

## Hint

Bézout: $au+bv=1$. Multiply by $c$.

## Solution

**Bézout.** $au+bv=1$.

**Multiply by $c$.** $acu+bcv=c$.

**Divisibility.** $a\mid acu$ trivially, and $a\mid bcv$ since $a\mid bc$. So $a\mid c$.

**Conclusion.** Gauss's lemma holds.

**Application.** If $p$ is prime and $p\mid bc$, then $p\mid b$ or $p\mid c$ (Euclid's lemma).

## Statement

Let $a,b\in\mathbb{N}^*$. Show that $\gcd(a,b)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab$.

## Hint

Write $a=\delta a'$, $b=\delta b'$ with $\gcd(a',b')=1$, where $\delta=\gcd(a,b)$.

## Solution

**Setup.** $a=\delta a'$, $b=\delta b'$, $\gcd(a',b')=1$.

**LCM.** Any common multiple $M$ satisfies $M=\alpha a=\beta b$, so $\alpha a'=\beta b'$. Since $\gcd(a',b')=1$, $a'\mid\beta$, say $\beta=ka'$. Then $M=k\delta a'b'$. The smallest positive is $k=1$, so $\operatorname{lcm}=\delta a'b'=ab/\delta$.

**Conclusion.** $\gcd(a,b)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab$.

**Remark.** In particular, if $\gcd(a,b)=1$, then $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab$.

Show that every integer $n\ge2$ has at least one prime divisor.

## Solution

**Well-ordering.** Let $D=\{d\ge2:d\mid n\}$, nonempty since $n\in D$. Let $p=\min D$.

## Statement

Prove that there are infinitely many prime numbers.

## Hint

Assume finitely many $p_1,\ldots,p_n$ and consider $N=p_1\cdots p_n+1$.

## Solution

**Contradiction.** Suppose finitely many primes $p_1,\ldots,p_n$. Let $N=p_1\cdots p_n+1>1$.

**Prime divisor.** $N$ has a prime divisor $p$, which must be one of the $p_i$.

**Contradiction.** $p\mid N$ and $p\mid p_1\cdots p_n$, so $p\mid N-p_1\cdots p_n=1$, impossible.

**Conclusion.** There are infinitely many primes.

## Statement

State the fundamental theorem of arithmetic and prove uniqueness of the prime factorization.

## Hint

Existence by strong induction; uniqueness by Euclid's lemma.

## Solution

**Statement.** Every integer $n\ge2$ can be written uniquely as a product of primes (up to order).

**Existence.** By strong induction: $n$ has a prime divisor $p$ (previous exercise), $n=p\cdot m$ with $m<n$; if $m=1$ done, else $m$ factors by induction.

**Uniqueness.** Suppose $n=p_1\cdots p_r=q_1\cdots q_s$. Since $p_1\mid q_1\cdots q_s$, Euclid's lemma gives $p_1=q_j$ for some $j$. Cancel and repeat by induction.

**Conclusion.** The prime factorization exists and is unique.

## Statement

Let $\sigma(n)$ be the sum of the positive divisors of $n$. Show that if $n=\prod p_i^{\alpha_i}$, then
$$\sigma(n)=\prod_{i}\frac{p_i^{\alpha_i+1}-1}{p_i-1}.$$

The sum of divisors is multiplicative and $\sigma(p^\alpha)=1+p+\cdots+p^\alpha$.

## Solution

**Multiplicativity.** If $\gcd(m,n)=1$, then every divisor of $mn$ is uniquely $d_1d_2$ with $d_1\mid m$, $d_2\mid n$. So $\sigma(mn)=\sigma(m)\sigma(n)$.

**Prime power.** $\sigma(p^\alpha)=1+p+\cdots+p^\alpha=\frac{p^{\alpha+1}-1}{p-1}$.

## Statement

A positive integer $n$ is perfect if $\sigma(n)=2n$. Show that if $2^p-1$ is prime (a Mersenne prime), then $n=2^{p-1}(2^p-1)$ is perfect.

## Hint

Use multiplicativity of $\sigma$ since $2^{p-1}$ and $2^p-1$ are coprime.

## Solution

**Coprimality.** $\gcd(2^{p-1},2^p-1)=1$ since $2^{p-1}$ is a power of $2$ and $2^p-1$ is odd.

## Statement

Define a Carmichael number. Show that $561$ is a Carmichael number.

$561=3\cdot11\cdot17$. Show $a^{560}\equiv1\pmod{561}$ for every $a$ coprime to $561$, by checking mod $3,11,17$ and using CRT.

## Statement

Describe the Miller–Rabin primality test for odd $n>2$, writing $n-1=2^s\cdot d$ with $d$ odd.

## Hint

For a base $a$, compute $a^d\bmod n$, then square repeatedly up to $s$ times.

## Solution

**Setup.** Write $n-1=2^s\cdot d$ with $d$ odd. Choose a base $a$ with $1<a<n-1$.

**Test.** Compute $x=a^d\bmod n$. Then square $s$ times:
- if $x\equiv1$ or $x\equiv n-1$, the base is a **witness for primality**;
- otherwise, if after squaring $s$ times we reach neither $1$ nor $n-1$, the base is a **witness for compositeness**.

**Correctness.** If $n$ is prime, Fermat's little theorem plus the structure of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}^*$ ensures every coprime base is a witness for primality. If $n$ is composite, at least $3/4$ of bases are witnesses for compositeness (for odd composite $n$).

**Conclusion.** Miller–Rabin is a probabilistic test with error probability $\le(1/4)^k$ for $k$ bases.

Simplify $\dfrac{1725}{1995}$ to an irreducible fraction.

## Hint

Compute $\divisibility(1725,1995)$ with the Euclidean algorithm and divide.

## Solution

**Euclidean algorithm.**
\begin{align*}
1995 &= 1\cdot1785+210,\\
1785 &= 8\cdot210+105,\\
210 &= 2\cdot105+0.
\end{align*}

**Simplification.**
$$\frac{1725}{1995}=\frac{1725/105}{1995/105}=\frac{17}{19}.$$

## Statement

Show that every common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $\divisibility(a,b)$, and every common multiple of $a$ and $b$ is a multiple of $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$.

## Hint

Use the prime factorizations, or the Bézout identity for the first part.

## Solution

**Common divisors.** Let $d=\divisibility(a,b)$ and let $c$ be any common divisor of $a,b$. Write $d=au+bv$ (Bézout). Since $c\mid a$ and $c\mid b$, we get $c\mid au+bv=d$.

**Common multiples.** Let $M=\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$ and let $N$ be any common multiple. Write the Euclidean division $N=Mq+r$ with $0\le r<M$. Since $N$ and $M$ are both multiples of $a$ and $b$, so is $r=N-Mq$. But $M$ is the *smallest* positive common multiple, so $r=0$. Hence $M\mid N$.

## Statement

Let $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$, and $F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_n$ (Fibonacci). Show that $\divisibility(F_n,F_{n+1})=1$ for all $n$.

## Hint

Use the Euclidean algorithm relation $\divisibility(F_{n+1},F_n)=\divisibility(F_n,F_{n-1})$.

## Solution

**Recurrence.** From $F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}$, we have
$$\divisibility(F_{n+1},F_n)=\divisibility(F_n+F_{n-1},F_n)=\divisibility(F_{n-1},F_n).$$
Iterating:
$$\divisibility(F_{n+1},F_n)=\divisibility(F_n,F_{n-1})=\cdots=\divisibility(F_2,F_1)=\divisibility(1,1)=1.$$

**Conclusion.** Consecutive Fibonacci numbers are always coprime.

**Remark.** This is a nice application of the invariance of the divisibility under the Euclidean step.

## Statement

Show that $\divisibility(a+b,\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))=\divisibility(a,b)$ for all positive integers $a,b$.

## Hint

Write $a=\delta a'$, $b=\delta b'$ with $\divisibility(a',b')=1$. Then $\operatorname{lcm}=\delta a'b'$ and $a+b=\delta(a'+b')$.

## Solution

$$a+b=\delta(a'+b'),\qquad \operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=\delta a'b'.$$
So
$$\divisibility(a+b,\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))=\delta\cdot\divisibility(a'+b',a'b').$$

**Coprimality.** Since $\divisibility(a',b')=1$, any prime dividing $a'b'$ divides exactly one of $a',b'$, hence cannot divide $a'+b'$. Therefore $\divisibility(a'+b',a'b')=1$.

**Conclusion.** $\divisibility(a+b,\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))=\delta=\divisibility(a,b)$.

**Factorization.**

## Statement

## Hint

For the forward direction, note that $\divisibility(a,b)$ divides every linear combination. For the converse, use Bézout.

## Solution

**Necessity.** If $ax+by=c$ and $d=\divisibility(a,b)$, then $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$, so $d\mid ax+by=c$.

$$a(uq)+b(vq)=dq=c.$$
So $(x,y)=(uq,vq)$ is a solution.

## Statement

## Hint

**Case 1.** If $p\mid a$, done.

## Statement

Show that a positive integer $n$ has an odd number of positive divisors if and only if $n$ is a perfect square.

## Hint

Pair each divisor $d$ with $n/d$. The pairing is perfect except when $d=n/d$.

## Solution

**Pairing.** For each divisor $d$ of $n$, $n/d$ is also a divisor. The map $d\mapsto n/d$ is an involution. Fixed points satisfy $d=n/d$, i.e. $d^2=n$.

**Conclusion.** If $n$ is not a square, all divisors pair up, so the number of divisors is even. If $n=k^2$, all divisors pair up except $d=k$, so the number of divisors is odd.
