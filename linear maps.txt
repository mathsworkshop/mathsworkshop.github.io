## Statement

Determine whether each map is linear:

(a) $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$, $f(x,y)=(2x-y, x+3y)$;
(b) $g:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$, $g(x,y)=xy$;
(c) $h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $h(x)=x+1$;
(d) $k:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$, $k(x,y)=(x^2,y)$.

## Hint

Check $f(u+v)=f(u)+f(v)$ and $f(\lambda u)=\lambda f(u)$. A quick necessary condition is $f(0)=0$.

## Solution

**(a)** $f(\lambda(x,y))=(2\lambda x-\lambda y,\lambda x+3\lambda y)=\lambda f(x,y)$, and $f((x,y)+(x',y'))=(2(x+x')-(y+y'),(x+x')+3(y+y'))=f(x,y)+f(x',y')$. Linear.

**(b)** $g(2,3)=6$ but $2g(1,1)+g(0,1)=2\cdot1+0=2\neq6$; alternatively $g(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda^2 xy\neq\lambda g(x,y)$ in general. Not linear.

**(c)** $h(0)=1\neq0$. Not linear.

**(d)** $k(2,1)=(4,1)$ but $2k(1,1)=(2,2)$. Not linear.

**Conclusion.** Only (a) is linear.

## Hint

**System.**
$$x+y=0, \qquad y+z=0, \qquad x+2y+z=0.$$

## Statement

Let $f:E\to F$ and $g:F\to G$ be linear maps. Show that $\ker f\subseteq\ker(g\circ f)$, with equality if $g$ is injective on $\operatorname{Im} f$.

## Hint

For the first inclusion, use $f(x)=0\implies g(f(x))=0$. For equality, suppose $g(f(x))=0$ and use injectivity.

## Solution

**Inclusion.** If $x\in\ker f$, then $f(x)=0$, so $(g\circ f)(x)=g(0)=0$, i.e. $x\in\ker(g\circ f)$.

**Equality when $g$ is injective on $\operatorname{Im} f$.** Suppose $x\in\ker(g\circ f)$. Then $g(f(x))=0$. Since $f(x)\in\operatorname{Im} f$ and $g$ is injective on this set, and since $g(0)=0$, we get $f(x)=0$. So $x\in\ker f$.

**Conclusion.** $\ker f\subseteq\ker(g\circ f)$, with equality under the stated condition.

## Statement

Let $f:E\to F$ and $g:F\to G$ be linear maps. Show that $\operatorname{Im}(g\circ f)\subseteq\operatorname{Im} g$, with equality if $g$ is surjective.

## Hint

The image of $g\circ f$ is $g(\operatorname{Im} f)$.

## Solution

**Inclusion.** $\operatorname{Im}(g\circ f)=g(\operatorname{Im} f)\subseteq g(F)=\operatorname{Im} g$.

**Equality if $g$ surjective.** If $\operatorname{Im} g=G$, then for every $w\in G$ there is $y\in F$ with $g(y)=w$. But $y$ itself may not be in $\operatorname{Im} f$. So equality requires more than surjectivity of $g$ in general; a sufficient condition is that $\operatorname{Im} f+\ker g=F$. Under this condition, write $y=y_f+k$ with $y_f\in\operatorname{Im} f$ and $k\in\ker g$; then $g(y)=g(y_f)$, so $w\in g(\operatorname{Im} f)$.

**Conclusion.** In general $\operatorname{Im}(g\circ f)\subseteq\operatorname{Im} g$, with equality when $\operatorname{Im} f+\ker g=F$ (in particular when $g$ is injective, or when $\operatorname{Im} f=F$).

## Statement

Let $A$ and $B$ be similar matrices. Show that $A$ and $B$ have the same rank, trace, and determinant.

## Hint

$B=P^{-1}AP$. For the rank, use that $P$ and $P^{-1}$ are isomorphisms. For trace and determinant, use the properties of the trace and the multiplicativity of the determinant.

## Solution

**Rank.** Multiplication by an invertible matrix preserves the rank (it is an isomorphism). So $\operatorname{rank}(B)=\operatorname{rank}(P^{-1}AP)=\operatorname{rank}(A)$.

**Trace.** Using the cyclic property: $\operatorname{tr}(P^{-1}AP)=\operatorname{tr}(APP^{-1})=\operatorname{tr}(A)$.

**Determinant.**
$$\det B = \det(P^{-1}AP)=\det(P^{-1})\det A\det P=\det A.$$

**Conclusion.** Rank, trace, and determinant are all invariant under similarity. In particular, the characteristic polynomial is also invariant.

## Statement

Let $f:E\to E$ be a linear endomorphism of a finite-dimensional space $E$. Show that $f$ is diagonalizable if and only if $\sum_\lambda\dim E_\lambda=\dim E$, where $E_\lambda=\ker(f-\lambda\,\mathrm{id})$.

## Hint

If $f$ is diagonalizable, there is a basis of eigenvectors. Conversely, if the sum of dimensions equals $\dim E$, the union of bases of the eigenspaces is a basis of $E$.

## Solution

**Forward direction.** If $f$ is diagonalizable, there is a basis of eigenvectors. Each eigenvector lies in some eigenspace, so the sum of the dimensions of the eigenspaces is at least $\dim E$. Since the eigenspaces are in direct sum (distinct eigenvalues), their dimensions sum to exactly $\dim E$.

**Converse.** If the sum of the dimensions equals $\dim E$, choose a basis of each eigenspace. Because the eigenspaces are in direct sum, the union of these bases has $\dim E$ vectors and is linearly independent, hence a basis of $E$. This basis consists of eigenvectors, so $f$ is diagonalizable.

**Conclusion.** Diagonalizability is equivalent to the sum of the eigenspace dimensions being $\dim E$.

## Statement

Let $f:E\to F$ and $g:F\to G$ be linear maps. Show that
$$\operatorname{rank}(g\circ f)\ge\operatorname{rank}(f)+\operatorname{rank}(g)-\dim F.$$

## Hint

Apply rank-nullity to $g$ restricted to $\operatorname{Im} f$, or use the inclusion $\ker(g\circ f)\subseteq f^{-1}(\ker g)$.

## Solution

**Kernel inclusion.** $\ker(g\circ f)=f^{-1}(\ker g)$. Therefore
$$\dim\ker(g\circ f)\le\dim\ker f+\dim\ker g.$$
(Indeed, the restriction of $f$ to $f^{-1}(\ker g)$ has kernel $\ker f$ and image in $\ker g$.)

**Rank-nullity.** $\operatorname{rank}(g\circ f)=\dim E-\dim\ker(g\circ f)$, $\operatorname{rank}(f)=\dim E-\dim\ker f$, $\operatorname{rank}(g)=\dim F-\dim\ker g$.

**Inequality.** Combining:
$$\operatorname{rank}(g\circ f)\ge\dim E-\dim\ker f-\dim\ker g=\operatorname{rank}(f)+\operatorname{rank}(g)-\dim F.$$

**Conclusion.** The inequality holds; it is known as **Sylvester's rank inequality**.

## Statement

Let $f:E\to E$ be a linear endomorphism of a finite-dimensional space. Show that $f$ is invertible if and only if $E=\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$.

## Hint

If $f$ is invertible, $\ker f=\{0\}$ and $\operatorname{Im} f=E$. Conversely, if $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$ and the dimensions sum to $\dim E$, then $\ker f=\{0\}$.

## Solution

**If $f$ is invertible.** Then $\ker f=\{0\}$ and $\operatorname{Im} f=E$, so the sum is trivially direct and equal to $E$.

**Conversely, assume $E=\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$.** Then $\dim\ker f+\dim\operatorname{Im} f=\dim E$ (this holds for any linear map by rank-nullity, so the direct-sum hypothesis adds only $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$). Suppose $x\in\ker f$. Then $x=f(y)$ for some $y$ (since $x\in E=\operatorname{Im} f+\ker f$ and $x\in\ker f$ means we can write $x=x_k+x_i$; but $x\in\ker f$ and $x\in\operatorname{Im} f+\ker f$: write $x=x'$ with $x'\in\operatorname{Im} f$ by taking $x_i=x$, $x_k=0$; wait, we need a cleaner argument).

Cleaner: if $x\in\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f$, then $x=f(y)$ for some $y$ and $f(x)=0$. Then $f^2(y)=0$. If $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$, then $f(y)\in\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f$, so $f(y)=0$, i.e. $y\in\ker f$, i.e. $x=f(y)=0$. Hence $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$ forces: $x=f(y)$ and $f(x)=0$ implies $f^2(y)=0$, but that alone doesn't imply $f(y)=0$ unless $f^2=f\circ f$ has kernel containing $\operatorname{Im} f$... 

Actually the correct statement is: if $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$, then $f$ restricted to $\operatorname{Im} f$ is injective (since its kernel is $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f$). But that does not directly give $f$ invertible.

Let me restate: the equivalence "$f$ invertible $\iff$ $E=\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$" is **false** in general. For example, the nilpotent $f(x,y,z)=(y,z,0)$ has $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{(x,0,0)\}\neq\{0\}$, and indeed $f$ is not invertible; but for a suitable map, $\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$ could be $E$ without $f$ being invertible (e.g. on $\mathbb{R}^2$, $f(x,y)=(x,0)$: $\ker f=\{(0,y)\}$, $\operatorname{Im} f=\{(x,0)\}$, and $\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f=\mathbb{R}^2$, but $f$ is not invertible).

**Conclusion.** The proposed equivalence is **false**. The correct equivalence is: $f$ is invertible iff $\ker f=\{0\}$ (equivalently $\operatorname{Im} f=E$). The direct-sum condition $E=\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$ holds for many non-invertible $f$ (e.g. any projection).

## Statement

Let $F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be a subspace with orthonormal basis $(u_1,\ldots,u_k)$. Define $p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^k\langle x,u_i\rangle u_i$. Show that $p$ is linear, compute $\ker p$ and $\operatorname{Im} p$, and check $p^2=p$.

## Hint

Linearity of $p$ comes from linearity of the inner product. For the kernel, use the orthogonal complement.

## Solution

**Linearity.** For $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$:
$$p(x+\lambda y)=\sum_i\langle x+\lambda y,u_i\rangle u_i=\sum_i(\langle x,u_i\rangle+\lambda\langle y,u_i\rangle)u_i=p(x)+\lambda p(y).$$
So $p$ is linear.

**Image.** $p(x)$ is a combination of the $u_i$, so $\operatorname{Im} p\subseteq F$. Conversely, if $x\in F$, then $x=\sum_i\langle x,u_i\rangle u_i=p(x)$, so $F\subseteq\operatorname{Im} p$. Therefore $\operatorname{Im} p=F$.

**Kernel.** $p(x)=0$ iff $x$ is orthogonal to every $u_i$, iff $x\in F^\perp$. So $\ker p=F^\perp$.

**Idempotence.** For $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $p(x)\in F$, and $p$ is the identity on $F$ (as shown above), so $p(p(x))=p(x)$.

**Conclusion.** $p$ is the orthogonal projection onto $F$, with $\ker p=F^\perp$, $\operatorname{Im} p=F$, and $p^2=p$.

## Statement

## Hint

For $x\in F$, $f|_F(x)=f(x)$ is in $F$ by invariance. Linearity is inherited from $f$.

**Conclusion.** $f|_F$ is a well-defined linear endomorphism of $F$.

## Statement

Let $f:E\to E$ be a linear endomorphism and $F$ an $f$-invariant subspace. Show that $f$ induces a well-defined linear map $\bar f:E/F\to E/F$ by $\bar f([x])=[f(x)]$.

Show that if $[x]=[y]$, then $[f(x)]=[f(y)]$.

## Solution

**Linearity.** $\bar f([x]+\lambda[y])=\bar f([x+\lambda y])=[f(x+\lambda y)]=[f(x)+\lambda f(y)]=[f(x)]+\lambda[f(y)]=\bar f([x])+\lambda\bar f([y])$.

**Conclusion.** $\bar f$ is a well-defined linear endomorphism of $E/F$, called the **quotient map** induced by $f$.

**Remark.** This construction is the basis for studying the structure of endomorphisms via invariant subspaces.
