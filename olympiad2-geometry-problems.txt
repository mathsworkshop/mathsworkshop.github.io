date: 2026-11-05

## Statement

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $BC=a$, $CA=b$, $AB=c$. Let $D$ be a point of the segment $BC$ with $BD=m$, $DC=n$ (so $m+n=a$) and $AD=d$.

1. Prove Stewart's theorem: $b^2m+c^2n=a\,(d^2+mn)$.
2. Deduce the length of the median from $A$: $d^2=\dfrac{2b^2+2c^2-a^2}{4}$.
3. Deduce the length $\ell$ of the internal bisector from $A$: $\ell^2=bc\left(1-\dfrac{a^2}{(b+c)^2}\right)$.
4. Compute the median and the bisector from $A$ in the triangle with $a=14$, $b=15$, $c=13$.

## Hint

The angles $\angle ADB$ and $\angle ADC$ are supplementary, so their cosines are opposite. Write the law of cosines in triangles $ABD$ and $ACD$ and eliminate the cosine.

## Solution

**1.** Let $\theta=\angle ADB$, so $\angle ADC=180^\circ-\theta$ and $\cos\angle ADC=-\cos\theta$. The law of cosines gives
$$c^2=d^2+m^2-2dm\cos\theta,\qquad b^2=d^2+n^2+2dn\cos\theta.$$
Multiply the first by $n$, the second by $m$, and add: the cosine terms cancel and
$$c^2n+b^2m=d^2(m+n)+mn(m+n)=a\,(d^2+mn).$$

**2.** For the median, $m=n=\frac a2$: $\frac a2(b^2+c^2)=a\left(d^2+\frac{a^2}4\right)$, hence $d^2=\frac{b^2+c^2}2-\frac{a^2}4=\frac{2b^2+2c^2-a^2}4$.

**3.** By the angle bisector theorem $\frac mn=\frac cb$, so $m=\frac{ac}{b+c}$ and $n=\frac{ab}{b+c}$. Then
$$b^2m+c^2n=\frac{abc\,(b+c)}{b+c}=abc,$$
and Stewart gives $d^2=bc-mn=bc-\frac{a^2bc}{(b+c)^2}=bc\left(1-\frac{a^2}{(b+c)^2}\right)$.

**4.** Median: $\frac{2\cdot225+2\cdot169-196}4=148$, so the median is $\sqrt{148}=2\sqrt{37}\approx12.17$. Bisector: $\ell^2=195\left(1-\frac{196}{784}\right)=195\cdot\frac34=146.25$, so $\ell\approx12.09$ (the bisector is slightly shorter than the median, as expected).

**Remark.** Stewart's theorem also gives the length of an altitude or any cevian once $m$ and $n$ are known.

**Lesson.** When two angles are supplementary, write the law of cosines in both triangles and eliminate the cosine.

title: The Apollonius circle
category: olympiad/oly-geometry
level: olympiad
difficulty: 3
tags: locus, Apollonius circle, coordinates, angle bisector
date: 2026-11-05
---

## Statement

Let $A$ and $B$ be two points at distance $d>0$, and let $k>0$, $k\ne1$.

1. Prove that the locus of the points $P$ with $PA=k\cdot PB$ is a circle. Find its centre and radius.
2. Let $E$ and $F$ be the two points of line $AB$ on this locus. Prove that the centre of the circle is the midpoint of $EF$.
3. Let $P$ be a point of the locus not on line $AB$. Prove that $PE$ and $PF$ are the internal and external bisectors of $\angle APB$.

## Hint

Put $A=(0,0)$ and $B=(d,0)$ and square the condition $PA=kPB$.

## Solution

**1.** For $P=(x,y)$: $x^2+y^2=k^2\left((x-d)^2+y^2\right)$, i.e.
$$(k^2-1)(x^2+y^2)-2k^2d\,x+k^2d^2=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \left(x-\frac{k^2d}{k^2-1}\right)^2+y^2=\frac{k^4d^2}{(k^2-1)^2}-\frac{k^2d^2}{k^2-1}.$$
The right side equals $\dfrac{k^4d^2-k^2d^2(k^2-1)}{(k^2-1)^2}=\dfrac{k^2d^2}{(k^2-1)^2}>0$. So the locus is the circle with centre $\left(\dfrac{k^2d}{k^2-1},0\right)$ and radius $\dfrac{kd}{|k^2-1|}$.

**2.** On line $AB$ (the $x$-axis) the circle meets the axis at $x=\frac{k^2d\pm kd}{k^2-1}$, that is at $x=\frac{kd}{k-1}$ and $x=\frac{kd}{k+1}$. These are the points $F$ and $E$ with $\frac{EA}{EB}=\frac{FA}{FB}=k$ (internal point $E=\frac{kd}{k+1}$, external point $F=\frac{kd}{k-1}$). Their midpoint is $\frac12\cdot kd\cdot\frac{(k+1)+(k-1)}{k^2-1}=\frac{k^2d}{k^2-1}$, the centre. Hence $EF$ is a diameter.

**3.** Since $PA/PB=k=EA/EB$, the point $E$ lies on segment $AB$ and divides it in the ratio of $PA$ to $PB$, so by the converse of the angle bisector theorem $PE$ bisects $\angle APB$ internally. Similarly $F$ lies outside the segment on line $AB$ with $FA/FB=PA/PB$, so $PF$ is the external bisector. (Consistently, $\angle EPF=90^\circ$ because $EF$ is a diameter.)

**Remark.** This gives a second proof of the angle bisector theorem's converse and explains why "the points whose distances to two fixed points are in a fixed ratio" form a circle: it is the Apollonius circle of $A,B$ for the ratio $k$. For $k=1$ the locus degenerates to the perpendicular bisector.

**Lesson.** A locus defined by a distance relation is found by coordinates and completing the square; then identify the special points on the axis.

title: Excircles and tangent lengths

## Statement

Let $ABC$ have sides $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, semiperimeter $s=\frac{a+b+c}2$, area $S$, inradius $r$. The incircle touches $BC$ at $D'$. The excircle opposite $A$ (tangent to segment $BC$ and to the extensions of $AB$ and $AC$ beyond $B$ and $C$) has radius $r_a$ and touches $BC$ at $D$, line $AB$ at $E$ and line $AC$ at $F$.

1. Prove $AE=AF=s$.
2. Prove $BD=s-c$ and $CD=s-b$, and $BD'=s-b$, $CD'=s-c$. Conclude that $D$ and $D'$ are symmetric with respect to the midpoint of $BC$.
3. Prove $S=r_a(s-a)$, and deduce $r\,r_a=(s-b)(s-c)$.

## Hint

Two tangent segments from the same point to a circle are equal. For part 3 split the area of $ABC$ using the excentre $I_a$.

## Solution

**1.** Tangent segments from $B$ give $BE=BD$ and from $C$ give $CF=CD$; from $A$, $AE=AF$. Since $E$ lies on the extension of $AB$ beyond $B$ and $F$ on the extension of $AC$ beyond $C$,
$$AE+AF=(AB+BE)+(AC+CF)=c+b+(BD+DC)=a+b+c=2s.$$
Hence $AE=AF=s$.

**2.** $BD=BE=AE-AB=s-c$ and $CD=CF=AF-AC=s-b$. For the incircle, with tangent points $D'$, $E'$ on $CA$, $F'$ on $AB$, let $x=AE'=AF'$, $y=BD'=BF'$, $z=CD'=CE'$. Then $x+y=c$, $x+z=b$, $y+z=a$, so $y=\frac{a+c-b}2=s-b$ and $z=s-c$. Therefore $BD=s-c=CD'$ and $CD=s-b=BD'$: the points $D$ and $D'$ are exchanged by the reflection in the midpoint of $BC$.

**3.** Let $I_a$ be the excentre; it is at distance $r_a$ from the three sides. Then
$$[ABC]=[ABI_a]+[ACI_a]-[BCI_a]=\tfrac12cr_a+\tfrac12br_a-\tfrac12ar_a=r_a\cdot\frac{b+c-a}2=r_a(s-a).$$
(The triangle $ABC$ is the union of $ABI_a$ and $ACI_a$ minus the overlap $BCI_a$, because $I_a$ lies on the other side of $BC$ from $A$.) Also $S=rs$, and Heron's formula gives $S^2=s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)$. Multiplying $S=rs$ and $S=r_a(s-a)$:
$$S^2=r\,r_a\,s(s-a)\ \Longrightarrow\ r\,r_a=(s-b)(s-c).$$

**Remark.** From part 2, the distance between the two touch points on $BC$ is $|b-c|$. The formula $S=r_a(s-a)$ is the analogue for the excircle of $S=rs$.

**Lesson.** Tangent-length bookkeeping turns circle configurations into linear equations in the semiperimeter.

title: Euler's formula OI² = R² − 2Rr
category: olympiad/oly-geometry

## Statement

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcentre $O$, circumradius $R$, incentre $I$ and inradius $r$. Prove that
$$OI^2=R^2-2Rr.$$
Deduce Euler's inequality $R\ge2r$, with equality only for the equilateral triangle.

## Hint

Let the bisector from $A$ meet the circumcircle again at $M$. Compute the power of $I$ with respect to the circumcircle in two ways. You will need $MI=MB$.

## Solution

**Step 1: $MB=MI$.** Let $A,B,C$ also denote the angles of the triangle. In triangle $ABI$ the exterior angle at $I$ gives $\angle BIM=\angle IAB+\angle IBA=\frac A2+\frac B2$. On the other hand $\angle IBM=\angle IBC+\angle CBM=\frac B2+\angle CAM=\frac B2+\frac A2$ (inscribed angles on the same arc $CM$). So triangle $MBI$ is isosceles with $MB=MI$.

**Step 2: lengths.** In the circumcircle the chord $MB$ subtends the inscribed angle $\angle MAB=\frac A2$, so $MB=2R\sin\frac A2$. In the right triangle formed by $I$, the foot of the perpendicular from $I$ to $AB$, and $A$, we have $AI=\dfrac r{\sin\frac A2}$.

**Step 3: power of the point.** The point $I$ is inside the circumcircle and the chord through $I$ is $AM$, so
$$R^2-OI^2=IA\cdot IM=\frac r{\sin\frac A2}\cdot2R\sin\frac A2=2Rr.$$
Hence $OI^2=R^2-2Rr$.

**Consequence.** $OI^2\ge0$ gives $R\ge2r$, with equality iff $O=I$. When the circumcentre and incentre coincide, the perpendicular bisectors and angle bisectors coincide, so each angle bisector is also a perpendicular bisector, which forces $AB=AC=BC$: the triangle is equilateral.

**Check.** For the $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle, $R=2.5$ and $r=1$, so $OI^2=6.25-5=1.25$; coordinates (right angle at the origin, legs on the axes: $I=(1,1)$, $O=(1.5,2)$) give $OI^2=0.25+1=1.25$ ✓.

**Lesson.** Compute a power of a point in two ways, and use the incentre–excentre lemma ($MB=MI$) to link the incentre to the circumcircle.

title: Trigonometric Ceva and a hidden isosceles triangle
category: olympiad/oly-geometry
level: olympiad
difficulty: 4
tags: trigonometric Ceva, angle chasing, monotonicity
date: 2026-11-05

## Statement

Let $P$ be a point inside triangle $ABC$ such that
$$\angle BAP=10^\circ,\quad\angle PAC=40^\circ,\quad\angle ABP=20^\circ,\quad\angle ACP=30^\circ.$$
Prove that triangle $ABC$ is isosceles and find its angles.

## Hint

Let $x=\angle PBC$ and $y=\angle PCB$. The angle sum gives $x+y$. Then use the trigonometric form of Ceva's theorem, and show that the resulting equation in $x$ has only one solution.

## Solution

**Angle sum.** $\angle A=50^\circ$, $\angle B=20^\circ+x$, $\angle C=30^\circ+y$. The sum is $180^\circ$, so $x+y=80^\circ$, i.e. $y=80^\circ-x$ with $0<x<80^\circ$.

**Trigonometric Ceva.** For a point $P$ inside triangle $ABC$,
$$\sin\angle BAP\cdot\sin\angle CBP\cdot\sin\angle ACP=\sin\angle PAC\cdot\sin\angle PBA\cdot\sin\angle PCB.$$
(Proof: in triangles $ABP$ and $ACP$ the law of sines applied to the common side $AP$, $BP$, $CP$ gives $\frac{PB}{PA}=\frac{\sin\angle BAP}{\sin\angle ABP}$, $\frac{PC}{PB}=\frac{\sin\angle CBP}{\sin\angle BCP}$, $\frac{PA}{PC}=\frac{\sin\angle ACP}{\sin\angle CAP}$. The product of the three left sides is $1$.)

Here this reads
$$\sin10^\circ\sin x\sin30^\circ=\sin40^\circ\sin20^\circ\sin(80^\circ-x),\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\frac{\sin x}{\sin(80^\circ-x)}=\frac{\sin40^\circ\sin20^\circ}{\sin10^\circ\sin30^\circ}.$$

**Uniqueness.** On $(0^\circ,80^\circ)$ the function $x\mapsto\frac{\sin x}{\sin(80^\circ-x)}$ is strictly increasing (the numerator increases, the denominator decreases and stays positive). So there is at most one solution.

**The solution is $x=60^\circ$.** We need $\sin10^\circ\sin60^\circ\sin30^\circ=\sin40^\circ\sin20^\circ\sin20^\circ$. Using $2\sin20^\circ\sin40^\circ=\cos20^\circ-\cos60^\circ=\cos20^\circ-\frac12$,
$$\sin40^\circ\sin^220^\circ=\tfrac12\sin20^\circ\left(\cos20^\circ-\tfrac12\right)=\tfrac14\left(\sin40^\circ-\sin20^\circ\right)=\tfrac12\cos30^\circ\sin10^\circ=\sin10^\circ\sin60^\circ\sin30^\circ,$$
using $\sin20^\circ\cos20^\circ=\frac12\sin40^\circ$ and $\sin40^\circ-\sin20^\circ=2\cos30^\circ\sin10^\circ$. So $x=60^\circ$ is a solution, hence the only one.

**Conclusion.** $x=60^\circ$, $y=20^\circ$, so
$$\angle A=50^\circ,\quad\angle B=80^\circ,\quad\angle C=50^\circ.$$
Since $\angle A=\angle C$, we have $BA=BC$: triangle $ABC$ is isosceles.

**Lesson.** Trigonometric Ceva converts a cevian configuration into one trigonometric equation; monotonicity then shows that a guessed solution is the only one.

title: Harmonic division from Ceva and Menelaus
category: olympiad/oly-geometry
level: olympiad

tags: Ceva, Menelaus, harmonic conjugates, cevians
date: 2026-11-05

## Statement

Let $D,E,F$ be points on the sides $BC,CA,AB$ of triangle $ABC$ (interior to the sides) such that $AD$, $BE$, $CF$ are concurrent. Suppose that line $EF$ is not parallel to $BC$ and meets line $BC$ at $G$.

1. Prove that $\dfrac{BD}{DC}=\dfrac{BG}{GC}$.
2. Conclude that $G$ lies outside segment $BC$ and that $D$ and $G$ divide $BC$ internally and externally in the same ratio.
3. Deduce that if $D$ is the midpoint of $BC$, then $EF\parallel BC$.

## Hint

Write Ceva's theorem for the concurrent lines and Menelaus' theorem for the line $EFG$; both contain the same product $\frac{CE}{EA}\cdot\frac{AF}{FB}$.

## Solution

**1.** Ceva's theorem (concurrent cevians):
$$\frac{BD}{DC}\cdot\frac{CE}{EA}\cdot\frac{AF}{FB}=1.$$
Menelaus' theorem for the triangle $ABC$ and the line $EFG$ (in terms of unsigned lengths):
$$\frac{BG}{GC}\cdot\frac{CE}{EA}\cdot\frac{AF}{FB}=1.$$
Dividing the two relations gives $\dfrac{BD}{DC}=\dfrac{BG}{GC}$.

**2.** A line cannot meet all three open sides of a triangle, and $E,F$ are interior to the sides $CA,AB$; hence the line $EF$ meets line $BC$ outside the segment $BC$. So $G$ is outside segment $BC$ while $D$ is inside, and they satisfy $\frac{DB}{DC}=\frac{GB}{GC}$: $D$ is the internal and $G$ the external point of division in the same ratio, that is $(B,C;D,G)=-1$ in cross-ratio language.

**3.** If $BD=DC$ then $\frac{CE}{EA}\cdot\frac{AF}{FB}=1$ by Ceva, i.e. $\frac{AF}{FB}=\frac{AE}{EC}$. By the converse of Thales' theorem $EF\parallel BC$. (This is the statement that the medians' cevian triangle is the medial triangle when all three cevians are medians.)

**Remark.** Another consequence: if $AD$ is the bisector of $\angle BAC$ and $AG$ is the external bisector, then $D$ and $G$ are harmonic conjugates; this is the same ratio that defines the Apollonius circle.

**Lesson.** Ceva and Menelaus share a common product; dividing one by the other isolates the harmonic ratio.

## Statement

Let $M$ be the midpoint of a chord $PQ$ of a circle. Through $M$ draw two other chords $AB$ and $CD$. The lines $AD$ and $BC$ meet the line $PQ$ at $X$ and $Y$ respectively (assume they are not parallel to $PQ$). Prove that $M$ is the midpoint of $XY$.

## Hint

Take $M$ as the origin and $PQ$ as the $x$-axis. A line through $M$ in direction $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ meets the circle at the points $t\,(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ where $t$ satisfies a quadratic: use Vieta's relations.

## Solution

**Setup.** Put $M=(0,0)$, $P=(-p,0)$, $Q=(p,0)$. The centre is on the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$, so it is $(0,c)$, and the circle is $x^2+(y-c)^2=p^2+c^2$. For the line through $M$ with unit direction $u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, the point $tu$ lies on the circle iff
$$t^2-2ct\sin\theta-p^2=0.$$
Its two roots $t_1,t_2$ satisfy $t_1+t_2=2c\sin\theta$ and $t_1t_2=-p^2$.

**Parametrising the points.** Let the chord $AB$ have direction $u=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$, with $A=t_1u$, $B=t_2u$, and the chord $CD$ have direction $v=(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$, with $C=s_1v$, $D=s_2v$, where $t_1+t_2=2c\sin\alpha$, $t_1t_2=-p^2$, $s_1+s_2=2c\sin\beta$, $s_1s_2=-p^2$.

**The intersection $X=AD\cap Ox$.** The points of line $AD$ are $(1-\lambda)t_1u+\lambda s_2v$. Their $y$-coordinate vanishes when $(1-\lambda)t_1\sin\alpha+\lambda s_2\sin\beta=0$, i.e. $\lambda=\dfrac{t_1\sin\alpha}{t_1\sin\alpha-s_2\sin\beta}$. The corresponding $x$-coordinate is
$$x_X=\frac{-s_2\sin\beta\cdot t_1\cos\alpha+t_1\sin\alpha\cdot s_2\cos\beta}{t_1\sin\alpha-s_2\sin\beta}=\frac{t_1s_2\sin(\alpha-\beta)}{t_1\sin\alpha-s_2\sin\beta}.$$
In the same way, for $Y=BC\cap Ox$ (replace $t_1,s_2$ by $t_2,s_1$):
$$x_Y=\frac{t_2s_1\sin(\alpha-\beta)}{t_2\sin\alpha-s_1\sin\beta}.$$

**Showing $x_X+x_Y=0$.** Since $\sin(\alpha-\beta)\ne0$ (the chords are distinct), $x_X+x_Y=0$ is equivalent to
$$t_1s_2\,(t_2\sin\alpha-s_1\sin\beta)+t_2s_1\,(t_1\sin\alpha-s_2\sin\beta)=0.$$
Group: the left side equals
$$\sin\alpha\;t_1t_2\,(s_1+s_2)-\sin\beta\;s_1s_2\,(t_1+t_2).$$
Substituting Vieta's relations:
$$\sin\alpha\cdot(-p^2)\cdot2c\sin\beta-\sin\beta\cdot(-p^2)\cdot2c\sin\alpha=0.$$
Hence $x_X=-x_Y$, so $M$ is the midpoint of $XY$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The theorem holds for any configuration (the labelling of the four endpoints matters: $A,B$ on one chord, $C,D$ on the other, with $X=AD\cap PQ$ and $Y=BC\cap PQ$).

**Lesson.** Parametrise all points of a configuration by distance along lines through a base point; the symmetric functions of the roots (Vieta) then do the work.

title: Two circles and a line through a common point

tags: spiral similarity, two intersecting circles, chord length, projection

## Statement

Two circles $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$, with centres $O_1,O_2$ and radii $R_1,R_2$, meet at two points $A$ and $B$. A line $\ell$ through $A$ meets $\Gamma_1$ again at $P$ and $\Gamma_2$ again at $Q$.

1. Prove that $\dfrac{BP}{BQ}=\dfrac{R_1}{R_2}$ for every such line $\ell$.
2. Prove that $PQ=2\,\bigl|\overrightarrow{O_1O_2}\cdot u\bigr|$, where $u$ is a unit vector along $\ell$.
3. Deduce that $PQ\le2\,O_1O_2$, with equality exactly when $\ell\parallel O_1O_2$.

## Hint

Part 1: in each circle, a chord equals $2R\sin$ of the inscribed angle that it subtends. Part 2: the foot of the perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord is the midpoint of the chord.

## Solution

**1.** In $\Gamma_1$, the chord $BP$ subtends the inscribed angle $\angle BAP$ at $A$, so $BP=2R_1\sin\angle BAP$. In $\Gamma_2$, $BQ=2R_2\sin\angle BAQ$. The points $P$, $A$, $Q$ are on the same line $\ell$, so the angles $\angle BAP$ and $\angle BAQ$ are equal or supplementary; in either case their sines are equal. Therefore
$$\frac{BP}{BQ}=\frac{R_1}{R_2}.$$
(So all the triangles $BPQ$ have the same ratio $BP:BQ$; they are all similar to each other, being images of one another by a spiral similarity centred at $B$.)

**2.** Take $A$ as origin and $u$ as the direction of $\ell$. The foot of the perpendicular from $O_1$ to $\ell$ is the midpoint of the chord $AP$, so $P=2(O_1\cdot u)\,u$. Similarly $Q=2(O_2\cdot u)\,u$. Hence
$$\overrightarrow{PQ}=2\bigl((O_2-O_1)\cdot u\bigr)u,\qquad PQ=2\,\bigl|\overrightarrow{O_1O_2}\cdot u\bigr|.$$

**3.** By Cauchy–Schwarz, $\bigl|\overrightarrow{O_1O_2}\cdot u\bigr|\le O_1O_2$, with equality iff $u$ is parallel to $\overrightarrow{O_1O_2}$. So $PQ\le2\,O_1O_2$, and the maximum is attained when $\ell$ is parallel to the line of centres.

**Remark.** The maximal segment $PQ$ has length exactly $2\,O_1O_2$; it is the "longest chord through $A$ cutting both circles".

**Lesson.** For two circles meeting at $A$, project onto the line through $A$: midpoints of chords are the feet of perpendiculars from the centres.

title: Ptolemy's inequality by inversion
category: olympiad/oly-geometry

difficulty: 4
tags: inversion, Ptolemy's inequality, triangle inequality
date: 2026-11-05

## Statement

For any four points $A,B,C,D$ in the plane, prove Ptolemy's inequality
$$AB\cdot CD+AD\cdot BC\ \ge\ AC\cdot BD.$$

## Hint

Invert about $A$ with radius $1$. First find the distance between the images of two points.

## Solution

**The inversion.** Assume $B,C,D\ne A$ (otherwise the inequality is trivial). The inversion with centre $A$ and radius $1$ sends a point $X\ne A$ to the point $X'$ on ray $AX$ with $AX\cdot AX'=1$.

**Lemma.** For $X,Y\ne A$: $X'Y'=\dfrac{XY}{AX\cdot AY}$.

*Proof.* Triangles $AX'Y'$ and $AYX$ share the angle at $A$ (the rays $AX'=AX$ and $AY'=AY$), and $\frac{AX'}{AY}=\frac{1/AX}{AY}=\frac1{AX\cdot AY}=\frac{AY'}{AX}$. So they are similar by SAS, with ratio $\frac1{AX\cdot AY}$, and $X'Y'=\frac{XY}{AX\cdot AY}$. (If $A,X,Y$ are collinear the formula follows directly from $\left|\frac1{AX}\mp\frac1{AY}\right|=\frac{|AY\mp AX|}{AX\cdot AY}$.)

**The inequality.** The triangle inequality in the images gives $B'D'\le B'C'+C'D'$. By the lemma,
$$\frac{BD}{AB\cdot AD}\le\frac{BC}{AB\cdot AC}+\frac{CD}{AC\cdot AD}.$$
Multiply by $AB\cdot AC\cdot AD$:
$$BD\cdot AC\le BC\cdot AD+CD\cdot AB.$$
This is Ptolemy's inequality. $\blacksquare$

**Remark (equality).** Equality holds in the triangle inequality when $C'$ lies on the segment $B'D'$. Since inversion maps a circle through $A$ to a line, this happens exactly when $A,B,C,D$ lie on a circle in this order (or on a line): Ptolemy's theorem for cyclic quadrilaterals.

**Lesson.** Inversion turns a product of distances into a sum of distances; the triangle inequality in the image gives the inequality in the original configuration.

## Statement

1. A circle has centre $O$ and radius $R$, and $P\ne O$ is a point inside the circle. What is the locus of the midpoints of all the chords passing through $P$?
2. Let $A\ne B$ be two points and $k$ a real number. What is the locus of the points $X$ such that $XA^2-XB^2=k$?

For part 1, the line from the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to the chord. For part 2, use coordinates with $A$ and $B$ on the $x$-axis.

## Solution

**1.** Let $M$ be the midpoint of a chord through $P$. If $M\ne O$ and $M\ne P$, then $OM\perp$ chord, and the chord passes through $P$, so $OM\perp MP$: $M$ is on the circle with diameter $OP$. If $M=P$ (the chord is perpendicular to $OP$) or $M=O$ (the chord is a diameter, contained in line $OP$), $M$ is still on this circle, since $O$ and $P$ are on it.

*Conversely*, let $M$ be on the circle with diameter $OP$. If $M\ne P$ and $M\ne O$, then the line $PM$ meets the big circle in a chord (as $P$ is inside), and the foot of the perpendicular from $O$ to this line is $M$ (since $\angle OMP=90^\circ$), which is the midpoint of the chord. For $M=P$ take the chord perpendicular to $OP$; for $M=O$ take the diameter through $P$.

**Answer.** The locus is the whole circle with diameter $OP$.

**2.** Put $A=(0,0)$ and $B=(d,0)$ with $d=AB>0$. For $X=(x,y)$,
$$XA^2-XB^2=x^2+y^2-(x-d)^2-y^2=2dx-d^2.$$
This equals $k$ iff $x=\dfrac{k+d^2}{2d}$.

**Answer.** The locus is a line perpendicular to $AB$, at abscissa $\frac{k+d^2}{2d}$ (for $k=0$, the perpendicular bisector of $AB$).

**Remark.** Part 2 is the key lemma behind radical axes: the points having equal powers with respect to two circles satisfy $XO_1^2-XO_2^2=R_1^2-R_2^2$, a line perpendicular to $O_1O_2$.

**Lesson.** A right angle gives a circle (Thales); a difference of squared distances gives a line.

## Statement

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with circumcentre $O$, circumradius $R$, orthocentre $H$, centroid $G$ and sides $a,b,c$.

1. Using vectors with origin $O$, prove that $\vec{OH}=\vec{OA}+\vec{OB}+\vec{OC}$, and deduce that $O,G,H$ are collinear with $OH=3\,OG$.
2. Prove that $OH^2=9R^2-(a^2+b^2+c^2)$.
3. Deduce $a^2+b^2+c^2\le9R^2$, with equality iff $ABC$ is equilateral.

## Hint

Set $h=a_v+b_v+c_v$ for the vectors $a_v=\vec{OA}$, etc., and show that $h-a_v$ is orthogonal to $b_v-c_v$ using $|b_v|=|c_v|=R$.

## Solution

Write $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$ for the vectors $\vec{OA},\vec{OB},\vec{OC}$; $|\mathbf a|=|\mathbf b|=|\mathbf c|=R$.

**1.** Let $\mathbf h=\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c$ and $K$ the point with $\vec{OK}=\mathbf h$. Then
$$(\mathbf h-\mathbf a)\cdot(\mathbf b-\mathbf c)=(\mathbf b+\mathbf c)\cdot(\mathbf b-\mathbf c)=|\mathbf b|^2-|\mathbf c|^2=0,$$
so $AK\perp BC$ (or $K=A$). By symmetry $BK\perp CA$ and $CK\perp AB$. So $K$ lies on the three altitudes: $K=H$. The centroid is $\vec{OG}=\frac{\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c}3$, hence $\vec{OH}=3\vec{OG}$.

**2.** Expanding,
$$OH^2=|\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c|^2=3R^2+2(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b+\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c+\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf a).$$
Also $c^2=|\mathbf a-\mathbf b|^2=2R^2-2\,\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b$, and similarly for $a^2,b^2$, so
$$a^2+b^2+c^2=6R^2-2(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b+\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c+\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf a).$$
Eliminating the sum of dot products, $OH^2=3R^2+\bigl(6R^2-(a^2+b^2+c^2)\bigr)=9R^2-(a^2+b^2+c^2)$.

**3.** $OH^2\ge0$ gives the inequality, with equality iff $H=O$, i.e. iff the altitudes pass through the circumcentre, which happens exactly for the equilateral triangle (each altitude is then also a perpendicular bisector).

**Check.** Right triangle with sides $3,4,5$ ($R=2.5$): the orthocentre is the right-angle vertex, and $OH$ is the median $2.5$; formula: $9\cdot6.25-50=6.25=2.5^2$ ✓.

**Remark.** Part 1 contains the Euler line: $H$, $G$, $O$ are collinear, with $G$ at one third of $OH$.

**Lesson.** Placing the origin at the circumcentre makes $|\mathbf a|=|\mathbf b|=|\mathbf c|$ and turns the orthocentre into the vector sum.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c$ be three complex numbers. Prove that $a,b,c$ are the vertices of an equilateral triangle (possibly degenerate, with $a=b=c$) if and only if
$$a^2+b^2+c^2=ab+bc+ca.$$

## Hint

Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. Factor $a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca$ using $\omega$ and $\omega^2$.

## Solution

**Factorisation.** With $\omega^2+\omega+1=0$ and $\omega^3=1$,
$$(a+\omega b+\omega^2c)(a+\omega^2b+\omega c)=a^2+b^2+c^2+(\omega+\omega^2)(ab+bc+ca)=a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca.$$
(The coefficient of $ab$ is $\omega^2+\omega=-1$, and similarly for $bc$ and $ca$; the squares appear with coefficient $1$ because $\omega\cdot\omega^2=1$ gives $b^2$, $c^2$ with coefficient $1$.) So the condition holds iff
$$a+\omega b+\omega^2c=0\qquad\text{or}\qquad a+\omega^2b+\omega c=0.$$

**Geometric meaning.** Since $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$, we have $a+\omega b+\omega^2c=(a-c)+\omega(b-c)$. Therefore $a+\omega b+\omega^2c=0$ iff
$$a-c=-\omega\,(b-c)=e^{-i\pi/3}(b-c).$$
This says: the vector $\vec{CA}$ is the vector $\vec{CB}$ rotated by $-60^\circ$. Then $CA=CB$ and $\angle ACB=60^\circ$ (in the non-degenerate case), so $ABC$ is equilateral. Likewise $a+\omega^2b+\omega c=0$ says $\vec{CA}$ is $\vec{CB}$ rotated by $+60^\circ$, the equilateral triangle with the other orientation. Conversely, every equilateral triangle satisfies one of these two relations, since $\vec{CA}$ is obtained from $\vec{CB}$ by a rotation of $\pm60^\circ$.

**Conclusion.** $a^2+b^2+c^2=ab+bc+ca$ iff $a,b,c$ form an equilateral triangle (degenerate when $b=c$ forces $a=b=c$).

**Check.** $a=0$, $b=1$, $c=e^{i\pi/3}$ gives $c^2=e^{2i\pi/3}$ and $ab+bc+ca=c$; we need $1+c^2=c$, i.e. $c^2-c+1=0$, whose roots are $e^{\pm i\pi/3}$ ✓.

**Lesson.** Factor symmetric expressions with cube roots of unity: multiplication by $\omega$ is a rotation by $120^\circ$, which is the language of equilateral triangles.

## Statement

In a triangle with sides $a,b,c$ and area $S$, prove that
$$a^2+b^2+c^2\ge4\sqrt3\,S,$$
with equality iff the triangle is equilateral.

## Hint

Use Heron's formula in the form $16S^2=2a^2b^2+2b^2c^2+2c^2a^2-a^4-b^4-c^4$, and square the inequality.

## Solution

**Heron's formula.** $16S^2=(a+b+c)(-a+b+c)(a-b+c)(a+b-c)=2(a^2b^2+b^2c^2+c^2a^2)-(a^4+b^4+c^4)$.

**Reduction.** Both sides of the claim are positive, so it is equivalent to its square:
$$(a^2+b^2+c^2)^2\ge48S^2=3\left[2(a^2b^2+b^2c^2+c^2a^2)-(a^4+b^4+c^4)\right].$$
Write $p=a^4+b^4+c^4$ and $q=a^2b^2+b^2c^2+c^2a^2$. The left side is $p+2q$, the right side $6q-3p$. The inequality becomes
$$4p\ge4q\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a^4+b^4+c^4\ge a^2b^2+b^2c^2+c^2a^2.$$

**Proof of the last inequality.**
$$a^4+b^4+c^4-a^2b^2-b^2c^2-c^2a^2=\tfrac12\left[(a^2-b^2)^2+(b^2-c^2)^2+(c^2-a^2)^2\right]\ge0.$$

**Equality.** It needs $a^2=b^2=c^2$, i.e. $a=b=c$.

**Remark.** The constant $4\sqrt3$ is sharp. A stronger inequality is Hadwiger–Finsler: $a^2+b^2+c^2\ge4\sqrt3S+(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2$.

**Lesson.** A geometric inequality involving an area becomes algebraic through Heron's formula; after squaring it is often a sum of squares.

title: The Erdős–Mordell inequality
category: olympiad/oly-geometry

difficulty: 5
tags: Erdős–Mordell, projections, pedal points, geometric inequality
date: 2026-11-05

## Statement

Let $P$ be a point inside a triangle $ABC$, and let $D,E,F$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $P$ to the lines $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ respectively. Prove that
$$PA+PB+PC\ge2\,(PD+PE+PF).$$

## Hint

Show first that $PA\ge\dfrac ca\,PE+\dfrac ba\,PF$. For this, note that $A,F,P,E$ lie on a circle of diameter $AP$, so $EF=PA\sin A$, and compare $EF$ with its projection on $BC$.

## Solution

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides, and $A,B,C$ the angles.

**Step 1: $EF=PA\sin A$.** The angles $\angle AFP$ and $\angle AEP$ are right angles, so $E$ and $F$ lie on the circle with diameter $AP$. In this circle the chord $EF$ subtends an inscribed angle at $A$ equal to $A$ or $180^\circ-A$ (it is the angle between lines $AB$ and $AC$), so $EF=AP\sin A$.

**Step 2: the projection of $EF$ on $BC$.** Let $d$ be a unit vector along $BC$. Place $B$ at the origin, $C=(a,0)$, with $A$ above the $x$-axis, $d=(1,0)$. The vector $\vec{PF}$ points from $P$ towards line $AB$, perpendicular to it; since $P$ is inside the triangle, it is the outward normal of side $AB$, namely $|PF|\,(-\sin B,\cos B)$. The vector $\vec{PE}$ is $|PE|$ times the outward normal of $CA$, which is $(\sin C,\cos C)$. Then
$$\vec{FE}=\vec{PE}-\vec{PF}\quad\text{has }x\text{-component}\quad|PE|\sin C+|PF|\sin B.$$
A segment is at least as long as its projection on any line, so
$$EF\ge PE\sin C+PF\sin B.$$

**Step 3.** Combining with Step 1: $PA\sin A\ge PE\sin C+PF\sin B$. Dividing by $\sin A$ and using the law of sines ($\frac{\sin C}{\sin A}=\frac ca$, $\frac{\sin B}{\sin A}=\frac ba$):
$$PA\ge\frac ca\,PE+\frac ba\,PF.$$
By cyclic permutation,
$$PB\ge\frac ab\,PF+\frac cb\,PD,\qquad PC\ge\frac bc\,PD+\frac ac\,PE.$$

**Step 4: summing.**
$$PA+PB+PC\ge PD\left(\frac cb+\frac bc\right)+PE\left(\frac ca+\frac ac\right)+PF\left(\frac ba+\frac ab\right)\ge2\,(PD+PE+PF),$$
because $x+\frac1x\ge2$ for $x>0$. $\blacksquare$

**Equality.** It requires $a=b=c$ and $P$ the centre of the equilateral triangle.

**Remark.** This is a famous result (conjectured by Erdős in 1935, first proved by Mordell and Barrow); the proof above is a short projection argument.

**Lesson.** A segment is longer than its projection; choose the projection direction so that the lengths to compare appear as projections of known perpendiculars.

## Statement

**Pick's theorem.** A polygon with vertices at lattice points (integer coordinates), $I$ lattice points in its interior and $B$ lattice points on its boundary, has area $I+\frac B2-1$.

Using it:

1. Compute the area and the number of interior lattice points of the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(5,2)$, $(2,3)$.
2. Prove that a lattice triangle having no lattice point other than its vertices has area $\frac12$.
3. Prove that there is no equilateral triangle with all three vertices at lattice points.

## Hint

For part 1, the number of lattice points on a segment from $(0,0)$ to $(p,q)$ (endpoints included) is $\gcd(p,q)+1$. For part 3, compare the area of an equilateral triangle with the rational numbers produced by Pick's formula.

## Solution

**1. The triangle.** Its area is $\frac12|5\cdot3-2\cdot2|=\frac{11}2$. The sides are the vectors $(5,2)$, $(-3,1)$, $(-2,-3)$, with gcds $1,1,1$, so each side contains only its two endpoints as lattice points: $B=3$. Pick's theorem: $\frac{11}2=I+\frac32-1$, so $I=5$. (Check by listing: the interior lattice points are $(1,1),(2,1),(2,2),(3,2),(4,2)$.)

**2. Triangles without other lattice points.** Here $I=0$ and $B=3$, so the area is $0+\frac32-1=\frac12$.

**3. No lattice equilateral triangle.** Suppose the vertices are lattice points and the side has squared length $n=a^2$, where $n=p^2+q^2$ is a positive integer (the squared distance between two lattice points). The area of the equilateral triangle is $\frac{\sqrt3}4\,n$. By Pick's theorem the area of a lattice polygon is a rational number $I+\frac B2-1$. But $\frac{\sqrt3}4n$ is irrational since $n$ is a positive integer and $\sqrt3$ is irrational. Contradiction.

**Remark.** The same argument rules out the regular hexagon and the regular $n$-gon for $n=3,6$ (areas are rational multiples of $\sqrt3$). In fact the square is the only regular polygon with lattice vertices in the plane; in three dimensions equilateral lattice triangles exist, e.g. $(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1)$.

**Lesson.** Pick's theorem forces rationality of areas of lattice polygons: any area containing $\sqrt3$ is impossible.

title: The symmedian and the tangents at B and C

## Statement

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with $\angle A$ acute, inscribed in a circle $\omega$. The tangents to $\omega$ at $B$ and $C$ meet at a point $T$. The line $AT$ meets $BC$ at $D$. Prove that
$$\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{c^2}{b^2},\qquad\text{where }b=CA,\ c=AB.$$
(Thus $AD$ is the reflection of the median in the bisector of $\angle A$: the symmedian.)

## Hint

Compare the areas of triangles $ABT$ and $ACT$, using $TB=TC$ and the tangent–chord angle.

## Solution

**Setup.** Since $\angle A$ is acute, the tangents at $B$ and $C$ meet at a point $T$ on the other side of $BC$ from $A$, and $TB=TC$ (equal tangent segments). By the tangent–chord theorem, $\angle TBC=\angle TCB=\angle A$.

**Angles at $B$ and $C$.** $\angle ABT=\angle ABC+\angle CBT=B+A$, so $\sin\angle ABT=\sin(A+B)=\sin C$. Similarly $\angle ACT=C+A$ and $\sin\angle ACT=\sin B$.

**Area ratio.** The points $B$ and $C$ are on opposite sides of line $AT$, and $D=AT\cap BC$, so
$$\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{[ABT]}{[ACT]}$$
(the triangles $ABT$ and $ACT$ have the common base $AT$ and heights proportional to $BD$ and $DC$). Using $[XYZ]=\frac12\,XY\cdot YZ\sin\angle XYZ$:
$$\frac{[ABT]}{[ACT]}=\frac{\frac12\,AB\cdot BT\sin\angle ABT}{\frac12\,AC\cdot CT\sin\angle ACT}=\frac{c\cdot\sin C}{b\cdot\sin B}\quad(\text{since }BT=CT).$$
By the law of sines $\frac{\sin C}{\sin B}=\frac cb$, so
$$\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{c^2}{b^2}.$$

**Why "symmedian".** The median from $A$ meets $BC$ at the midpoint with ratio $1$, the bisector at ratio $\frac cb$, and the symmedian at ratio $\frac{c^2}{b^2}$: the reflection of the median in the bisector has the squared ratio, as can be checked with the law of sines in the two sub-triangles.

**Remark.** If $\angle A$ is obtuse, the same computation works with $\angle ABT=|A+B-180^\circ|=C$ and the same sines. For $\angle A=90^\circ$ the tangents at $B$ and $C$ are parallel (they are perpendicular to the diameter $BC$) and $T$ does not exist.

**Lesson.** A ratio of segments on a line equals a ratio of areas with a common base; the tangent–chord angle then makes the sines match the sides.
