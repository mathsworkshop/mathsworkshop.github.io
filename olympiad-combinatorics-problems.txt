## Statement

At a party with $n\ge2$ people, friendship is mutual. Prove that two people have the same number of friends at the party.

## Hint

The number of friends of a person is an integer between $0$ and $n-1$. That gives $n$ possible values for $n$ people, which is one too many to conclude directly. Can the values $0$ and $n-1$ both occur?

## Solution

**Idea.** Count the possible values and show that there are really only $n-1$ of them.

**Values.** Each person has between $0$ and $n-1$ friends. Suppose some person $A$ has $0$ friends and some person $B$ has $n-1$ friends. Then $B$ is a friend of everybody, in particular of $A$, so $A$ has at least one friend: contradiction. Hence the values $0$ and $n-1$ do not occur together.

**Pigeonhole.**
- If nobody has $0$ friends, all the numbers of friends lie in $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$.
- If somebody has $0$ friends, then nobody has $n-1$ friends, and all the numbers lie in $\{0,\dots,n-2\}$.

In both cases the $n$ people take at most $n-1$ different values, so two people have the same number of friends.

**Remark.** The statement is false if we allow a person to be their own friend or friendships to be one-way. The mutuality is used exactly once, in the key step.

**Lesson.** Pigeonhole: count the boxes carefully, and look for a hidden constraint that removes one box.

## Statement

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ be integers. Prove that there exist $i\le j$ such that the sum $a_i+a_{i+1}+\dots+a_j$ is divisible by $n$.

## Hint

Consider the $n+1$ partial sums $S_0=0$, $S_1=a_1$, $S_2=a_1+a_2$, $\dots$, $S_n$ and their remainders modulo $n$.

## Solution

**Idea.** A block sum is a difference of two partial sums.

**Pigeonhole.** The $n+1$ numbers $S_0,S_1,\dots,S_n$ have only $n$ possible remainders modulo $n$, so two of them, say $S_p$ and $S_q$ with $p<q$, have the same remainder. Then $n$ divides
$$S_q-S_p=a_{p+1}+a_{p+2}+\dots+a_q.$$
Taking $i=p+1$ and $j=q$ gives the required block.

**Remark.** The result is sharp: for $n-1$ numbers it fails. Take $a_1=\dots=a_{n-1}=1$: every block sum is between $1$ and $n-1$, hence not divisible by $n$.

**Lesson.** Turn sums of blocks into differences of prefix sums, then apply the pigeonhole principle to remainders.

title: Monotone subsequences (Erdős–Szekeres)
category: olympiad/oly-combinatorics

tags: pigeonhole, sequences, Erdős–Szekeres
date: 2026-11-04

## Statement

Let $n\ge1$. Prove that every sequence of $n^2+1$ distinct real numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length $n+1$ or a decreasing subsequence of length $n+1$.

## Hint

To each term $a_i$ attach the pair $(x_i,y_i)$, where $x_i$ is the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending at $a_i$ and $y_i$ the length of the longest decreasing subsequence ending at $a_i$. Show that these pairs are pairwise distinct.

## Solution

**Idea.** Label every term by two numbers, and show that the labels never repeat.

**Distinct labels.** Let $i<j$.
- If $a_i<a_j$, any increasing subsequence ending at $a_i$ can be extended by $a_j$, so $x_j\ge x_i+1$.
- If $a_i>a_j$ (the numbers are distinct), any decreasing subsequence ending at $a_i$ can be extended by $a_j$, so $y_j\ge y_i+1$.

In both cases $(x_i,y_i)\ne(x_j,y_j)$.

**Counting.** Suppose, for contradiction, that all the increasing and decreasing subsequences have length at most $n$. Then $1\le x_i,y_i\le n$ for all $i$, so there are at most $n^2$ possible pairs. But we have $n^2+1$ terms with pairwise distinct pairs: contradiction. Hence some $x_i$ or $y_i$ is at least $n+1$.

**Remark.** The bound $n^2+1$ is sharp: split $n^2$ numbers into $n$ blocks of $n$ consecutive values, arrange each block in decreasing order and put the blocks in increasing order. The longest monotone subsequence has length $n$.

**Lesson.** When pigeonhole directly fails, build a cleverly chosen label for each object so that distinct objects get distinct labels.

## Statement

Let $n\ge1$ and let $S$ be a set of $n+1$ distinct integers chosen from $\{1,2,\dots,2n\}$. Prove that $S$ contains two numbers such that one divides the other. Show that the statement fails for sets of $n$ numbers.

## Hint

Write every integer as $2^k\cdot m$ with $m$ odd. How many possible values of $m$ are there?

## Solution

**Idea.** Sort the numbers into boxes according to their odd part.

**Boxes.** Every $a\in\{1,\dots,2n\}$ can be written uniquely as $a=2^km$ with $k\ge0$ and $m$ odd. The odd part $m$ is one of the $n$ odd numbers $1,3,\dots,2n-1$.

**Pigeonhole.** The $n+1$ chosen numbers go into $n$ boxes, so two of them, $a=2^km$ and $b=2^lm$ with $a\ne b$, share the same odd part $m$. Then $k\ne l$, and if $k<l$ then $a$ divides $b$ (since $b=2^{l-k}a$); otherwise $b$ divides $a$.

**Sharpness.** The $n$ numbers $n+1,n+2,\dots,2n$ contain no pair where one divides the other, because a proper multiple of $a\ge n+1$ is at least $2a>2n$.

**Lesson.** Decompose objects into a canonical form (here, power of two times odd part) and use one component as the pigeonhole box.

## Statement

The numbers $1,2,\dots,10$ are written on a board. A move consists of erasing two numbers $a$ and $b$ and writing $|a-b|$ instead. After nine moves, a single number remains. Prove that it is odd.

## Hint

How does the parity of the sum of all numbers on the board change after a move?

## Solution

**Idea.** Find a quantity that a move cannot change, at least modulo $2$.

**Invariant.** A move replaces the two numbers $a,b$ by $|a-b|$, so the sum $T$ of the numbers on the board changes by
$$-(a+b)+|a-b|=-2\min(a,b),$$
which is even. Hence the parity of $T$ never changes.

**Conclusion.** At the start $T=1+2+\dots+10=55$, which is odd. After nine moves, $T$ is the single remaining number, and it is odd.

**Remark.** The same argument works for $1,\dots,n$: the final number has the parity of $\frac{n(n+1)}2$. In particular, for $n\equiv1,2\pmod4$ (where $\frac{n(n+1)}2$ is odd) the final number can never be $0$.

**Lesson.** Invariant: find a quantity (often a parity, a sum modulo something, or a product) that every allowed move preserves.

## Statement

In a parliament, each member has at most $3$ enemies, and enmity is mutual. Prove that the members can be split into two houses so that every member has at most $1$ enemy in his own house.

## Hint

Start with an arbitrary split. If somebody has at least $2$ enemies in his own house, move him to the other house. Consider the number of pairs of enemies who sit in the same house.

## Solution

**Idea.** Use a monovariant: a nonnegative integer that strictly decreases at each step, so that the process must stop.

**Process.** Start with any split of the members into two houses. Let $E$ be the number of pairs of enemies belonging to the same house. If every member has at most one enemy in his own house, we are done. Otherwise, take a member $x$ with at least $2$ enemies in his own house.

**Effect of the move.** Since $x$ has at most $3$ enemies in total, he has at most $1$ enemy in the other house. Move $x$ to the other house.
- The pairs $\{x,y\}$ with $y$ an enemy in his former house are no longer in the same house: at least $2$ pairs are removed from the count.
- The pairs $\{x,y\}$ with $y$ an enemy in the other house now count: at most $1$ new pair.
- All other pairs are unaffected.

Hence $E$ decreases by at least $1$.

**Termination.** $E$ is a nonnegative integer, so the process cannot continue forever. When it stops, nobody has $2$ or more enemies in his own house, which is what we wanted.

**Lesson.** Monovariant: to prove that a configuration with some property exists, start anywhere and improve a nonnegative integer quantity until it can no longer be improved.

## Statement

Two opposite corner squares are removed from an $8\times8$ chessboard. Can the remaining $62$ squares be tiled by $31$ dominoes ($1\times2$ rectangles)?

## Hint

Use the usual black and white colouring and count the squares of each colour.

## Solution

**Idea.** Every domino covers exactly one black and one white square.

**Colouring.** Colour the board like a chessboard. Two opposite corners, for instance $(1,1)$ and $(8,8)$, have the same colour: the colour of $(i,j)$ depends on the parity of $i+j$, and $1+1$ and $8+8$ are both even.

**Counting.** The full board has $32$ black and $32$ white squares. Removing two squares of the same colour, say both white, leaves $32$ black and $30$ white squares.

**Contradiction.** Any tiling by $31$ dominoes covers exactly $31$ black and $31$ white squares, since each domino covers one square of each colour. But there are $32$ black squares: impossible.

**Answer.** No.

**Remark.** If the two removed squares have different colours, a tiling always exists (a theorem of Gomory). So the colouring argument is not just a trick: it captures the exact obstruction here.

**Lesson.** Colouring: choose a colouring in which every tile covers a prescribed number of squares of each colour, then compare with the colour counts of the region.

## Statement

Is it possible to tile a $10\times10$ board with $25$ rectangles of size $1\times4$ (each placed horizontally or vertically)?

## Hint

The chessboard colouring is useless here. Try four colours, according to the remainder of $i+j$ modulo $4$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Choose a colouring for which every tile covers one square of each colour.

**Colouring.** Give the square in row $i$ and column $j$ ($1\le i,j\le10$) the colour $(i+j)\bmod4\in\{0,1,2,3\}$. A tile placed horizontally covers squares $(i,j),(i,j+1),(i,j+2),(i,j+3)$, whose colours are four consecutive residues modulo $4$: all different. The same holds for a vertical tile.

**Consequence.** A tiling by $25$ tiles would cover exactly $25$ squares of each colour.

**Counting.** Counting the squares of each colour on the $10\times10$ board gives
- colour $0$: $25$ squares,
- colour $1$: $24$ squares,
- colour $2$: $25$ squares,
- colour $3$: $26$ squares.

(For instance, for colour $3$ the pairs $(i,j)$ with $i+j\equiv3\pmod4$ are counted row by row, giving $26$ in total.) Since colour $3$ has $26$ squares, a tiling is impossible.

**Answer.** No.

**Remark.** The argument adapts to every $n\times n$ board: a tiling by $1\times4$ tiles exists if and only if $4$ divides $n$.

**Lesson.** When a tile has length $k$, colour the board with $k$ colours along diagonals; each tile then covers all the colours once.

## Statement

Prove that there is no finite non-empty set $S$ of points in the plane such that every point of $S$ is the midpoint of two other points of $S$.

## Hint

Look at the point of $S$ that is largest in some sense: for instance, largest abscissa, and among those the largest ordinate.

## Solution

**Idea.** An extreme point can never be the midpoint of two other points.

**Extreme point.** Since $S$ is finite and non-empty, we can choose the point $P=(p,q)$ of $S$ with the largest abscissa $p$, and, among the points with abscissa $p$, the largest ordinate $q$.

**Contradiction.** Suppose $P$ is the midpoint of two other points $A=(a_1,a_2)$ and $B=(b_1,b_2)$ of $S$. Then $\frac{a_1+b_1}2=p$ with $a_1\le p$ and $b_1\le p$, so $a_1=b_1=p$. Now $A$ and $B$ both have abscissa $p$, hence $a_2\le q$ and $b_2\le q$, and $\frac{a_2+b_2}2=q$ forces $a_2=b_2=q$. Then $A=B=P$, but $A$ and $B$ were supposed to differ from $P$. Contradiction.

**Remark.** For an infinite set the statement is false: $\mathbb{Z}^2$ has this property. The proof uses finiteness exactly to guarantee that an extreme point exists.

**Lesson.** Extremal principle: look at a point that maximises or minimises some quantity (a coordinate, a distance, an area); it often has special properties that lead to a contradiction.

## Statement

An L-tromino is the shape made of three unit squares forming an "L". Prove that for every $n\ge1$, a $2^n\times2^n$ board from which any single square has been removed can be tiled by L-trominoes.

## Hint

Divide the board into four quadrants. After placing one L-tromino well, each quadrant becomes an instance of the problem for $n-1$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Induction on $n$, with a first move that recreates four smaller copies of the same problem.

**Base case.** For $n=1$, a $2\times2$ board minus one square is exactly one L-tromino.

**Induction step.** Assume the statement for $n-1$ and take a $2^n\times2^n$ board with one square removed. Divide it into four quadrants of size $2^{n-1}\times2^{n-1}$. The removed square lies in one of them.

Consider the four squares at the centre of the board: one in each quadrant. Place one L-tromino covering the three central squares that lie in the three quadrants **not** containing the removed square. These three central squares do form an L-shape, since any three of the four central squares do.

Now each quadrant has exactly one square that is missing (the removed one in the first quadrant, and a square covered by the central tromino in each of the other three). By the induction hypothesis, each quadrant minus that square can be tiled by L-trominoes. Together with the central tromino, this tiles the whole board.

**Remark.** The count is consistent: $4^n-1$ is divisible by $3$, so the number of squares left is a multiple of $3$. The proof is also an algorithm, which gives a tiling explicitly.

**Lesson.** Induction with a self-similar structure: choose a first step that reduces the problem to several smaller problems of exactly the same kind.

## Statement

$n$ lines are drawn in the plane so that no two are parallel and no three pass through a common point. Prove that they divide the plane into $\dfrac{n^2+n+2}2$ regions.

## Hint

Add the lines one at a time and count how many new regions the last line creates.

## Solution

**Idea.** Let $R_n$ be the number of regions. Compare $R_{n+1}$ and $R_n$.

**Induction.** For $n=0$ there is one region, and $\frac{0+0+2}2=1$.

Suppose the formula holds for $n$ lines and add an $(n+1)$-th line $\ell$. It crosses each of the $n$ existing lines, at $n$ distinct points (no two lines are parallel, and no three lines are concurrent). These $n$ points cut $\ell$ into $n+1$ pieces (two rays and $n-1$ segments).

Each piece lies inside one existing region and splits it into two parts. Different pieces lie in different regions: two pieces in the same region would be separated by no line, so the region (convex) would contain the segment between them, which would then be inside the region, a contradiction with $\ell$ crossing a line between them. Thus exactly $n+1$ new regions are created:
$$R_{n+1}=R_n+n+1.$$

**Computation.**
$$\frac{n^2+n+2}2+n+1=\frac{n^2+3n+4}2=\frac{(n+1)^2+(n+1)+2}2,$$
which is the formula for $n+1$.

**Remark.** Equivalently $R_n=1+\binom n1+\binom n2$. Such "general position" counts are the starting point of the theory of hyperplane arrangements.

**Lesson.** To count regions or objects built incrementally, count the change produced by the last object added.

title: Triangle-free graphs have at most n²/4 edges (Mantel)
category: olympiad/oly-combinatorics
level: olympiad
difficulty: 4
tags: double counting, graphs, Cauchy–Schwarz, extremal graph theory
date: 2026-11-04

## Statement

A graph on $n$ vertices has no triangle. Prove that it has at most $\dfrac{n^2}4$ edges. When is equality possible?

## Hint

If $u$ and $v$ are adjacent, can they have a common neighbour? Deduce a bound on $d(u)+d(v)$, then sum over all edges.

## Solution

**Idea.** Count the pairs (vertex, edge containing it) in two ways.

**Local bound.** Let $uv$ be an edge. If a vertex $w$ were adjacent to both $u$ and $v$, then $uvw$ would be a triangle. So the neighbourhoods of $u$ and $v$ are disjoint, and they are contained in the $n$ vertices of the graph:
$$d(u)+d(v)\le n.$$

**Summation.** Let $e$ be the number of edges. Summing over all edges $uv$,
$$\sum_{uv\in E}\bigl(d(u)+d(v)\bigr)\le ne.$$
On the left, each vertex $v$ appears once for every edge containing it, that is $d(v)$ times, each time with the term $d(v)$. So the left side equals $\sum_vd(v)^2$:
$$\sum_vd(v)^2\le ne.$$

**Cauchy–Schwarz.** Since $\sum_vd(v)=2e$,
$$\sum_vd(v)^2\ge\frac{\left(\sum_vd(v)\right)^2}n=\frac{4e^2}n.$$

**Conclusion.** $\dfrac{4e^2}n\le ne$, so $e\le\dfrac{n^2}4$.

**Equality.** For $n$ even, the complete bipartite graph $K_{n/2,n/2}$ is triangle-free and has $\frac{n^2}4$ edges. For $n$ odd, the bound is $\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}4\right\rfloor$ and is attained by $K_{(n-1)/2,(n+1)/2}$.

**Lesson.** Double counting: when a sum over edges involves degrees, rewrite it as a sum over vertices; combine with Cauchy–Schwarz to compare $\sum d^2$ with $\left(\sum d\right)^2$.

title: A tree has at least as many leaves as its maximum degree

## Statement

A tree is a connected graph without cycles. A leaf is a vertex of degree $1$.

1. Prove that a tree with $n\ge2$ vertices has exactly $n-1$ edges.
2. Prove that if a tree has a vertex of degree $k$, then it has at least $k$ leaves.

## Hint

For part 1, remove a leaf. For part 2, use the handshake lemma $\sum_v\deg v=2\times\#\text{edges}$.

## Solution

**1.** First, a tree with $n\ge2$ vertices has a leaf: take a longest path $v_0v_1\dots v_m$ in the tree (it has at least one edge). The vertex $v_0$ has no neighbour outside the path (the path would be longer) and no neighbour $v_i$ with $i\ge2$ (that would create a cycle), so its only neighbour is $v_1$.

Now induct on $n$. For $n=2$ the tree is one edge. For $n>2$, remove a leaf and its edge: the remaining graph is still connected and acyclic, with $n-1$ vertices and, by induction, $n-2$ edges. So the original tree has $n-1$ edges.

**2.** Let $v$ be a vertex of degree $k\ge2$ (for $k=1$ the claim is trivial as there are at least $2$ leaves... in fact at least $2\ge1$). Let $L$ be the number of leaves. Each leaf has degree $1$; $v$ has degree $k$; all other $n-L-1$ vertices are not leaves and have degree at least $2$. By the handshake lemma and part 1,
$$2(n-1)=\sum_u\deg u\ge L+k+2(n-L-1).$$
Simplifying the right side: $2n-L-2+k$. Therefore $2n-2\ge2n-L-2+k$, i.e. $L\ge k$.

**Equality.** It holds when all vertices other than $v$ and the leaves have degree exactly $2$, that is when the tree is a "spider": paths glued at $v$.

**Lesson.** Handshake lemma: relate the degrees to the number of edges, and use inequalities on the degrees of the non-special vertices.

## Statement

In a tournament, $n$ players play each other exactly once and there are no draws. Prove that the players can be ordered $P_1,P_2,\dots,P_n$ so that $P_1$ beat $P_2$, $P_2$ beat $P_3$, and so on up to $P_{n-1}$ beat $P_n$.

## Hint

Induction on $n$: remove one player, order the others, and find a place where the removed player can be inserted.

## Solution

**Idea.** Insert the last player into an ordering of the others.

**Base case.** For $n=1$ there is nothing to prove.

**Induction step.** Take a tournament with $n+1$ players and remove a player $v$. By the induction hypothesis the other $n$ players can be ordered $P_1,\dots,P_n$ with $P_i$ beating $P_{i+1}$ for all $i$. We insert $v$.
- If $v$ beat $P_1$, put $v$ first: $v,P_1,\dots,P_n$.
- If $P_n$ beat $v$, put $v$ last.
- Otherwise $P_1$ beat $v$ and $v$ beat $P_n$. Let $j$ be the smallest index with $v$ beating $P_j$. It exists since $v$ beats $P_n$, and $j\ge2$ since $P_1$ beat $v$. By minimality of $j$, $v$ did not beat $P_{j-1}$, so $P_{j-1}$ beat $v$ (there are no draws). Insert $v$ between $P_{j-1}$ and $P_j$:
$$\dots,P_{j-1},v,P_j,\dots$$
Then $P_{j-1}$ beats $v$ and $v$ beats $P_j$, and all the other consecutive pairs are unchanged.

In all cases we obtain the required ordering of all $n+1$ players.

**Remark.** An ordering is not unique, and a tournament need not have a player who beats everybody (a three-player cycle). The statement is Rédei's theorem (1934).

**Lesson.** Induction on the number of vertices: delete one, apply the hypothesis, and find a flexible place to reinsert.

## Statement

Two players take turns placing identical round coins on a circular table. Each coin must lie completely on the table and must not overlap the coins already placed (touching is allowed). The player who cannot place a coin loses. (A coin fits on the table.) Prove that the first player has a winning strategy.

## Hint

Use the centre of the table. The first player should have a reply to every possible move of the second one.

## Solution

**Idea.** A symmetry strategy: after the first move, always answer by the symmetric move.

**Strategy.** Let $O$ be the centre of the table and $r$ the radius of a coin. The first player puts his first coin with its centre at $O$. Afterwards, each time the opponent places a coin with centre $P$, the first player places a coin with centre $P'$, the reflection of $P$ in $O$.

**Invariant.** Just before each move of the second player, the position is symmetric with respect to $O$: this is true after the first move, and each pair of moves (opponent's, then ours) adds two symmetric coins.

**The reply is legal.** Let the second player place a coin at $P$ (legal, so $P\ne O$ since the centre is occupied).
- *On the table:* the table is symmetric about $O$, so the coin at $P'$ lies completely on the table.
- *No overlap with the coin at $P$:* two coins overlap iff their centres are at distance less than $2r$. Here $PP'=2\,OP$. If $2\,OP<2r$, then $OP<r<2r$, so the coin at $P$ would overlap the central coin: impossible, as the move at $P$ was legal. So $PP'\ge2r$.
- *No overlap with the other coins:* suppose the coin at $P'$ overlapped some coin $C$ already present. By the symmetry of the position before the move, the reflection $C'$ of $C$ is also present, and the coin at $P$ would overlap $C'$: impossible.

**Conclusion.** The first player always has a legal reply. Since the table has finite area, the game ends after finitely many moves, and the player who cannot move is the second one.

**Lesson.** Symmetry strategy: if the board has a central symmetry, occupy the centre and mirror every move of the opponent; check carefully that the mirrored move is always legal.

date: 2026-11-04
---

## Statement

1. A pile contains $n$ stones. Two players alternately remove between $1$ and $5$ stones. The player who takes the last stone wins. For which $n$ does the first player have a winning strategy?
2. There are two piles with the same number $m\ge1$ of stones. Two players alternately remove any positive number of stones from one pile of their choice. The player who takes the last stone wins. Who wins?

## Hint

For part 1, find the positions that are lost for the player to move, starting from small $n$. Then guess the pattern. For part 2, think of a pairing strategy.

## Solution

**1. Winning positions.** Positions $n=1,\dots,5$ are winning for the player to move (take everything). The position $n=6$ is losing: whatever he removes ($k$ stones, $1\le k\le5$), the opponent faces $6-k\in\{1,\dots,5\}$ and wins. We claim that **the first player loses if and only if $6$ divides $n$**.

- *If $6\mid n$:* the second player answers each move "remove $k$" by "remove $6-k$", which is legal since $1\le6-k\le5$. After each of his moves the number of stones is a multiple of $6$. The first player can never reach $0$, and the second player eventually does take the last stone.
- *If $6\nmid n$:* the first player removes $r=n\bmod6\in\{1,\dots,5\}$ stones, leaving a multiple of $6$ and then plays the second player's strategy above.

**Answer 1.** The first player wins iff $n$ is not a multiple of $6$.

**2. Pairing.** The second player wins. After each move of the first player, he removes the same number of stones from the other pile. Then the piles are equal after each of his moves. The first player can never take the last stone, since after his move one pile is non-empty (the other has more stones than it); the second player finishes by taking the last stones of the second pile.

**Remark.** These are the simplest cases of the theory of impartial games (Sprague–Grundy), whose general result for sums of piles is Bouton's solution of Nim.

**Lesson.** Compute the losing positions from the end of the game, then find a pairing or complement strategy that keeps the opponent in a losing position.

## Statement

1. Let $a_n$ be the number of subsets of $\{1,2,\dots,n\}$ (including the empty set) that contain no two consecutive integers. Prove that $a_n=F_{n+2}$, where $F_1=F_2=1$ and $F_{k+2}=F_{k+1}+F_k$.
2. Prove that the number of $k$-element subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ without two consecutive integers is $\dbinom{n-k+1}k$.

## Hint

For part 1, split according to whether $n$ belongs to the subset. For part 2, subtract $i-1$ from the $i$-th smallest element.

## Solution

**1. Recurrence.** Take $n\ge2$ and split the admissible subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ into two classes.
- Those not containing $n$: they are exactly the admissible subsets of $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$, so there are $a_{n-1}$ of them.
- Those containing $n$: they do not contain $n-1$, and removing $n$ leaves an admissible subset of $\{1,\dots,n-2\}$; conversely adding $n$ to such a subset is always admissible. So there are $a_{n-2}$ of them.

Hence $a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}$. The initial values are $a_0=1$ (the empty set) and $a_1=2$ (the empty set and $\{1\}$), which are $F_2$ and $F_3$. By induction $a_n=F_{n+2}$. (For example $a_9=89=F_{11}$.)

**2. Bijection.** Let $x_1<x_2<\dots<x_k$ be an admissible subset, so $x_{i+1}\ge x_i+2$. Define $y_i=x_i-(i-1)$. Then
$$y_{i+1}-y_i=x_{i+1}-x_i-1\ge1,$$
so $y_1<y_2<\dots<y_k$, and $1\le y_1$, $y_k=x_k-k+1\le n-k+1$. Conversely, from any $k$-subset $y_1<\dots<y_k$ of $\{1,\dots,n-k+1\}$, setting $x_i=y_i+(i-1)$ gives an admissible subset of $\{1,\dots,n\}$. These maps are inverse to each other, so the count is $\binom{n-k+1}k$.

**Check.** Summing part 2 over $k$ gives $\sum_k\binom{n-k+1}k=F_{n+2}$, a classical identity.

**Lesson.** Recurrence by conditioning on the last element, and bijections that "squeeze out" a constraint by shifting indices.

## Statement

A derangement of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ is a permutation with no fixed point. Let $D_n$ be their number.

1. Prove that $D_n=n!\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$.
2. Compute $D_4$, and show that for $n\ge2$, $D_n$ is the integer closest to $\dfrac{n!}e$.

## Hint

Let $A_i$ be the set of permutations fixing $i$. Apply the inclusion–exclusion principle to $A_1\cup\dots\cup A_n$.

## Solution

**1. Inclusion–exclusion.** The number of permutations is $n!$, and $D_n=n!-\left|A_1\cup\dots\cup A_n\right|$. For a set $I$ of $k$ indices, the permutations fixing every element of $I$ number $(n-k)!$: they are arbitrary permutations of the other $n-k$ elements. There are $\binom nk$ such sets $I$. Therefore
$$D_n=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nk(n-k)!=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\frac{n!}{k!}=n!\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}.$$

**2. Values.** $D_4=24-24+12-4+1=9$. (Direct check: the $9$ derangements of $\{1,2,3,4\}$ are the three products of two disjoint transpositions and the six $4$-cycles.)

**Closest integer.** Since $e^{-1}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$,
$$\frac{n!}e-D_n=n!\sum_{k>n}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}.$$
The series is alternating with decreasing terms, so its absolute value is at most the first term: $\left|\frac{n!}e-D_n\right|\le\frac{n!}{(n+1)!}=\frac1{n+1}\le\frac13<\frac12$ for $n\ge2$. So $D_n$ is the integer closest to $\frac{n!}e$.

**Consequence.** The probability that a random permutation has no fixed point tends to $\frac1e\approx0.368$ and is already $\frac{9}{24}=0.375$ for $n=4$.

**Lesson.** Inclusion–exclusion: when the intersections of the "bad" events all have the same size depending only on the number of events, the sum collapses to a clean formula.

## Statement

Prove that for every $n\ge1$,
$$\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk^2=\binom{2n}n.$$

## Hint

Count in two ways the committees of $n$ people chosen from a group of $n$ men and $n$ women.

## Solution

**Idea.** Interpret both sides as the number of ways of choosing $n$ people among $2n$.

**Right side.** Among $n$ men and $n$ women, the number of committees of $n$ people is $\binom{2n}n$.

**Left side.** Classify the committees according to the number $k$ of men, with $0\le k\le n$. Then there are $n-k$ women. The number of such committees is
$$\binom nk\binom n{n-k}=\binom nk\binom nk=\binom nk^2,$$
using $\binom n{n-k}=\binom nk$. Summing over $k$ gives the left side.

**Check.** For $n=3$: $1+9+9+1=20=\binom63$.

**Generalisation.** The same argument with $m$ men and $n$ women, and a committee of size $r$, gives Vandermonde's identity
$$\sum_{k}\binom mk\binom n{r-k}=\binom{m+n}r.$$

**Lesson.** Double counting: to prove an identity, find a set whose size can be computed in two different ways, one for each side.

title: Paths that never go below the axis: Catalan numbers

## Statement

A path of length $2n$ starts at height $0$ and at each step goes up by $1$ or down by $1$. Prove that the number of such paths that end at height $0$ and never go below height $0$ is
$$C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}n.$$

## Hint

Count all paths from $0$ to $0$, then subtract the bad ones. For a bad path, reflect the part before the first visit to height $-1$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The reflection principle: bad paths are in bijection with paths that are easy to count.

**All paths.** A path from $0$ to $0$ with $2n$ steps has $n$ up-steps and $n$ down-steps, so there are $\binom{2n}n$ of them.

**Bad paths.** A path from $0$ to $0$ is bad if it reaches height $-1$ at some moment. Consider the first such moment $t$, and reflect the portion of the path before $t$ about the line $y=-1$ (exchange up and down in the first $t$ steps), keeping the rest unchanged. The new path starts at $-2$ (the reflection of $0$), still reaches $-1$ for the first time at time $t$, and ends at $0$.

Conversely, take any path from $-2$ to $0$ with $2n$ steps. It must cross the height $-1$ (it goes from $-2$ to $0$ and steps are $\pm1$); reflecting the part before the first visit to $-1$ gives a bad path from $0$ to $0$. These two operations are inverse to each other (the first visit to $-1$ is the same for both paths), so bad paths are in bijection with all paths from $-2$ to $0$.

**Counting those.** A path from $-2$ to $0$ in $2n$ steps has $u$ up-steps and $d$ down-steps with $u+d=2n$ and $u-d=2$: $u=n+1$, $d=n-1$. So there are $\binom{2n}{n+1}$ of them.

**Conclusion.**
$$C_n=\binom{2n}n-\binom{2n}{n+1}=\binom{2n}n\left(1-\frac n{n+1}\right)=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}n.$$
The first values are $1,2,5,14,42,132,\dots$

**Remark.** The same numbers count the well-formed sequences of $n$ pairs of brackets, the triangulations of a convex $(n+2)$-gon and many other families.

**Lesson.** Reflection principle: reflect an initial segment to transform a constrained count into an unconstrained one.

## Statement

The squares of an $n\times n$ board ($n\ge2$) are all white. A move chooses one row or one column and changes the colour of every square in it (white to black and black to white). Prove that it is impossible to obtain a board with exactly one black square.

## Hint

Look at a $2\times2$ block formed by two rows and two columns, and count its black squares modulo $2$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Find a quantity that no move can change, and compare the start with the target.

**Invariant.** Fix two rows $r\ne r'$ and two columns $c\ne c'$, and consider the four squares $(r,c),(r,c'),(r',c),(r',c')$. Look at the parity of the number of black squares among these four.
- A move on row $r$ (or $r'$) changes the colour of exactly two of these four squares, $(r,c)$ and $(r,c')$ (or the two squares of row $r'$).
- A move on column $c$ (or $c'$) likewise changes exactly two of them.
- A move on any other row or column changes none of them.

Changing the colour of two squares changes the number of black squares among them by $-2$, $0$ or $+2$: the parity of the number of black squares among the four is invariant.

**Conclusion.** At the start this number is $0$, which is even, so it stays even forever. Now suppose a board with a single black square $(a,b)$ could be reached. Choose any other row $r'\ne a$ and column $c'\ne b$ (possible since $n\ge2$) and the block of the rows $a,r'$ and columns $b,c'$: it contains exactly one black square, an odd number. Contradiction.

**Remark.** In fact the reachable boards are exactly those in which every $2\times2$ block as above has an even number of black squares, but we only needed the necessary condition.

**Lesson.** Invariant: restrict attention to a small sub-configuration (here a $2\times2$ block) on which each move acts in a controlled way.

## Statement

Among $N$ coins, exactly one is heavier than the others, which all have the same weight. You may use a balance scale (comparing the weights of two groups of coins, with three possible outcomes: left heavier, right heavier, balance).

1. Prove that if $N=3^k$, the heavy coin can always be found in $k$ weighings.
2. Prove that if $N>3^k$, it is not always possible to find it in $k$ weighings.

## Hint

For part 1, divide the coins into three equal groups. For part 2, count the possible sequences of outcomes of the weighings.

## Solution

**1. Strategy.** Induction on $k$. For $k=0$ there is a single coin, and it is the heavy one. For $k\ge1$, divide the $3^k$ coins into three groups $A,B,C$ of $3^{k-1}$ coins and weigh $A$ against $B$.
- If $A$ is heavier, the heavy coin is in $A$; if $B$ is heavier, it is in $B$.
- If the scale balances, it is in $C$.

In each case, the heavy coin is among $3^{k-1}$ coins, which can be found in $k-1$ further weighings by the induction hypothesis. In total $k$ weighings suffice.

**2. Lower bound.** Fix a strategy using at most $k$ weighings (each weighing may depend on the outcomes of the previous ones). If we know which coin is the heavy one, the sequence of outcomes is determined: so each possible heavy coin gives a sequence of at most $k$ outcomes, each outcome being one of $3$ values. (If the strategy stops earlier, we may complete the sequence arbitrarily; this only helps the counting.) There are at most $3^k$ such sequences.

If two different coins gave the same sequence of outcomes, the strategy could not tell them apart. So a strategy that always finds the coin must give distinct sequences to distinct coins, hence $N\le3^k$. Therefore $N>3^k$ coins cannot be handled in $k$ weighings.

**Remark.** Part 2 is an *information-theoretic* argument: each weighing provides at most $\log_23$ bits of information, and finding one coin among $N$ needs $\log_2N$ bits. The classical harder variant, with a coin that may be heavier or lighter, is solved by the same principle: $12$ coins in $3$ weighings.

**Lesson.** Optimal algorithm problems come in two halves: an explicit strategy, and a counting argument on outcomes showing that nothing better is possible.
