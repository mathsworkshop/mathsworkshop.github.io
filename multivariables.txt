## Statement

Let $f(x,y) = x^2 y + e^{xy}$. Compute the partial derivatives $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$, then the gradient $\nabla f(1,0)$.

## Hint

Differentiate with respect to $x$ treating $y$ as a constant, and vice versa.

## Solution

**Partial derivative in $x$.**
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy + y e^{xy}.$$

**Partial derivative in $y$.**
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + x e^{xy}.$$

**Gradient at $(1,0)$.** With $x=1, y=0$:
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(1,0) = 0 + 0 \cdot e^0 = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(1,0) = 1 + 1 \cdot e^0 = 2.$$
Hence
$$\nabla f(1,0) = (0, 2).$$

**Remark.** The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase of $f$ at the given point.

## Statement

Let $f(x,y) = x^2 y$ and $\gamma(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$. Compute $\frac{d}{dt} f(\gamma(t))$ in two ways: by direct composition, then by the chain rule.

## Hint

The chain rule gives $\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t)) = \nabla f(\gamma(t)) \cdot \gamma'(t)$.

## Solution

**Method 1: direct composition.**
$$f(\gamma(t)) = \cos^2 t \cdot \sin t.$$
Differentiating:
$$\frac{d}{dt}(\cos^2 t \sin t) = 2\cos t (-\sin t)\sin t + \cos^2 t \cos t = -2\cos t \sin^2 t + \cos^3 t.$$

**Method 2: chain rule.**
$$\nabla f(x,y) = (2xy, x^2), \qquad \gamma'(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t).$$
Hence
$$\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t)) = 2\cos t \sin t \cdot (-\sin t) + \cos^2 t \cdot \cos t = -2\cos t \sin^2 t + \cos^3 t.$$

**Conclusion.** Both methods give the same result, illustrating the chain rule.

title: Is a function $C^1$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$?
category: multivariable/partial-derivatives

date: 2026-11-02

## Statement

Let
$$f(x,y) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x^3 y}{x^2+y^2} & \text{if } (x,y) \neq (0,0), \\[2mm] 0 & \text{if } (x,y) = (0,0). \end{cases}$$
Show that $f$ is of class $C^1$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

## Hint

Compute the partial derivatives on $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\}$, then study their continuity at $(0,0)$ by bounding with $\|(x,y)\|$.

## Solution

**Partial derivatives away from the origin.** For $(x,y) \neq (0,0)$,
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \frac{(3x^2 y)(x^2+y^2) - (x^3 y)(2x)}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{x^2 y(x^2+3y^2)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.$$
Similarly,
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{x^3(x^2+y^2) - x^3 y(2y)}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{x^3(x^2-y^2)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.$$

**Partial derivatives at $(0,0)$.** By definition,
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(0,0) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h} = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{0}{h} = 0.$$
Similarly, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(0,0) = 0$.

**Continuity at $(0,0)$.** Using $|x| \le \|(x,y)\|$ and $|y| \le \|(x,y)\|$,
$$\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,y)\right| \le \frac{\|(x,y)\|^3 \cdot \|(x,y)\| \cdot 4\|(x,y)\|^2}{\|(x,y)\|^4} = 4\|(x,y)\| \to 0.$$
So $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ is continuous at $(0,0)$, and similarly for $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$.

**Conclusion.** The partial derivatives exist and are continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2$, so $f$ is $C^1$.

## Statement

Let $D = \{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x \ge 0, y \ge 0, x+y \le 1\}$. Compute
$$\iint_D (x^2 + y^2) \, dx \, dy.$$

## Hint

Integrate first in $y$ for fixed $x$, with $0 \le y \le 1-x$, then in $x$ on $[0,1]$.

## Solution

**Setup.**
$$\iint_D (x^2+y^2) \, dx \, dy = \int_0^1 \left( \int_0^{1-x} (x^2+y^2) \, dy \right) dx.$$

**Integration in $y$.**
$$\int_0^{1-x} (x^2+y^2) \, dy = x^2(1-x) + \frac{(1-x)^3}{3}.$$

**Integration in $x$.**
$$\int_0^1 \left( x^2(1-x) + \frac{(1-x)^3}{3} \right) dx = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} - \frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{(1-x)^4}{12} \right]_0^1.$$
At $x=1$: $\frac13 - \frac14 - 0 = \frac{1}{12}$. At $x=0$: $0 - 0 - \frac{1}{12} = -\frac{1}{12}$. Hence
$$\iint_D (x^2+y^2) \, dx \, dy = \frac{1}{12} - \left(-\frac{1}{12}\right) = \frac16.$$

**Remark.** This is consistent with the area of the triangle ($1/2$) and the symmetry of the domain.

## Statement

Compute
$$\iint_D e^{-(x^2+y^2)} \, dx \, dy$$
where $D = \{(x,y) : x^2+y^2 \le 1\}$.

## Hint

Switch to polar coordinates: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$.

## Solution

**Change of variables.**
$$x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \quad dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta.$$
The domain becomes $0 \le r \le 1$, $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$.

**Computation.**
$$\iint_D e^{-(x^2+y^2)} \, dx \, dy = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 e^{-r^2} r \, dr \, d\theta.$$
The $r$-integral:
$$\int_0^1 r e^{-r^2} \, dr = \left[ -\frac12 e^{-r^2} \right]_0^1 = \frac12(1-e^{-1}).$$
Hence
$$\iint_D e^{-(x^2+y^2)} \, dx \, dy = 2\pi \cdot \frac12(1-e^{-1}) = \pi(1-e^{-1}).$$

**Remark.** This computation is the basis for proving $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt{\pi}$.

## Statement

Compute
$$I = \iiint_D x \, dx \, dy \, dz$$
where $D = \{(x,y,z) : x>0, y>0, z>0, x+y+z<1\}$.

## Hint

Integrate first in $z$ for fixed $(x,y)$, with $0 < z < 1-x-y$, then in $y$ and $x$.

## Solution

**Setup.**
$$I = \int_0^1 \int_0^{1-x} \int_0^{1-x-y} x \, dz \, dy \, dx.$$

**Integration in $z$.**
$$\int_0^{1-x-y} x \, dz = x(1-x-y).$$

**Integration in $y$.**
$$\int_0^{1-x} x(1-x-y) \, dy = x\left[ (1-x)y - \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_0^{1-x} = x \cdot \frac{(1-x)^2}{2}.$$

**Integration in $x$.**
$$I = \frac12 \int_0^1 x(1-x)^2 \, dx = \frac12 \int_0^1 (x - 2x^2 + x^3) \, dx = \frac12 \left( \frac12 - \frac23 + \frac14 \right) = \frac12 \cdot \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{24}.$$

**Remark.** The volume of the simplex is $1/6$, and the mean $x$-coordinate is $1/4$, consistent by symmetry.

## Statement

Compute the area of the parallelogram $P$ bounded by the lines $y = 3x$, $y = x$, $y = 3x - 1$ and $y = x + 4$, using a change of variables.

## Hint

Set $u = y - x$ and $v = 3x - y$. Determine the image domain and the Jacobian.

## Solution

**Change of variables.** Set
$$u = y - x, \quad v = 3x - y.$$
The lines become $u = 0$, $u = 4$, $v = 0$, $v = 1$. The image domain is the rectangle $T = [0,4] \times [0,1]$.

**Jacobian.** The determinant of the Jacobian matrix of $(x,y) \mapsto (u,v)$ is

$$\text{Area}(P) = \iint_P dx\,dy = \iint_T \frac12 \, du\,dv = \frac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 1 = 2.$$

## Statement

Let $C$ be the unit circle oriented counterclockwise. Compute
$$\int_C (x^2 - y)dx + (y^2 + x)dy$$
using the Green-Riemann formula.

## Hint

Apply Green-Riemann: $\int_C Pdx + Qdy = \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dx\,dy$.

## Solution

**Identification.** $P(x,y) = x^2 - y$, $Q(x,y) = y^2 + x$.

**Partial derivatives.**
$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = -1.$$

$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 1 - (-1) = 2.$$

**Green-Riemann.**
$$\int_C Pdx + Qdy = \iint_D 2 \, dx\,dy = 2 \cdot \text{Area}(D) = 2\pi.$$

**Remark.** One can also compute directly by parametrizing the circle, but Green-Riemann avoids trigonometric computation.

## Statement

Find the extrema of $f(x,y) = 3x - y$ under the constraint $x^2 + y^2 = 5$.

## Hint

Apply Lagrange: $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ with $g(x,y) = x^2+y^2-5$.

## Solution

**Lagrange system.**
$$\begin{cases} 3 = 2\lambda x \\ -1 = 2\lambda y \\ x^2 + y^2 = 5 \end{cases}$$

**Resolution.** From the first two equations, $x = \frac{3}{2\lambda}$ and $y = -\frac{1}{2\lambda}$, so $y = -\frac{x}{3}$. Substituting into the constraint:
$$x^2 + \frac{x^2}{9} = 5 \implies \frac{10x^2}{9} = 5 \implies x^2 = \frac{9}{2} \implies x = \pm \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}.$$
The critical points are $\left(\frac{3}{\sqrt2}, -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)$ and $\left(-\frac{3}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)$.

**Evaluation.**
$$f\left(\frac{3}{\sqrt2}, -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right) = \frac{9}{\sqrt2} + \frac{1}{\sqrt2} = \frac{10}{\sqrt2} = 5\sqrt2.$$
$$f\left(-\frac{3}{\sqrt2}, \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right) = -\frac{9}{\sqrt2} - \frac{1}{\sqrt2} = -5\sqrt2.$$

**Conclusion.** Global maximum $5\sqrt2$ at the first point, global minimum $-5\sqrt2$ at the second. The constraint is a compact circle, so the extrema exist and are attained at these points.

## Statement

Let $f(x,y) = e^{axy}$ with $a>0$, under the constraint $g(x,y) = x^3 + y^3 + x + y - 4 = 0$. Show that $f$ has a global maximum at $(1,1)$.

## Hint

Apply Lagrange to identify the only possible critical point, then use a compactness argument and the behaviour at infinity.

## Solution

**Lagrange system.**
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = ay e^{axy}, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 3x^2+1,$$
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = ax e^{axy}, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 3y^2+1.$$
The condition $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ gives
$$\frac{ay e^{axy}}{3x^2+1} = \frac{ax e^{axy}}{3y^2+1}.$$
Simplifying by $a e^{axy} > 0$:
$$\frac{y}{3x^2+1} = \frac{x}{3y^2+1} \implies y(3y^2+1) = x(3x^2+1).$$
The function $t \mapsto 3t^3+t$ is strictly increasing, so $x = y$.

**Critical point.** Substituting $x=y$ into the constraint:
$$2x^3 + 2x - 4 = 0 \implies x^3 + x - 2 = 0.$$
The only real root is $x=1$ (since $x^3+x-2 = (x-1)(x^2+x+2)$ and the discriminant of $x^2+x+2$ is negative). So the only possible critical point is $(1,1)$.

**Compactness argument.** On the curve $G$, if $\|(x,y)\| \to +\infty$, then $x$ and $y$ have opposite signs (since $x^3+y^3$ must stay bounded), so $axy \to -\infty$ and $f(x,y) \to 0$. The function $f$ is continuous on $G$ (which is closed), and $f(1,1) = e^a > 0$. Hence $f$ attains its maximum on a compact set, and this maximum cannot be at infinity (where $f \to 0$). The only candidate is $(1,1)$, so it is the global maximum.

## Statement

Let $(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)$ be points. Minimize
$$F(m,p) = \sum_{k=1}^n (y_k - m x_k - p)^2$$
over $\mathbb{R}^2$.

## Hint

Compute the partial derivatives $\frac{\partial F}{\partial m}$ and $\frac{\partial F}{\partial p}$, set them to zero, and solve the system.

## Solution

**Partial derivatives.**
$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial m} = -2 \sum_{k=1}^n x_k(y_k - m x_k - p) = 0,$$
$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial p} = -2 \sum_{k=1}^n (y_k - m x_k - p) = 0.$$

**Normal system.** Dividing by $-2$:
$$\sum x_k y_k = m \sum x_k^2 + p \sum x_k, \qquad \sum y_k = m \sum x_k + n p.$$
From the second equation, $p = \bar y - m \bar x$. Substituting into the first:
$$\sum x_k y_k = m \sum x_k^2 + (\bar y - m \bar x) n \bar x = m \left( \sum x_k^2 - n \bar x^2 \right) + n \bar x \bar y.$$
Hence
$$m = \frac{\sum x_k y_k - n \bar x \bar y}{\sum x_k^2 - n \bar x^2} = \frac{\sum (x_k - \bar x)(y_k - \bar y)}{\sum (x_k - \bar x)^2}.$$
This is the slope of the least-squares line.

**Global minimum.** $F$ is a convex quadratic function (sum of squares of affine forms), so the critical point is a global minimum.

## Statement

Using Lagrange multipliers, prove that for all $x_1,\ldots,x_n > 0$,
$$\sqrt[n]{x_1 \cdots x_n} \le \frac{x_1 + \cdots + x_n}{n}.$$

## Hint

Maximize the product $P = x_1 \cdots x_n$ under the constraint $x_1 + \cdots + x_n = S$ fixed. The maximum is attained at $x_1 = \cdots = x_n = S/n$.

## Solution

**Lagrange.** Maximize $\ln P = \sum \ln x_i$ under the constraint $\sum x_i = S$. The system is
$$\frac{1}{x_i} = \lambda \quad \text{for all } i.$$
So all $x_i$ are equal to $1/\lambda$, and the constraint gives $x_i = S/n$ for all $i$.

**Maximum.** At this point, $P = (S/n)^n$. Since the domain $x_i > 0$, $\sum x_i = S$ is bounded and the product tends to $0$ on the boundary (if some $x_i \to 0$), the maximum is indeed attained at this point.

**Conclusion.** For all $x_i > 0$ with sum $S$,
$$x_1 \cdots x_n \le \left(\frac{S}{n}\right)^n = \left(\frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}\right)^n.$$
Taking the $n$-th root gives the AM-GM inequality.

$$\nabla f = 2Ax, \qquad \nabla g = 2x.$$
The condition $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ gives
$$Ax = \lambda x.$$
So $x$ is an eigenvector of $A$ associated with the eigenvalue $\lambda$.

**Value at a critical point.** If $Ax = \lambda x$, then
$$f(x) = x^T A x = x^T (\lambda x) = \lambda x^T x = \lambda.$$
So the maximum of $f$ on the sphere is the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$.

**Rayleigh quotient.** For $x \neq 0$, $R(x) = f(x/\|x\|)$, so the maximum of $R$ over $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$ is also $\lambda_{\max}$.

**Conclusion.** The maximum is attained at a unit eigenvector associated with $\lambda_{\max}$.

## Statement

Prove the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:
$$|x \cdot y| \le \|x\| \, \|y\|$$
for all $x,y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, using an optimisation method.

## Hint

For fixed nonzero $y$, maximize $f(x) = x \cdot y$ under the constraint $\|x\| = 1$. The maximum is $\|y\|$.

## Solution

**Lagrange.** Maximize $f(x) = \sum x_i y_i$ under $g(x) = \sum x_i^2 = 1$. The gradients are $\nabla f = y$ and $\nabla g = 2x$. The condition $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ gives
$$y = 2\lambda x \implies x = \frac{y}{2\lambda}.$$
Imposing $\|x\| = 1$:
$$\left\| \frac{y}{2\lambda} \right\| = 1 \implies |2\lambda| = \|y\| \implies x = \pm \frac{y}{\|y\|}.$$

**Value.** At $x = y/\|y\|$, $f(x) = \frac{y \cdot y}{\|y\|} = \|y\|$. At $x = -y/\|y\|$, $f(x) = -\|y\|$. So the maximum is $\|y\|$, and the minimum is $-\|y\|$.

**Conclusion.** For all $x$ with $\|x\|=1$, we have $|x \cdot y| \le \|y\|$. For arbitrary nonzero $x$, setting $u = x/\|x\|$ gives
$$|x \cdot y| = \|x\| \, |u \cdot y| \le \|x\| \, \|y\|.$$
