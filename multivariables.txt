## Statement

Summarize the key methods for constrained optimization.

## Hint

Review critical points, Hessian, Lagrange, boundary.

## Solution

**Unconstrained critical points.**
- Solve $\nabla f=0$.
- Classify via the Hessian: positive definite (min), negative definite (max), indefinite (saddle).

**Lagrange multipliers.** For $f$ under $g=0$: $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$, plus the constraint.

**Boundary.** Parameterize or restrict $f$ to the boundary surface; combine with interior results.

**Compact sets.** If the set is compact, global extrema exist; check interior, boundary, and corners.

**KKT for inequalities.** For $g\le0$: Lagrange with nonnegativity conditions.

**Examples.** Circles, lines, ellipsoids, quadratics, multi-constraint problems.

**Conclusion.** Constrained optimization reduces to finding critical points of the Lagrangian and evaluating on boundaries.

## Statement

State the fundamental theorem for line integrals: if $\vec F=\nabla\phi$, then $\int_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\phi(B)-\phi(A)$.

## Hint

## Solution

**Proof.** Let $\gamma(t)$, $t\in[a,b]$, parametrize $C$ from $A$ to $B$. Then

**Conclusion.** For conservative fields, the integral only depends on endpoints.

## Statement

$$f_{xy}(0,0)=\lim_{y\to0}\frac{f_x(0,y)-f_x(0,0)}{y},\qquad f_{yx}(0,0)=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{f_y(x,0)-f_y(0,0)}{x}.$$

Schwarz's hypothesis ($C^2$ regularity near the point) fails, so the conclusion need not hold.

**Conclusion.** Schwarz's theorem is compatible with the existence of such counterexamples: only $C^2$ functions are guaranteed to have symmetric mixed partials.

$$\frac{d}{dt}(\cos^2 t \sin t) = 2\cos t (-\sin t)\sin t + \cos^2 t \cos t = -2\cos t \sin^2 t + \cos^3 t.$$

$$\nabla f(x,y) = (2xy, x^2), \qquad \gamma'(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t).$$
Hence
$$\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t)) = 2\cos t \sin t \cdot (-\sin t) + \cos^2 t \cdot \cos t = -2\cos t \sin^2 t + \cos^3 t.$$

**Conclusion.** Both methods give the same result, illustrating the chain rule.

$$y = 2\lambda x \implies x = \frac{y}{2\lambda}.$$
Imposing $\|x\| = 1$:
$$\left\| \frac{y}{2\lambda} \right\| = 1 \implies |2\lambda| = \|y\| \implies x = \pm \frac{y}{\|y\|}.$$

**Value.** At $x = y/\|y\|$, $f(x) = \frac{y \cdot y}{\|y\|} = \|y\|$. At $x = -y/\|y\|$, $f(x) = -\|y\|$. So the maximum is $\|y\|$, and the minimum is $-\|y\|$.

**Conclusion.** For all $x$ with $\|x\|=1$, we have $|x \cdot y| \le \|y\|$. For arbitrary nonzero $x$, setting $u = x/\|x\|$ gives
$$|x \cdot y| = \|x\| \, |u \cdot y| \le \|x\| \, \|y\|.$$
