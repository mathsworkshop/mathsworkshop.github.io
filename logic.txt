## Statement

Let $A$ and $B$ be subsets of a set $E$, and let $\bar A$ and $\bar B$ be their complements in $E$. Prove:
$$A\subseteq B\implies\bar B\subseteq\bar A.$$
Give two proofs: (a) by direct reasoning; (b) by contradiction.

## Hint

For the direct proof, take $x\in\bar B$ and use $A\subseteq B$. For contradiction, suppose $\bar B\not\subseteq\bar A$.

## Solution

**(a) Direct proof.** Let $x\in\bar B$, i.e. $x\notin B$. Since $A\subseteq B$, every element of $A$ belongs to $B$; as $x\notin B$, we get $x\notin A$, i.e. $x\in\bar A$. Hence $\bar B\subseteq\bar A$.

**(b) Proof by contradiction.** Suppose $\bar B\not\subseteq\bar A$. Then there exists $x\in\bar B$ with $x\notin\bar A$, i.e. $x\notin B$ and $x\in A$. But $A\subseteq B$ gives $x\in B$, contradiction.

**Conclusion.** $A\subseteq B\implies\bar B\subseteq\bar A$.

## Statement

Let $A,B\subseteq E$ and $\bar A$ the complement of $A$ in $E$. Prove the equivalence
$$A\subseteq B\iff\bar A\cup B=E.$$

## Hint

Prove both implications separately, by element chasing.

## Solution

**$\Rightarrow$.** Suppose $A\subseteq B$ and let $x\in E$. If $x\in B$, then $x\in\bar A\cup B$. If $x\notin B$, then $x\notin A$ (since $A\subseteq B$), so $x\in\bar A\subseteq\bar A\cup B$. Hence every element of $E$ is in $\bar A\cup B$.

**$\Leftarrow$.** Suppose $\bar A\cup B=E$. If $A\not\subseteq B$, there is $x\in A$ with $x\notin B$. Then $x\notin\bar A$ and $x\notin B$, so $x\notin\bar A\cup B$, contradicting $\bar A\cup B=E$. Hence $A\subseteq B$.

**Conclusion.** The equivalence holds.

## Statement

## Statement

Let $p,q$ be propositions. Suppose both implications are true:
$$(1)\ p\land q\implies\bar q,\qquad (2)\ \bar p\land\bar q\implies q.$$
Show that $p$ is false, first directly, then using truth tables.

## Hint

Examine the cases $p=1$ and $p=0$ separately.

## Solution

**Direct reasoning.** If $p$ is false, the antecedents $p\land q$ and $\bar p\land\bar q$ of (1) can be true only if $q=1$, but then (1) requires $\bar q=1$, false. More systematically:

- If $p=1$ and $q=1$: (1) is $1\Rightarrow0$, false.
- If $p=1$ and $q=0$: (2) is $1\Rightarrow0$, false.
- If $p=0$: both antecedents are always false, so both implications are vacuously true.

Hence both implications hold simultaneously iff $p=0$.

**Truth table.** Writing $\text{impl}(x,y)=\bar x\lor y$, one checks that (1) and (2) are both true only in the rows where $p=0$.

**Conclusion.** $p$ must be false.

## Statement

Three numbers $a,b,c$ — one positive, one negative, one zero — satisfy:
$$a=0\implies b>0,\qquad a>0\implies b<0,\qquad b\ne0\implies c>0.$$
Determine the sign of each.

## Hint

Test each possible sign of $a$; use the implications and the "one of each sign" constraint.

## Solution

**Test $a=0$.** Then $b>0$, and $b\ne0$ gives $c>0$. We would have two positive numbers $b,c$, contradicting the hypothesis. So $a\ne0$.

**Test $a>0$.** Then $b<0$, and $b\ne0$ gives $c>0$. Again two positives ($a,c$), contradiction. So $a\not>0$; hence $a<0$.

**Test $b>0$.** Then $b\ne0$ gives $c>0$, another positive besides $b$, contradiction. So $b\le0$; since $a<0$ is the negative number and one number must be zero, $b=0$.

**Remaining.** $c>0$ is the positive number.

**Conclusion.** $a<0$, $b=0$, $c>0$.

## Statement

Define an equivalence relation on a set $A$ and give an example.

## Solution

**Definition.** $\sim$ is an equivalence relation on $A$ if:
- *Reflexive*: $\forall x\in A$, $x\sim x$;
- *Symmetric*: $\forall x,y\in A$, $x\sim y\Rightarrow y\sim x$;
- *Transitive*: $\forall x,y,z\in A$, $(x\sim y\land y\sim z)\Rightarrow x\sim z$.

**Nonempty.** $x\in[x]$ (reflexive).

**Cover.** Every $x$ belongs to its own class.

**Disjoint.** If $[x]\cap[y]\neq\emptyset$, take $z$ in both. Then $z\sim x$ and $z\sim y$, so by symmetry and transitivity $x\sim y$. Then for any $w\in[x]$, $w\sim x\sim y$, so $w\in[y]$: $[x]\subseteq[y]$. Symmetrically, $[y]\subseteq[x]$. So $[x]=[y]$.

**Conclusion.** The classes form a partition of $A$; the set of classes is $A/\sim$ (the quotient).

## Hint

Apply the negation rules.

## Solution

## Statement

Summarize the key facts about quantifiers.

## Hint

Review definitions, negation rules, and order.

## Solution

**Definitions.**
- $\forall x,\ P(x)$: $P$ holds for all $x$.
- $\exists x,\ P(x)$: some $x$ satisfies $P$.
- $\exists! x,\ P(x)$: exactly one $x$ satisfies $P$.

**Negation.**
- $\neg\forall x,\ P(x)\equiv\exists x,\ \neg P(x)$.
- $\neg\exists x,\ P(x)\equiv\forall x,\ \neg P(x)$.
- $\neg\exists!x,\ P(x)$: no $x$ or at least two $x$ satisfy $P$.

**Order.** $\forall\exists$ does not imply $\exists\forall$ in general.

**Vacuous truth.** $\forall x\in\emptyset,\ P(x)$ is true.

**Conclusion.** Quantifiers are the backbone of mathematical statements; negation and order are critical for correct reasoning.

**Inductive step.** Suppose the tree has root with left subtree $L$ and right subtree $R$. Let $n_L,n_R$ be internal node counts and $\ell_L,\ell_R$ leaf counts. By induction, $\ell_L=n_L+1$, $\ell_R=n_R+1$. Total internal nodes: $n=n_L+n_R+1$ (including root). Total leaves: $\ell=\ell_L+\ell_R=n_L+1+n_R+1=n+1$.

**Conclusion.** A full binary tree with $n$ internal nodes has $n+1$ leaves.

## Statement

State the principle of real induction and give an application.

## Hint

If a property holds at $a$, propagates locally, and extends up to limits, then it holds on $[a,b]$.

## Solution

**Principle (real induction).** Let $S\subseteq[a,b]$ satisfy:
1. $a\in S$;
2. If $x\in S$ and $x<b$, then $[x,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq S$ for some $\varepsilon>0$;
3. If $[a,x)\subseteq S$ with $x\le b$, then $x\in S$.
Then $S=[a,b]$.

**Application.** Proving that a continuous function has the intermediate value property.

**Conclusion.** Real induction is a powerful tool for proofs on intervals.

## Statement

Summarize the key proof techniques.

## Hint

Review the main methods.

## Solution

**Direct proof.** Assume hypothesis, derive conclusion step by step.

**Contraposition.** Prove $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$ instead of $P\Rightarrow Q$.

**Contradiction.** Assume the negation of the claim, derive a contradiction.

**Cases.** Split into exhaustive cases and prove each.

**Quantifiers.** $\forall$ and $\exists$; order matters.

**Conclusion.** These techniques cover most standard mathematical proofs.

title: Summary of induction
category: logic/proof-techniques/induction
level: lycee
difficulty: 1
tags: summary, induction, techniques
date: 2026-11-03
---

## Statement

Summarize the forms of induction.

## Hint

Review weak, strong, structural.

## Solution

**Weak induction.** Base case $P(n_0)$; step $P(n)\Rightarrow P(n+1)$. Concludes $P(n)$ for all $n\ge n_0$.

**Strong induction.** Base case $P(n_0)$; step $[\forall k\in[n_0,n],P(k)]\Rightarrow P(n+1)$. Allows using more hypotheses.

**Structural induction.** For recursively defined objects (trees, formulas, etc.): prove for base objects; step from subobjects to compound objects.

**Well-ordering.** Equivalent to induction: every nonempty subset of $\mathbb{N}$ has a least element.

**Conclusion.** Choose the form best suited to the problem's recursive structure.
