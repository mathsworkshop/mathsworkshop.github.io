## Statement

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that for all real $x,y$,
$$f(x)f(y)-f(xy)=x+y.$$

## Hint

Try $y=0$: the term $f(xy)$ becomes a constant.

## Solution

**Substitution $y=0$.** $f(x)f(0)-f(0)=x$, i.e.
$$f(0)\bigl(f(x)-1\bigr)=x\quad\text{for all }x.$$
If $f(0)=0$ the left side is $0$ for all $x$, which is absurd (take $x=1$). So $f(0)\ne0$ and
$$f(x)=1+\frac x{f(0)}.$$

**Determining the constant.** Setting $x=0$ in this formula gives $f(0)=1$. Hence $f(x)=x+1$.

**Verification.** $(x+1)(y+1)-(xy+1)=x+y$ ✓.

**Answer.** $f(x)=x+1$ is the only solution.

**Lesson.** Always: (1) try $x=0$, $y=0$, $x=y$, $y=1$; (2) when an equation is solved by a formula, plug the formula back to check it is a solution.

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be a **monotone** function such that $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$ for all real $x,y$. Prove that there is a constant $c$ with $f(x)=cx$ for all $x$.

## Hint

First show $f(q)=qf(1)$ for rational $q$ by pure algebra, then squeeze an arbitrary real number between two rationals, using the monotonicity.

## Solution

We assume $f$ non-decreasing (otherwise replace $f$ by $-f$).

**Step 1: algebra.** $x=y=0$ gives $f(0)=0$. Then $0=f(x+(-x))=f(x)+f(-x)$, so $f$ is odd. By induction $f(nx)=nf(x)$ for $n\in\mathbb{N}$. For $q=\frac mn$ with $m\in\mathbb{Z}$, $n\ge1$: $nf\left(\frac mn\right)=f(m)=mf(1)$, hence $f(q)=qc$ where $c=f(1)$. Note $c=f(1)\ge f(0)=0$.

**Step 2: squeeze.** Let $x$ be real and $q_1<x<q_2$ rationals. Since $f$ is non-decreasing,
$$cq_1=f(q_1)\le f(x)\le f(q_2)=cq_2.$$
Letting $q_1,q_2\to x$ (rationals are dense), $f(x)=cx$.

**Verification.** $f(x)=cx$ is additive.

**Remark.** Without any regularity assumption, there exist additive functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ that are not linear (their construction needs the axiom of choice, via a Hamel basis). Their graphs are dense in the plane. That is why olympiad problems usually add: continuity, monotonicity, boundedness on an interval, or work on $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Lesson.** Cauchy: algebra gives $\mathbb{Q}$; a regularity condition extends it to $\mathbb{R}$.

## Statement

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that for all real $x,y$:
$$f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(xy)=f(x)f(y).$$

## Hint

Compute $f(x^2)$ in two ways to prove that $f$ takes nonnegative values on $[0,\infty)$; deduce monotonicity and use Cauchy's equation.

## Solution

**Nonnegativity.** For $x\ge0$ write $x=t^2$. Then $f(x)=f(t\cdot t)=f(t)^2\ge0$.

**Monotonicity.** If $x\le y$ then $f(y)-f(x)=f(y-x)\ge0$ (additivity and nonnegativity on $[0,\infty)$). So $f$ is non-decreasing.

**Cauchy.** An additive monotone function is linear: $f(x)=cx$ (previous problem).

**The constant.** Multiplicativity: $f(1\cdot1)=f(1)^2$, so $c=c^2$, hence $c\in\{0,1\}$.

**Answer.** $f(x)=0$ for all $x$, or $f(x)=x$ for all $x$. Both satisfy both equations.

**Remark.** The same argument shows there is no "wild" ring homomorphism $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, in contrast with $\mathbb{C}$, which has wild automorphisms.

**Lesson.** The relation $f(t^2)=f(t)^2$ is the standard way to get *positivity*, and positivity gives monotonicity.

## Statement

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Q}$ such that $f(x+f(y))=f(x)+y$ for all rational $x,y$.

## Hint

Put $x=0$ to see that $f$ is injective, then put $y=0$.

## Solution

Let $c=f(0)$.

**Injectivity.** $x=0$: $f(f(y))=c+y$. If $f(y_1)=f(y_2)$ then $f(f(y_1))=f(f(y_2))$, so $y_1=y_2$.

**$c=0$.** $y=0$: $f(x+c)=f(x)$. By injectivity $x+c=x$, so $c=0$. Hence $f(f(y))=y$ ($f$ is an involution).

**Additivity.** Replace $y$ by $f(y)$ in the equation: $f(x+f(f(y)))=f(x)+f(y)$, i.e.
$$f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y).$$

**Solving on $\mathbb{Q}$.** As in the Cauchy problem, $f(q)=qa$ for every rational $q$, where $a=f(1)\in\mathbb{Q}$. Now $f(f(1))=1$ gives $a\cdot a=1$, so $a=\pm1$.

**Answer.** $f(x)=x$ and $f(x)=-x$. Check: for $f(x)=-x$, $f(x+f(y))=-(x-y)=y-x=f(x)+y$ ✓.

**Remark.** Over $\mathbb{R}$ without regularity, the same argument shows that $f$ is an *additive involution*, and there are exotic ones, so the problem on $\mathbb{R}$ needs an extra hypothesis (continuity, for example).

**Lesson.** Injectivity from $f(f(y))=\ldots$ is the first thing to look for; then show an involution and replace $y$ by $f(y)$ to simplify.

## Statement

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that for all real $x,y$,
$$f\left(x^2+f(y)\right)=y+f(x)^2.$$

## Hint

Put $x=0$ to get injectivity and surjectivity. Then use a $t$ with $f(t)=0$. Show $f$ is odd, $f(0)=0$, and that $f$ is additive and nonnegative on $[0,\infty)$.

## Solution

Let $a=f(0)$.

**Step 1.** $x=0$: $f(f(y))=y+a^2$. Thus $f$ is injective (as in the previous problem) and surjective (the right side takes every real value).

**Step 2.** By surjectivity pick $t$ with $f(t)=0$. Putting $y=t$ gives
$$f(x^2)=t+f(x)^2\qquad(\ast)$$
for all $x$.

**Step 3: oddness.** Replacing $x$ by $-x$ in $(\ast)$ gives $f(x)^2=f(-x)^2$, so $f(-x)=\pm f(x)$. If $x\neq0$, the case $f(-x)=f(x)$ contradicts injectivity. Hence $f(-x)=-f(x)$ for all $x\ne0$. If $t\ne0$, then $f(-t)=-f(t)=0=f(t)$, contradicting injectivity again. Therefore $t=0$, i.e. $f(0)=0$ and $f$ is odd.

**Step 4.** Now $a=0$, so $f(f(y))=y$ and $(\ast)$ becomes $f(x^2)=f(x)^2\ge0$: $f\ge0$ on $[0,\infty)$. The original equation reads $f(x^2+f(y))=y+f(x^2)$. For every $u\ge0$ (taking $x=\sqrt u$): $f(u+f(y))=y+f(u)$; replacing $y$ by $f(y)$,
$$f(u+y)=f(u)+f(y)\qquad(u\ge0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}).$$
For $u<0$, use oddness: $f(u+y)=-f((-u)+(-y))=-f(-u)-f(-y)=f(u)+f(y)$. So $f$ is additive on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 5.** An additive function that is $\ge0$ on $[0,\infty)$ is non-decreasing, hence $f(x)=cx$ (Cauchy with monotonicity). From $f(f(x))=x$: $c^2=1$, and $f(1)=c\ge0$ gives $c=1$.

**Answer.** $f(x)=x$ (check: $f(x^2+y)=x^2+y$ ✓).

**Lesson.** A strong sequence of moves: injective $\to$ locate the zero $\to$ oddness $\to$ $f(f(y))=y$ $\to$ additivity $\to$ monotonicity from nonnegativity of squares.

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be twice differentiable, not identically zero, with
$$f(x+y)+f(x-y)=2f(x)f(y)\quad\text{for all }x,y.$$
Find $f$.

## Hint

Show $f(0)=1$ and that $f$ is even. Differentiate twice with respect to $y$ and set $y=0$: you get a differential equation $f''=kf$.

## Solution

**$f(0)=1$.** $x=y=0$: $2f(0)=2f(0)^2$, so $f(0)\in\{0,1\}$. If $f(0)=0$, then $y=0$ gives $2f(x)=0$ for all $x$, excluded. So $f(0)=1$.

**Evenness.** $x=0$: $f(y)+f(-y)=2f(y)$, i.e. $f(-y)=f(y)$. Hence $f'(0)=0$.

**Differential equation.** Fix $x$ and differentiate twice with respect to $y$:
$$f''(x+y)+f''(x-y)=2f(x)f''(y).$$
Put $y=0$: $2f''(x)=2f(x)f''(0)$, i.e.
$$f''(x)=kf(x),\qquad k=f''(0),$$
with $f(0)=1$, $f'(0)=0$. By uniqueness of solutions of linear ODEs:
- $k=0$: $f(x)=1$;
- $k=\omega^2>0$: $f(x)=\cosh(\omega x)$;
- $k=-\omega^2<0$: $f(x)=\cos(\omega x)$.

**Verification.** $\cos(x+y)+\cos(x-y)=2\cos x\cos y$ and $\cosh(x+y)+\cosh(x-y)=2\cosh x\cosh y$; constant $1$ gives $2=2$ ✓.

**Remark.** This is d'Alembert's equation; the name "cosine functional equation" comes from the trigonometric identity. Continuity alone (without differentiability) is already enough, but needs a more delicate argument (integrate against a bump function to get smoothness).

**Lesson.** When a functional equation has a smoothness hypothesis, differentiating in one variable and plugging a special value often produces an ODE.

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{N}^*\to\mathbb{N}^*$ satisfy $f(n+1)>f(f(n))$ for every positive integer $n$. Prove that $f(n)=n$ for all $n$.

## Hint

Prove by induction on $n$ the statement $P(n)$: "$f(k)\ge n$ for all $k\ge n$". Deduce that $f(n)\ge n$, then that $f$ is strictly increasing, and finally compare.

## Solution

**Claim.** $P(n)$: for every $k\ge n$, $f(k)\ge n$.

*Base.* $P(1)$: all values of $f$ are at least $1$.

*Step.* Assume $P(n)$. Let $k\ge n+1$. Then $k-1\ge n$, so $f(k-1)\ge n$ by $P(n)$. Applying $P(n)$ again to the argument $f(k-1)\ge n$: $f(f(k-1))\ge n$. Therefore
$$f(k)>f(f(k-1))\ge n,$$
so $f(k)\ge n+1$. This proves $P(n+1)$.

**Consequence 1.** Taking $k=n$ in $P(n)$: $f(n)\ge n$ for all $n$.

**Consequence 2: $f$ is strictly increasing.** Apply Consequence 1 to $m=f(n)$: $f(f(n))\ge f(n)$. Hence $f(n+1)>f(f(n))\ge f(n)$.

**Conclusion.** Suppose $f(n)\ge n+1$ for some $n$. Since $f$ is increasing, $f(f(n))\ge f(n+1)$, contradicting $f(n+1)>f(f(n))$. So $f(n)\le n$. Together with $f(n)\ge n$: $f(n)=n$.

**Verification.** $f(n)=n$: $n+1>n$ ✓.

**Lesson.** For $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ with an inequality, prove a *lower bound by induction* (here "$f(k)\ge n$ for $k\ge n$"), then show monotonicity, which lets you "cancel" $f$ from both sides of an inequality.

## Statement

Let $P$ be a polynomial with $n$ distinct real roots. Show that $P^{(n-1)}$ has at least one real root.

## Hint

Apply Rolle's theorem repeatedly. If a differentiable function has $k$ distinct real roots, its derivative has at least $k-1$ distinct real roots.

## Solution

**Induction on the number of derivatives.** Let $r_0=n$ be the number of distinct real roots of $P^{(0)}=P$. We prove that for every $k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}$, $P^{(k)}$ has at least $n-k$ distinct real roots.

**Base case.** $k=0$: $P$ has $n$ distinct real roots by hypothesis, and $n-0=n$.

**Inductive step.** Suppose $P^{(k)}$ has at least $n-k$ distinct real roots, call them $x_1<x_2<\cdots<x_{n-k}$. Since $P^{(k)}$ is differentiable on each interval $]x_i,x_{i+1}[$ and continuous on $[x_i,x_{i+1}]$, and $P^{(k)}(x_i)=P^{(k)}(x_{i+1})=0$, Rolle's theorem gives a point $c_i\in]x_i,x_{i+1}[$ with
$$\bigl(P^{(k)}\bigr)'(c_i)=P^{(k+1)}(c_i)=0.$$
There are $n-k-1$ such intervals, so $P^{(k+1)}$ has at least $n-k-1$ distinct real roots. This completes the induction.

**Conclusion.** Taking $k=n-1$, $P^{(n-1)}$ has at least $n-(n-1)=1$ real root.

Apply Rolle to $h(x)=(f(b)-f(a))g(x)-(g(b)-g(a))f(x)$.

## Solution

**Setup.** For $h(x)=(f(b)-f(a))g(x)-(g(b)-g(a))f(x)$, we have 
$$h(a)=(f(b)-f(a))g(a)-(g(b)-g(a))f(a)$$ and $$h(b)=(f(b)-f(a))g(b)-(g(b)-g(a))f(b).$$ Hence $$h(b)-h(a)=(f(b)-f(a))(g(b)-g(a))-(g(b)-g(a))(f(b)-f(a))=0$$.

**Rolle.** $h$ satisfies $h(a)=h(b)$; there exists $c\in]a,b[$ with $h'(c)=0$:
$$0=(f(b)-f(a))g'(c)-(g(b)-g(a))f'(c).$$
Rearranging gives the result (note $g(b)-g(a)\ne0$ since $g'\ne0$).

## Statement

Summarize the mean value theorem and its consequences.

## Hint

Review the statements and main applications.

## Solution

**Rolle's theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $]a,b[$, and $f(a)=f(b)$, then $f'$ vanishes somewhere in $]a,b[$.

**Mean value theorem (Lagrange).** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $]a,b[$, then there exists $c\in]a,b[$ with $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$.

**Cauchy's extended MVT.** Ratio form $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}=\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$ when $g'\ne0$.

**Consequences.**
- $f'=0\Rightarrow f$ constant.
- $f'\ge0\Rightarrow f$ nondecreasing; $f'>0\Rightarrow$ strictly increasing.
- Bounded derivative $\Rightarrow$ Lipschitz.
- Convexity $\iff f'$ nondecreasing.

**Inequalities.** $\sin x<x$, $\ln x<x-1$, $e^x>1+x$, $\arctan t>t/(1+t^2)$.

**Uniqueness of roots.** Rolle + $f'>0$ gives at most one root.

**Darboux theorem.** $f'$ has the intermediate value property.

**Conclusion.** The MVT is one of the most powerful tools in single-variable calculus, connecting derivatives to function behavior.

## Statement

## Solution

**Remark.** Without continuity there are many weird (non-measurable) solutions, built with the axiom of choice. So here a single hypothesis, continuity, turns a "vast" family of solutions into just the lines through the origin.

Hence $f(t)\ge1$ exactly for $t\in[t_1,t_2]$.

**Locating $t_1$.** $f(0.25)=e^{-0.125}\approx0.88<1$ and $f(0.3)=1.2e^{-0.15}\approx1.03>1$, so $t_1\in(0.25,0.3)$, i.e. $t_1\approx0.3$.

**Locating $t_2$.** $f(6)=24e^{-3}\approx1.19>1$ and $f(7)=28e^{-3.5}\approx0.85<1$. Refining, $t_2\approx6.5$.

**Answer.** The drug is effective from about $0.3$ h (18 minutes) to about $6.5$ h after intake, a window of about $6.2$ hours.

## Statement

Let $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous and suppose $f(x)$ is rational for every $x$. Prove that $f$ is constant.

## Hint

Argue by contradiction. If $f(a)\ne f(b)$, what does the intermediate value theorem say about the values between them?

## Solution

**Idea.** A continuous function cannot skip any value between two of its values. But the rational numbers have gaps: between two rationals there are irrational numbers.

**Proof.** Suppose $f(a)\neq f(b)$ for some $a,b\in[0,1]$, say $f(a)<f(b)$. The interval $\bigl(f(a),f(b)\bigr)$ contains an irrational number $y$ (for example $f(a)+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\,\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)$, which lies strictly between since $0<\frac{\sqrt2}2<1$, and is irrational because $f(b)-f(a)$ is a non-zero rational).

By the intermediate value theorem on the interval between $a$ and $b$, there is $c$ with $f(c)=y$. But $f(c)$ is rational and $y$ is not. Contradiction.

**Conclusion.** $f(a)=f(b)$ for all $a,b$, so $f$ is constant.

**Remark.** The same argument shows that a continuous function with values in $\mathbb{Z}$ is constant, and more generally that a continuous function on an interval cannot take only values in a countable set unless it is constant.

## Statement

A function $f:I\to\mathbb{R}$ is **$K$-Lipschitz** if $|f(x)-f(y)|\le K|x-y|$ for all $x,y\in I$. Prove that a Lipschitz function is uniformly continuous. Give two examples.

## Hint

Given $\varepsilon$, find a $\delta$ that depends only on $\varepsilon$ and $K$, not on the point.

## Solution

**Definition reminder.** $f$ is uniformly continuous if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that for **all** $x,y$ with $|x-y|<\delta$, $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. The difference with ordinary continuity is that $\delta$ must work for all points at once.

**Proof.** Assume $K>0$ (the case $K=0$ is trivial since $f$ is constant). Let $\varepsilon>0$ and set $\delta=\frac\varepsilon K$. If $|x-y|<\delta$, then
$$|f(x)-f(y)|\le K|x-y|<K\delta=\varepsilon.$$

**Examples.**
- $x\mapsto\sin x$ is $1$-Lipschitz on $\mathbb{R}$, by the mean value theorem since $|\cos|\le1$.
- $x\mapsto|x|$ is $1$-Lipschitz by the reverse triangle inequality, although it is not differentiable at $0$.

**Remark.** On an interval, a differentiable function with bounded derivative $|f'|\le K$ is $K$-Lipschitz (mean value theorem). That is the most common way to prove uniform continuity in practice.

## Statement

1. Prove that $|\sqrt x-\sqrt y|\le\sqrt{|x-y|}$ for all $x,y\ge0$.
2. Deduce that $x\mapsto\sqrt x$ is uniformly continuous on $[0,+\infty)$.
3. Show that it is not Lipschitz on $[0,1]$.

## Hint

For part 1, assume $x\ge y$ and square the inequality $\sqrt x\le\sqrt y+\sqrt{x-y}$.

## Solution

**1.** By symmetry assume $x\ge y\ge0$. We want $\sqrt x\le\sqrt y+\sqrt{x-y}$. Both sides are non-negative, so it is equivalent to the inequality of squares:
$$x\le y+(x-y)+2\sqrt{y(x-y)}=x+2\sqrt{y(x-y)},$$
which is true. Hence $\sqrt x-\sqrt y\le\sqrt{x-y}$, and the left side is $\ge0$.

**2.** Let $\varepsilon>0$ and $\delta=\varepsilon^2$. If $|x-y|<\delta$, then $|\sqrt x-\sqrt y|\le\sqrt{|x-y|}<\sqrt\delta=\varepsilon$. The choice of $\delta$ does not depend on the point.

**3.** If $\sqrt x$ were $K$-Lipschitz on $[0,1]$, then taking $y=0$ gives $\sqrt x\le Kx$, i.e. $\frac1{\sqrt x}\le K$ for all $x\in(0,1]$. But $\frac1{\sqrt x}\to+\infty$ as $x\to0^+$. Contradiction.

**Remark.** So Lipschitz is strictly stronger than uniformly continuous. The problem is the vertical tangent of the graph of $\sqrt x$ at $0$: the slope is unbounded, but the function still moves slowly enough that $\delta=\varepsilon^2$ works.

## Statement

Show that $f(x)=x^2$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ but not uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. Show that it is uniformly continuous on $[0,5]$.

## Hint

To negate uniform continuity, find two sequences $(x_n)$, $(y_n)$ with $x_n-y_n\to0$ but $f(x_n)-f(y_n)\not\to0$. For the second part, factor $x^2-y^2$.

## Solution

**Negation.** $f$ is *not* uniformly continuous if there is an $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that for every $\delta>0$ one can find $x,y$ with $|x-y|<\delta$ and $|f(x)-f(y)|\ge\varepsilon_0$.

**On $\mathbb{R}$.** Take $x_n=n+\frac1n$ and $y_n=n$. Then $|x_n-y_n|=\frac1n\to0$, but
$$x_n^2-y_n^2=2+\frac1{n^2}\ge2.$$
So $\varepsilon_0=2$ works: however small $\delta$ is, for $n>\frac1\delta$ the points $x_n,y_n$ are $\delta$-close with values at least $2$ apart.

**On $[0,5]$.** For $x,y\in[0,5]$, $|x^2-y^2|=|x-y|\,|x+y|\le10|x-y|$. So $f$ is $10$-Lipschitz on $[0,5]$, hence uniformly continuous.

**Moral.** The faster the graph climbs, the smaller $\delta$ must be. On the whole line the slope $2x$ is unbounded, so no single $\delta$ can do. On a bounded interval it is bounded.

## Statement

1. Show that $f(x)=\frac1x$ is continuous on $(0,1]$ but not uniformly continuous there.
2. Is it uniformly continuous on $[\frac1{10},1]$? Which theorem explains the difference?

## Hint

Take two sequences tending to $0$ and getting closer to each other, while their images move apart.

## Solution

**1. Not uniformly continuous.** Take $x_n=\frac1n$ and $y_n=\frac1{2n}$ in $(0,1]$. Then $|x_n-y_n|=\frac1{2n}\to0$, but
$$|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|=|n-2n|=n\ge1.$$
Thus $\varepsilon_0=1$ defeats every $\delta$.

**2. On $[\frac1{10},1]$.** For $x,y\ge\frac1{10}$,
$$\left|\frac1x-\frac1y\right|=\frac{|x-y|}{xy}\le100\,|x-y|,$$
so $f$ is $100$-Lipschitz, hence uniformly continuous.

**The theorem.** Heine's theorem states that a continuous function on a **closed bounded** interval $[a,b]$ is automatically uniformly continuous. The problem on $(0,1]$ is that the interval is not closed: the function misbehaves near the missing endpoint $0$.

**Remark.** A continuous function on $(0,1]$ is uniformly continuous if and only if it has a finite limit at $0$, that is, if and only if it extends continuously to $[0,1]$.
