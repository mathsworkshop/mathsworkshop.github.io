## Statement

Show that the equation $x^3+x-1=0$ has exactly one real solution, and that it lies in $(0,1)$.

## Hint

Use the intermediate value theorem for existence and the derivative for uniqueness.

## Solution

Let $f(x)=x^3+x-1$. It is continuous, with $f(0)=-1<0$ and $f(1)=1>0$. By the intermediate value theorem, $f$ vanishes at some point of $(0,1)$.

Its derivative $f'(x)=3x^2+1$ is positive everywhere, so $f$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$. A strictly increasing function takes each value at most once, so the root is unique.

## Statement

Let $f,g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ be continuous and commute under composition:
$$f\circ g=g\circ f.$$

1. Assume $f(x)>g(x)$ for every $x\in[0,1]$. Show there is $\alpha>0$ such that, for every $n\ge1$ and every $x\in[0,1]$,
$$f^{[n]}(x)\ge g^{[n]}(x)+n\alpha,$$
where $f^{[n]}$ denotes the $n$-fold iterate of $f$.

2. Deduce that there exists $c\in[0,1]$ with $f(c)=g(c)$.

## Hint

1. Apply the extreme value theorem to $\phi=f-g$ to get $\alpha=\min_{[0,1]}\phi>0$; then argue by induction, using that $f^{[n]}$ and $g^{[n]}$ also commute (a consequence of $f\circ g=g\circ f$).

2. Argue by contradiction: if $\phi$ never vanishes, it keeps a constant strict sign by the IVT, contradicting 1. since both iterates stay in $[0,1]$.

## Solution

**Preliminary: iterates commute.** From $f\circ g=g\circ f$ one checks by induction that $f^{[n]}\circ g=g\circ f^{[n]}$ and, more generally, that $f^{[n]}\circ g^{[n]}=g^{[n]}\circ f^{[n]}$ for all $n\ge1$. We will use this in the inductive step.

**Part 1.** Let $\phi=f-g$. Since $\phi$ is continuous on the compact interval $[0,1]$, the extreme value theorem gives
$$\alpha=\min_{x\in[0,1]}\phi(x).$$
Because $\phi(x)>0$ for every $x$, we have $\alpha>0$, and
$$f(x)\ge g(x)+\alpha\quad\forall x\in[0,1].\tag{$\ast$}$$

*Base case $n=1$.* This is exactly $(\ast)$.

*Inductive step.* Suppose $f^{[n]}(x)\ge g^{[n]}(x)+n\alpha$ for all $x\in[0,1]$. Fix $x\in[0,1]$. Since $f(x)\in[0,1]$, we may apply the inductive hypothesis to the point $f(x)$:
$$f^{[n]}(f(x))\ge g^{[n]}(f(x))+n\alpha.$$
The left-hand side is $f^{[n+1]}(x)$. By the preliminary commutation, $g^{[n]}(f(x))=f(g^{[n]}(x))$. Applying $(\ast)$ at the point $y=g^{[n]}(x)\in[0,1]$ gives
$$f(g^{[n]}(x))\ge g(g^{[n]}(x))+\alpha=g^{[n+1]}(x)+\alpha.$$
Combining,
$$f^{[n+1]}(x)\ge g^{[n+1]}(x)+n\alpha+\alpha=g^{[n+1]}(x)+(n+1)\alpha.$$
This completes the induction.

**Part 2.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $\phi(x)\neq0$ for every $x\in[0,1]$. Since $\phi$ is continuous on the interval $[0,1]$, the intermediate value theorem forces $\phi$ to have a constant strict sign on $[0,1]$.

*Case 1: $\phi>0$ on $[0,1]$.* Part 1 then gives $f^{[n]}(x)\ge g^{[n]}(x)+n\alpha$ for every $n\ge1$ and $x\in[0,1]$. In particular,
$$f^{[n]}(x)\ge n\alpha.$$
Fix any $x\in[0,1]$. Since $f$ takes its values in $[0,1]$, we have $f^{[n]}(x)\le 1$ for all $n$. Hence $n\alpha\le1$ for all $n\ge1$, which is impossible because $\alpha>0$ and $n\to+\infty$.

*Case 2: $\phi<0$ on $[0,1]$.* Exchanging the roles of $f$ and $g$ in Part 1 produces the same contradiction.

Both cases are impossible, so the assumption $\phi\neq0$ everywhere fails. By continuity, there is $c\in[0,1]$ with $\phi(c)=0$, i.e. $f(c)=g(c)$.

## Statement

Let $a\in\mathbb{R}$. Discuss the continuity at $x=a$ of
$$f(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(x-a)\,e^{1/(x-a)}}{1+e^{1/(x-a)}}&\text{if }x\neq a,\\[2mm]0&\text{if }x=a.\end{cases}$$

## Hint

For $x=a+h$, factor out $h$ in the numerator and bound the remaining factor.

## Solution

For $x=a+h$ with $h\neq0$,
$$f(a+h)=h\cdot\frac{e^{1/h}}{1+e^{1/h}}.$$
Since $e^{1/h}>0$, we have $0<\dfrac{e^{1/h}}{1+e^{1/h}}<1$. Hence
$$|f(a+h)-f(a)|=|f(a+h)|<|h|.$$
As $h\to0$, $|h|\to0$, so $f(a+h)\to f(a)=0$. Therefore $f$ is continuous at $a$.

**Conclusion.** The two one-sided limits both equal $0$, which equals $f(a)$. Hence $f$ is **continuous at $x=a$**.

## Statement

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{N}^*\to\mathbb{N}^*$ (positive integers) such that $f(f(n))=n+2$ for all $n\ge1$.

## Hint

Apply $f$ to both sides to show that $f(n+2)=f(n)+2$. Then everything is determined by $f(1)$ and $f(2)$.

## Solution

**Step 1: a recursion.** Apply $f$ to $f(f(n))=n+2$ and use the equation with $n$ replaced by $f(n)$:
$$f(n+2)=f\bigl(f(f(n))\bigr)=f(n)+2.$$
Let $a=f(1)$ and $b=f(2)$. Then for $k\ge1$:
$$f(2k+1)=a+2k,\qquad f(2k)=b+2k-2.$$

**Step 2: using the equation at $n=1$.** $f(a)=3$.
- If $a$ is odd, $a=2m+1$, then $f(a)=a+2m=2a-1$; setting this equal to $3$ gives $a=2$, which is not odd. Contradiction.
- So $a$ is even, $a=2m$, and $f(a)=b+a-2=3$ gives $b=5-a\ge1$, so $a\in\{2,4\}$.

**Step 3: two candidates.**
- $a=2$, $b=3$: $f(2k+1)=2k+2$ and $f(2k)=2k+1$, i.e. $f(n)=n+1$.
- $a=4$, $b=1$: $f(2k+1)=2k+4$ and $f(2k)=2k-1$, i.e. $f(n)=n+3$ for odd $n$ and $f(n)=n-1$ for even $n$.

**Step 4: verification.** For $f(n)=n+1$: $f(f(n))=n+2$. For the second function, if $n$ is odd then $f(n)=n+3$ is even, so $f(f(n))=n+3-1=n+2$; if $n$ is even then $f(n)=n-1$ is odd (and $\ge1$), so $f(f(n))=n-1+3=n+2$.

**Answer.** Exactly two functions: $f(n)=n+1$, and $f(n)=n+3$ for odd $n$, $f(n)=n-1$ for even $n$.

**Lesson.** Reduce $f(f(n))=g(n)$ to a recursion by applying $f$ again, then use the small values to pin down the free parameters, and always check whether more than one solution survives.

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfy $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$ for all rationals $x,y$. Prove that $f(x)=cx$ for all $x\in\mathbb{Q}$, where $c=f(1)$.

## Hint

Show first $f(0)=0$ and $f(-x)=-f(x)$, then $f(n)=nf(1)$ for integers, then treat $\frac mn$.

## Solution

**Step 1.** $x=y=0$ gives $f(0)=2f(0)$, so $f(0)=0$. Then $0=f(x+(-x))=f(x)+f(-x)$, so $f$ is odd.

**Step 2: integers.** By induction $f(n)=nf(1)$ for $n\ge0$: $f(n+1)=f(n)+f(1)$. For negative integers use oddness.

**Step 3: rationals.** Let $x=\frac mn$ with $n\ge1$. Then
$$n\,f\!\left(\frac mn\right)=f\!\left(\underbrace{\frac mn+\dots+\frac mn}_{n}\right)=f(m)=mf(1),$$
hence $f\!\left(\frac mn\right)=\frac mnf(1)$.

**Conclusion.** $f(x)=cx$ with $c=f(1)$, and conversely every such function satisfies the equation.

**Remark.** On $\mathbb{R}$ the same argument only gives $f(x)=cx$ for rational $x$: there exist additive functions $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ that are not linear (they are wild and nowhere continuous). One needs an extra assumption (monotone, bounded on an interval, continuous at a point) to conclude.

**Lesson.** Start every additive equation with $f(0)=0$, oddness, and the integer case; the rationals follow by "multiply by the denominator".

## Statement

Find all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that $f(\lfloor x\rfloor y)=f(x)\lfloor f(y)\rfloor$ for all real $x,y$.

## Hint

Put $x=0$ to find that either $f(0)\ne0$, which forces $\lfloor f(y)\rfloor=1$ for all $y$, or $f(0)=0$. In the second case, prove that $f$ vanishes on $(0,1)$ first.

## Solution

**Case 1: $f(0)\ne0$.** Setting $x=0$: $f(0)=f(0)\lfloor f(y)\rfloor$, so $\lfloor f(y)\rfloor=1$ for every $y$. The equation becomes $f(\lfloor x\rfloor y)=f(x)$ for all $x,y$; with $y=0$ we get $f(0)=f(x)$, so $f$ is a constant $c$ with $\lfloor c\rfloor=1$, i.e. $c\in[1,2)$. These constants satisfy the equation: both sides equal $c$.

**Case 2: $f(0)=0$.**

*Step 1: $f=0$ on $(0,1)$.* For $0<x<1$, $\lfloor x\rfloor=0$ and the equation reads $0=f(x)\lfloor f(y)\rfloor$ for all $y$. If $f(x)\ne0$ for some $x\in(0,1)$, then $\lfloor f(y)\rfloor=0$ for all $y$. Now put $x=1$: $f(y)=f(1)\lfloor f(y)\rfloor=0$ for all $y$, contradicting $f(x)\ne0$. Hence $f=0$ on $(0,1)$.

*Step 2: $f=0$ on $(0,\infty)$.* Take $z>0$ and an integer $n\ge1$ with $z/n<1$. Putting $x=n$ and $y=z/n$: $f(z)=f(n)\lfloor f(z/n)\rfloor=f(n)\lfloor0\rfloor=0$.

*Step 3: $f=0$ on $(-\infty,0)$.* Put $x=-1$: $f(-y)=f(-1)\lfloor f(y)\rfloor$. For $y\in(0,1)$, $f(y)=0$ so $f(-y)=0$: $f$ vanishes on $(-1,0)$. For $z<0$, take an integer $m\ge1$ with $|z|/m<1$, and put $x=-m$ and $y=z/(-m)\in(0,1)$. Then $\lfloor x\rfloor y=z$ and $f(z)=f(-m)\lfloor f(y)\rfloor=f(-m)\lfloor0\rfloor=0$.

So $f\equiv0$.

**Answer.** $f\equiv0$, or $f\equiv c$ for a constant $c\in[1,2)$.

**Lesson.** Case-split on the "degenerate" value ($f(0)=0$ or not) first, then propagate zeros from a small interval outward using the scaling built into the equation.

## Statement

Let $a>0$ and let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfy
$$f(x+a)=\frac12+\sqrt{f(x)-f(x)^2}\quad\text{for all }x.$$
Prove that $f$ is periodic with period $2a$. Give a non-constant example.

## Hint

Note that $f(x)\ge\frac12$ for all $x$. Square the relation in the form $\left(f(x+a)-\frac12\right)^2=\ldots$ and complete the square on the right.

## Solution

**Lower bound.** The square root is defined, so $f(x)-f(x)^2\ge0$ and the equation shows $f(x+a)\ge\frac12$ for every $x$. Hence $f(t)\ge\frac12$ for all real $t$ (put $x=t-a$).

**Squaring.** From the equation, $\left(f(x+a)-\frac12\right)^2=f(x)-f(x)^2=\frac14-\left(f(x)-\frac12\right)^2$. Thus
$$\left(f(x+a)-\tfrac12\right)^2+\left(f(x)-\tfrac12\right)^2=\tfrac14\qquad\text{for all }x.$$

**Shifting by $a$.** Replace $x$ by $x+a$:
$$\left(f(x+2a)-\tfrac12\right)^2+\left(f(x+a)-\tfrac12\right)^2=\tfrac14.$$
Subtracting the two displayed relations,
$$\left(f(x+2a)-\tfrac12\right)^2=\left(f(x)-\tfrac12\right)^2.$$
Since $f(x+2a)-\frac12\ge0$ and $f(x)-\frac12\ge0$, we get $f(x+2a)=f(x)$.

**Example.** $f(x)=\dfrac12+\dfrac12\left|\sin\dfrac{\pi x}{2a}\right|$. Put $\theta=\frac{\pi x}{2a}$. Then $f(x+a)=\frac12+\frac12|\cos\theta|$, and $f(x)-f(x)^2=\frac14-\frac14\sin^2\theta=\frac14\cos^2\theta$, whose square root is $\frac12|\cos\theta|$ ✓.

**Lesson.** When a relation contains a square root, isolate it and square: the resulting symmetric identity in $f(x)$ and $f(x+a)$ often yields periodicity by shifting once.

## Statement

Find all $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that $f(f(x)+y)=2x+f(f(y)-x)$ for all real $x,y$.

## Hint

Choose $y$ so that the left side becomes $f(0)$, to show that $f$ is surjective. Then take $x=a$ with $f(a)=0$.

## Solution

**Step 1: surjectivity.** Put $y=-f(x)$: $f(0)=2x+f\bigl(f(-f(x))-x\bigr)$, so
$$f\bigl(f(-f(x))-x\bigr)=f(0)-2x.$$
As $x$ ranges over $\mathbb{R}$, the right side takes every real value, so $f$ is surjective.

**Step 2: a zero.** Let $a$ be such that $f(a)=0$ and put $x=a$:
$$f(y)=2a+f\bigl(f(y)-a\bigr)\qquad\text{for all }y.$$

**Step 3: linear form.** Let $u=f(y)-a$. Since $f$ is surjective, $u$ ranges over $\mathbb{R}$. Then $f(y)=u+a$ and the relation becomes $u+a=2a+f(u)$, i.e.
$$f(u)=u-a\qquad\text{for all }u.$$
So $f(x)=x+c$ with $c=-a$.

**Step 4: verification.** For $f(x)=x+c$: the left side is $f(x+c+y)=x+y+2c$, and the right side is $2x+f(y+c-x)=2x+y+c-x+c=x+y+2c$ ✓.

**Answer.** $f(x)=x+c$ for any constant $c\in\mathbb{R}$.

**Lesson.** Surjectivity (via a substitution that makes one side independent of the argument) lets you replace $f(y)$ by a free variable: this is the standard route from a messy equation to a linear one.

Find all $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$ and $f(x^2)=f(x)^2$ for all real $x,y$.

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{N}^*\to\mathbb{N}^*$ be strictly increasing with $f(f(n))=3n$ for all $n\ge1$. Determine $f(n)$ completely and compute $f(2024)$.

## Hint

First show $f(3n)=3f(n)$ and $n<f(n)<3n$. Then find $f(1)$ and $f(2)$, and use monotonicity on the blocks $[3^k,2\cdot3^k]$.

## Solution

**Step 1: $f(3n)=3f(n)$.** $f(3n)=f(f(f(n)))=3f(n)$ (apply the equation to $f(n)$).

**Step 2: $n<f(n)<3n$.** If $f(n)\le n$, monotonicity gives $3n=f(f(n))\le f(n)\le n$, impossible. If $f(n)\ge3n$, then $3n=f(f(n))\ge f(3n)=3f(n)\ge9n$, impossible.

**Step 3: small values.** $1<f(1)<3$ so $f(1)=2$, and $f(2)=f(f(1))=3$. By Step 1, $f(3^k)=2\cdot3^k$ and $f(2\cdot3^k)=3^{k+1}$.

**Step 4: first half of each block.** The integers $3^k,\dots,2\cdot3^k$ are $3^k+1$ numbers, and $f$ maps them strictly increasingly into $\{2\cdot3^k,\dots,3^{k+1}\}$, which also has $3^k+1$ elements, with the end points matching. So the map is the shift:
$$f(3^k+j)=2\cdot3^k+j,\qquad0\le j\le3^k.$$

**Step 5: second half.** For $0\le j\le3^k$, $f(2\cdot3^k+j)=f\bigl(f(3^k+j)\bigr)=3(3^k+j)=3^{k+1}+3j$.

**Formula.** For $3^k\le n\le2\cdot3^k$: $f(n)=n+3^k$. For $2\cdot3^k\le n\le3^{k+1}$: $f(n)=3n-3^{k+1}$. (The two formulas agree at the common point $n=2\cdot3^k$: $f=3^{k+1}$ in both. Both pieces are increasing and they match at the junction, so $f$ is strictly increasing.)

**Verification.** For $n$ in the first range, $f(n)=n+3^k$ lies in the second range, $f(f(n))=3(n+3^k)-3^{k+1}=3n$ ✓. For $n$ in the second range, $f(n)=3n-3^{k+1}$ lies in $[3^{k+1},2\cdot3^{k+1}]$ and $f(f(n))=3n-3^{k+1}+3^{k+1}=3n$ ✓.

**The value.** $3^6=729$, $2\cdot3^6=1458$, $3^7=2187$. Since $1458\le2024\le2187$ we use the second formula with $k=6$:
$$f(2024)=3\cdot2024-2187=3885.$$

**Lesson.** For $f(f(n))=g(n)$ with $f$ monotone, trap $f$ between two bounds, find the values on a geometric sequence, and use the pigeonhole/order argument to fill in the blocks.

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfy $f\circ f=\cos$. Show that $f$ is not continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.

## Hint

First show that $f$ commutes with $\cos$. Then focus on the interval $J=[\cos 1,1]$, where $\cos$ is strictly decreasing and maps $J$ into itself. Assume $f$ continuous on $J$ and derive a contradiction from the monotonicity of $f$ and $f\circ f$.

## Solution

**Step 1: $f$ commutes with $\cos$.** Composing $f\circ f=\cos$ on the left and on the right by $f$:
$$f\circ\cos=f\circ(f\circ f)=(f\circ f)\circ f=\cos\circ f.$$
So $f(\cos x)=\cos(f(x))$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 2: $f$ sends $[-1,1]$ into $[-1,1]$.** Since $\cos(\mathbb{R})=[-1,1]$, for $y\in[-1,1]$ we have $y=\cos x$ for some $x$, hence $y=f(f(x))\in f(\mathbb{R})$. Thus
$$f([-1,1])=f(\cos\mathbb{R})=\cos(f(\mathbb{R}))\subseteq[-1,1].$$

**Step 3: consider $J=[\cos1,1]$.** Since $0<\cos1<1$, the map $\cos$ is strictly decreasing on $[0,1]$, hence on $J\subseteq[0,1]$. Moreover $\cos(J)\subseteq J$: for $x\in J$, $x\le 1$ gives $\cos x\ge\cos1$, and $x\ge\cos1\ge0$ gives $\cos x\le 1$. So $\cos$ maps $J$ into $J$, strictly decreasing.

**Step 4: $f$ is injective on $J$.** If $x,y\in J$ and $f(x)=f(y)$, then $\cos x=f(f(x))=f(f(y))=\cos y$. Since $\cos$ is injective on $J$, $x=y$. Hence $f|_J$ is injective.

**Step 5: contradiction if $f$ is continuous.** Suppose $f$ continuous. Then $f|_J$ is continuous and injective on the interval $J$, hence **strictly monotonic** on $J$.

- If $f|_J$ is increasing, then for $x<y$ in $J$: $f(x)<f(y)$, so $f(f(x))<f(f(y))$, i.e. $\cos x<\cos y$. But $\cos$ is decreasing on $J$, so $x<y\Rightarrow\cos x>\cos y$ — contradiction.
- If $f|_J$ is decreasing, then for $x<y$: $f(x)>f(y)$, so $f(f(x))<f(f(y))$, i.e. $\cos x<\cos y$. Again, $\cos$ decreasing on $J$ gives $\cos x>\cos y$ — contradiction.

So $f$ cannot be continuous on $J$, hence **$f$ is not continuous on $\mathbb{R}$**.

## Statement

$$f\left(x^2+f(y)\right)=y+f(x)^2.$$

## Hint

Put $x=0$ to get injectivity and surjectivity. Then use a $t$ with $f(t)=0$. Show $f$ is odd, $f(0)=0$, and that $f$ is additive and nonnegative on $[0,\infty)$.

## Solution

Let $a=f(0)$.

**Step 1.** $x=0$: $f(f(y))=y+a^2$. Thus $f$ is injective and surjective (the right side takes every real value).

**Step 2.** By surjectivity pick $t$ with $f(t)=0$. Putting $y=t$ gives
$$f(x^2)=t+f(x)^2\qquad(\ast)$$
for all $x$.

**Step 3: oddness.** Replacing $x$ by $-x$ in $(\ast)$ gives $f(x)^2=f(-x)^2$, so $f(-x)=\pm f(x)$. If $x\neq0$, the case $f(-x)=f(x)$ contradicts injectivity. Hence $f(-x)=-f(x)$ for all $x\ne0$. $$f(u+y)=f(u)+f(y)\qquad(u\ge0,\ y\in\mathbb{R}).$$
For $u<0$, use oddness: $f(u+y)=-f((-u)+(-y))=-f(-u)-f(-y)=f(u)+f(y)$. So $f$ is additive on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 5.** An additive function that is $\ge0$ on $[0,\infty)$ is non-decreasing, hence $f(x)=cx$ (Cauchy with monotonicity). From $f(f(x))=x$: $c^2=1$, and $f(1)=c\ge0$ gives $c=1$.

**Answer.** $f(x)=x$ (check: $f(x^2+y)=x^2+y$ ✓).

**Lesson.** A strong sequence of moves: injective $\to$ locate the zero $\to$ oddness $\to$ $f(f(y))=y$ $\to$ additivity $\to$ monotonicity from nonnegativity of squares.

**Conclusion.** The functions $f+g$ and $f-g$ are continuous (sums of continuous functions), so $|f-g|$ is continuous (previous problem). Hence $\max(f,g)=\frac12(f+g+|f-g|)$ and $\min(f,g)=\frac12(f+g-|f-g|)$ are continuous.

**Application.** Setting $f^+=\max(f,0)$ and $f^-=\max(-f,0)$ shows that the positive and negative parts of a continuous function are continuous, and $f=f^+-f^-$.

- $g(1)=f(1)-1\le0$.

If $g(0)=0$ or $g(1)=0$, we are done. Otherwise $g(0)>0>g(1)$ and the intermediate value theorem gives $c\in(0,1)$ with $g(c)=0$, that is $f(c)=c$.

**Remark.** Both hypotheses matter. Continuity: $f(x)=1$ on $[0,\frac12)$ and $f(x)=0$ on $[\frac12,1]$ has no fixed point. The interval being closed: $f(x)=\frac x2$ maps $(0,1]$ to itself with no fixed point. This is the one-dimensional case of Brouwer's fixed point theorem.

## Solution

1. Show that $\cos x=x$ has exactly one solution $\alpha$ in $[0,1]$.
2. Perform ten steps of the bisection method starting from $[0,1]$.
3. How many steps are needed to locate $\alpha$ within $10^{-6}$?

Study $g(x)=\cos x-x$. At each step, cut the interval in half and keep the half where $g$ changes sign.

## Solution

**1. Existence and uniqueness.** $g(x)=\cos x-x$ is continuous with $g(0)=1>0$ and $g(1)=\cos1-1<0$, so a root exists by the intermediate value theorem. Moreover $g'(x)=-\sin x-1\le0$, and it vanishes only at isolated points, so $g$ is strictly decreasing and the root is unique.

- Step 1: $[0.5,\ 1]$
- Step 2: $[0.5,\ 0.75]$
- Step 3: $[0.625,\ 0.75]$
- Step 4: $[0.6875,\ 0.75]$
- Step 5: $[0.71875,\ 0.75]$
- Step 6: $[0.734375,\ 0.75]$
- Step 7: $[0.734375,\ 0.7421875]$
- Step 8: $[0.73828125,\ 0.7421875]$
- Step 9: $[0.73828125,\ 0.740234375]$
- Step 10: $[0.73828125,\ 0.7392578125]$

- On $[2,+\infty)$, $f$ is continuous and strictly decreasing from $2.94$ to $0$: it takes the value $1$ exactly once, at some $t_2$.

## Statement

Let $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous and suppose $f(x)$ is rational for every $x$. Prove that $f$ is constant.

## Hint

Argue by contradiction. If $f(a)\ne f(b)$, what does the intermediate value theorem say about the values between them?

## Solution

**Idea.** A continuous function cannot skip any value between two of its values. But the rational numbers have gaps: between two rationals there are irrational numbers.

**Proof.** Suppose $f(a)\neq f(b)$ for some $a,b\in[0,1]$, say $f(a)<f(b)$. The interval $\bigl(f(a),f(b)\bigr)$ contains an irrational number $y$ (for example $f(a)+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\,\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)$, which lies strictly between since $0<\frac{\sqrt2}2<1$, and is irrational because $f(b)-f(a)$ is a non-zero rational).

By the intermediate value theorem on the interval between $a$ and $b$, there is $c$ with $f(c)=y$. But $f(c)$ is rational and $y$ is not. Contradiction.

**Conclusion.** $f(a)=f(b)$ for all $a,b$, so $f$ is constant.

**Remark.** The same argument shows that a continuous function with values in $\mathbb{Z}$ is constant, and more generally that a continuous function on an interval cannot take only values in a countable set unless it is constant.

## Statement

A function $f:I\to\mathbb{R}$ is **$K$-Lipschitz** if $|f(x)-f(y)|\le K|x-y|$ for all $x,y\in I$. Prove that a Lipschitz function is uniformly continuous. Give two examples.

## Hint

Given $\varepsilon$, find a $\delta$ that depends only on $\varepsilon$ and $K$, not on the point.

## Solution

**Definition reminder.** $f$ is uniformly continuous if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that for **all** $x,y$ with $|x-y|<\delta$, $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. The difference with ordinary continuity is that $\delta$ must work for all points at once.

**Proof.** Assume $K>0$ (the case $K=0$ is trivial since $f$ is constant). Let $\varepsilon>0$ and set $\delta=\frac\varepsilon K$. If $|x-y|<\delta$, then
$$|f(x)-f(y)|\le K|x-y|<K\delta=\varepsilon.$$

**Examples.**
- $x\mapsto\sin x$ is $1$-Lipschitz on $\mathbb{R}$, by the mean value theorem since $|\cos|\le1$.
- $x\mapsto|x|$ is $1$-Lipschitz by the reverse triangle inequality, although it is not differentiable at $0$.

**Remark.** On an interval, a differentiable function with bounded derivative $|f'|\le K$ is $K$-Lipschitz (mean value theorem). That is the most common way to prove uniform continuity in practice.

1. Show that $f(x)=\frac1x$ is continuous on $(0,1]$ but not uniformly continuous there.
2. Is it uniformly continuous on $[\frac1{10},1]$? Which theorem explains the difference?

## Hint

Take two sequences tending to $0$ and getting closer to each other, while their images move apart.

## Solution

**1. Not uniformly continuous.** Take $x_n=\frac1n$ and $y_n=\frac1{2n}$ in $(0,1]$. Then $|x_n-y_n|=\frac1{2n}\to0$, but
$$|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|=|n-2n|=n\ge1.$$
Thus $\varepsilon_0=1$ defeats every $\delta$.

**2. On $[\frac1{10},1]$.** For $x,y\ge\frac1{10}$,
$$\left|\frac1x-\frac1y\right|=\frac{|x-y|}{xy}\le100\,|x-y|,$$
so $f$ is $100$-Lipschitz, hence uniformly continuous.

**The theorem.** Heine's theorem states that a continuous function on a **closed bounded** interval $[a,b]$ is automatically uniformly continuous. The problem on $(0,1]$ is that the interval is not closed: the function misbehaves near the missing endpoint $0$.

**Remark.** A continuous function on $(0,1]$ is uniformly continuous if and only if it has a finite limit at $0$, that is, if and only if it extends continuously to $[0,1]$.
