## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$. Prove that
1. $(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)\ge8abc$,
2. $(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)\ge\dfrac89(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)$.

## Hint

For 1, apply $x+y\ge2\sqrt{xy}$ to each factor. For 2, first expand $(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)$ in terms of $(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)$ and $abc$.

## Solution

**1.** By AM-GM, $a+b\ge2\sqrt{ab}$, $b+c\ge2\sqrt{bc}$, $c+a\ge2\sqrt{ca}$. All sides are positive, so we may multiply:
$$(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)\ge8\sqrt{a^2b^2c^2}=8abc.$$
Equality iff $a=b=c$.

**2.** Expanding, $(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)=\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b+3abc$ and $(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)=\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b+2abc$, where $\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b$ has the six terms $a^2b,a^2c,b^2a,b^2c,c^2a,c^2b$. Hence
$$(a+b)(b+c)(c+a)=(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)-abc.$$
Put $P=(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)$. The claim becomes $P-abc\ge\frac89P$, i.e. $P\ge9abc$. By AM-GM, $a+b+c\ge3\sqrt[3]{abc}$ and $ab+bc+ca\ge3\sqrt[3]{(abc)^2}$; multiplying gives $P\ge9abc$.

**Remark.** Part 1 is a very frequent step: it says that a product of pairwise sums is at least $8$ times the product.

**Lesson.** Apply AM-GM to *small groups* of variables and multiply; and rewrite symmetric expressions with $p=a+b+c$, $q=ab+bc+ca$, $r=abc$.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$. Prove that
$$\frac a{b+c}+\frac b{c+a}+\frac c{a+b}\ge\frac32.$$

## Hint

Write $\frac a{b+c}=\frac{a^2}{a(b+c)}$ and use Titu's lemma (the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in Engel form): $\sum\frac{x_i^2}{y_i}\ge\frac{(\sum x_i)^2}{\sum y_i}$ for $y_i>0$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Put every fraction in the form $\frac{x^2}{y}$.

**Titu.**
$$\sum\frac a{b+c}=\sum\frac{a^2}{a(b+c)}\ge\frac{(a+b+c)^2}{\sum a(b+c)}=\frac{(a+b+c)^2}{2(ab+bc+ca)}.$$

**Last step.** $(a+b+c)^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2(ab+bc+ca)\ge3(ab+bc+ca)$ because $a^2+b^2+c^2\ge ab+bc+ca$ (it is $\frac12\sum(a-b)^2\ge0$). Therefore
$$\frac{(a+b+c)^2}{2(ab+bc+ca)}\ge\frac{3(ab+bc+ca)}{2(ab+bc+ca)}=\frac32.$$

**Equality** iff $a=b=c$.

**Remark (Titu from Cauchy-Schwarz).** $\left(\sum\frac{x_i^2}{y_i}\right)\left(\sum y_i\right)\ge\left(\sum x_i\right)^2$ is Cauchy-Schwarz applied to the vectors $\left(\frac{x_i}{\sqrt{y_i}}\right)$ and $\left(\sqrt{y_i}\right)$.

**Lesson.** A sum of fractions $\frac{\text{something}}{\text{something}}$: multiply numerator and denominator to create squares in the numerators, then use Titu.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$. Prove that
$$\frac{a^2}b+\frac{b^2}c+\frac{c^2}a\ge a+b+c.$$

## Hint

Titu's lemma: $\sum\frac{x_i^2}{y_i}\ge\frac{(\sum x_i)^2}{\sum y_i}$. Choose $x_i$ and $y_i$ directly from the three fractions.

## Solution

**Titu with $(x_i)=(a,b,c)$ and $(y_i)=(b,c,a)$.**
$$\frac{a^2}b+\frac{b^2}c+\frac{c^2}a\ge\frac{(a+b+c)^2}{b+c+a}=a+b+c.$$

**Second proof (tangent trick).** For positive $x,y$: $\frac{x^2}y\ge2x-y$, because $\frac{x^2}y-2x+y=\frac{(x-y)^2}y\ge0$. Hence
$$\sum\frac{a^2}b\ge(2a-b)+(2b-c)+(2c-a)=a+b+c.$$

**Equality** iff $a=b=c$.

**Remark.** The second proof shows the strength of the inequality $\frac{x^2}y\ge2x-y$: it is the tangent line at $y=x$ of the convex function $y\mapsto\frac{x^2}y$.

**Lesson.** Know the two faces of the same tool: Titu (global) and $\frac{x^2}y\ge2x-y$ (term by term).

## Statement

Let $a,b,c\ge0$. Prove that $a^3+b^3+c^3\ge a^2b+b^2c+c^2a$, in two different ways.

## Hint

(i) The rearrangement inequality: if $x_1\le x_2\le x_3$ and $y_1\le y_2\le y_3$, then $\sum x_iy_i\ge\sum x_iy_{\sigma(i)}$ for every permutation $\sigma$. (ii) Weighted AM-GM on $a^3,a^3,b^3$.

## Solution

**Rearrangement.** The sequences $(a,b,c)$ and $(a^2,b^2,c^2)$ are ordered in the same way (if $a\le b$ then $a^2\le b^2$ for nonnegative numbers). The sum of products of the identically ordered terms is at least the sum for any other pairing:
$$a\cdot a^2+b\cdot b^2+c\cdot c^2\ge b\cdot a^2+c\cdot b^2+a\cdot c^2=a^2b+b^2c+c^2a.$$

**AM-GM.** $\frac{a^3+a^3+b^3}3\ge\sqrt[3]{a^6b^3}=a^2b$, i.e. $2a^3+b^3\ge3a^2b$. Similarly $2b^3+c^3\ge3b^2c$ and $2c^3+a^3\ge3c^2a$. Adding: $3(a^3+b^3+c^3)\ge3(a^2b+b^2c+c^2a)$.

**Equality** iff $a=b=c$.

**Remark.** The reverse cyclic sum $a^2c+b^2a+c^2b$ satisfies the same bound, of course (rename the variables).

**Lesson.** Rearrangement is the tool when two sequences are *similarly ordered* and the sum is a "mismatched pairing"; AM-GM with repeated terms is the tool when you need to produce a specific monomial like $a^2b$.

## Statement

1. Prove Chebyshev's inequality: if $x_1\le x_2\le x_3$ and $y_1\le y_2\le y_3$ then $3\sum x_iy_i\ge\left(\sum x_i\right)\left(\sum y_i\right)$.
2. Deduce that for $a,b,c>0$: $a^ab^bc^c\ge(abc)^{(a+b+c)/3}$.

## Hint

For 1, expand $\sum_{i,j}(x_i-x_j)(y_i-y_j)$. For 2, apply part 1 to $(a,b,c)$ and $(\ln a,\ln b,\ln c)$.

## Solution

**1.** For all $i,j$, the numbers $x_i-x_j$ and $y_i-y_j$ have the same sign (the sequences are similarly ordered), so $(x_i-x_j)(y_i-y_j)\ge0$. Summing over all ordered pairs $(i,j)$:
$$0\le\sum_{i,j}(x_i-x_j)(y_i-y_j)=2\cdot3\sum_ix_iy_i-2\left(\sum x_i\right)\left(\sum y_j\right).$$
Dividing by $2$ gives the claim.

**2.** Since $\ln$ is increasing, $(a,b,c)$ and $(\ln a,\ln b,\ln c)$ are similarly ordered. By part 1,
$$3(a\ln a+b\ln b+c\ln c)\ge(a+b+c)(\ln a+\ln b+\ln c).$$
Divide by $3$ and apply $\exp$:
$$a^ab^bc^c\ge(abc)^{(a+b+c)/3}.$$

**Equality** iff $a=b=c$ (equality in Chebyshev forces $(x_i-x_j)(y_i-y_j)=0$ for all pairs, and $\ln$ is strictly increasing).

**Remark.** The same proof works for any $n$ and any increasing function in place of $\ln$.

**Lesson.** When a sum looks like an "average of a product versus product of averages", Chebyshev (equivalently, the covariance of two monotone sequences is nonnegative) is the tool.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$ with $a+b+c=1$. Prove that
$$\frac a{a^2+1}+\frac b{b^2+1}+\frac c{c^2+1}\le\frac9{10}.$$

## Hint

Equality should hold at $a=b=c=\frac13$. Find a line $\ell(a)=\alpha a+\beta$, tangent to $f(a)=\frac a{a^2+1}$ at $a=\frac13$, such that $f(a)\le\ell(a)$ for all $a>0$.

## Solution

**Finding the line.** $f\left(\frac13\right)=\frac{1/3}{10/9}=\frac3{10}$ and $f'(a)=\frac{1-a^2}{(a^2+1)^2}$, so $f'\left(\frac13\right)=\frac{8/9}{100/81}=\frac{18}{25}$. The tangent is
$$\ell(a)=\frac3{10}+\frac{18}{25}\left(a-\frac13\right)=\frac{18}{25}a+\frac3{50}.$$

**The one-variable inequality.** For $a>0$ we claim $\frac a{a^2+1}\le\frac{18a}{25}+\frac3{50}$. Multiplying by $50(a^2+1)>0$ it reads $50a\le(36a+3)(a^2+1)$, i.e.
$$36a^3+3a^2-14a+3\ge0.$$
This factors as $(3a-1)^2(4a+3)\ge0$, true for $a>0$ (indeed for $a\ge-\frac34$).

**Summing.** Add the three inequalities and use $a+b+c=1$:
$$\sum\frac a{a^2+1}\le\frac{18}{25}(a+b+c)+\frac9{50}=\frac{36}{50}+\frac9{50}=\frac9{10}.$$

**Remark.** The tangent line trick works when the constraint is linear and the function is "locally below its tangent". If the inequality $f\le\ell$ fails somewhere, one needs another method (SOS, mixing variables).

**Lesson.** Constraint $a+b+c=\text{const}$ and a sum $f(a)+f(b)+f(c)$: guess the equality case, compute the tangent at that point, and check the one-variable inequality.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$ with $a+b+c=1$. Prove that
$$\left(a+\frac1a\right)^2+\left(b+\frac1b\right)^2+\left(c+\frac1c\right)^2\ge\frac{100}3.$$

## Hint

Show that $f(x)=\left(x+\frac1x\right)^2$ is convex on $(0,\infty)$ and apply Jensen's inequality.

## Solution

**Convexity.** $f(x)=x^2+2+\frac1{x^2}$, so
$$f''(x)=2+\frac6{x^4}>0\quad(x>0).$$

**Jensen.** For a convex $f$: $\frac{f(a)+f(b)+f(c)}3\ge f\left(\frac{a+b+c}3\right)=f\left(\frac13\right)$. Here $f\left(\frac13\right)=\left(\frac13+3\right)^2=\frac{100}9$. Hence
$$f(a)+f(b)+f(c)\ge3\cdot\frac{100}9=\frac{100}3.$$

**Equality** iff $a=b=c=\frac13$.

**Remark.** An alternative is the tangent line trick at $\frac13$: $f(x)\ge f\left(\frac13\right)+f'\left(\frac13\right)\left(x-\frac13\right)$ is the tangent inequality of a convex function, and summing it gives Jensen.

**Lesson.** If $\sum f(a_i)$ has a *fixed sum* $\sum a_i$ and $f''\ge0$, Jensen gives the minimum at the equal point; $f''\le0$ gives the maximum.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$ with $abc=1$. Prove that
$$\frac1{a^3(b+c)}+\frac1{b^3(c+a)}+\frac1{c^3(a+b)}\ge\frac32.$$

## Hint

Substitute $x=\frac1a$, $y=\frac1b$, $z=\frac1c$ (so $xyz=1$) and simplify each term. You should find a Nesbitt-type sum.

## Solution

**Substitution.** With $x=\frac1a$ etc., $xyz=1$ and
$$\frac1{a^3(b+c)}=\frac{x^3}{\frac1y+\frac1z}=\frac{x^3yz}{y+z}=\frac{x^2\cdot(xyz)}{y+z}=\frac{x^2}{y+z}.$$

**Titu.**
$$\sum\frac{x^2}{y+z}\ge\frac{(x+y+z)^2}{2(x+y+z)}=\frac{x+y+z}2.$$

**AM-GM.** $x+y+z\ge3\sqrt[3]{xyz}=3$, so the left side is at least $\frac32$.

**Equality** iff $a=b=c=1$.

**Remark.** The condition $abc=1$ was used twice: to simplify $x^3yz$ and to bound $x+y+z$ from below.

**Lesson.** With $abc=1$, try $a=\frac1x$ (or $a=\frac yx$, $b=\frac zy$, $c=\frac xz$): it turns the condition into something usable and often reveals a known inequality.

## Statement

1. Prove Schur's inequality: for $a,b,c\ge0$,
$$a(a-b)(a-c)+b(b-a)(b-c)+c(c-a)(c-b)\ge0.$$
2. Deduce $(a+b+c)^3+9abc\ge4(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)$.
3. For $x,y,z\ge0$ with $x+y+z=1$, prove $0\le xy+yz+zx-2xyz\le\dfrac7{27}$.

## Hint

For 1, assume $a\ge b\ge c$ and group the first two terms. For 3, use $p=1$, $q=xy+yz+zx$, $r=xyz$ and part 2 for the upper bound.

## Solution

**1.** The expression is symmetric, so assume $a\ge b\ge c$. Then
$$a(a-b)(a-c)+b(b-a)(b-c)=(a-b)\bigl[a(a-c)-b(b-c)\bigr]=(a-b)^2(a+b-c)\ge0,$$
and $c(c-a)(c-b)=c(a-c)(b-c)\ge0$. Hence the sum is $\ge0$. Equality iff $a=b=c$, or $a=b$ and $c=0$.

**2.** Expanding, Schur's inequality reads $a^3+b^3+c^3+3abc\ge\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b$ (six terms). Meanwhile
$$(a+b+c)^3=\sum a^3+3\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b+6abc,\qquad(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)=\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b+3abc.$$
So
$$(a+b+c)^3+9abc-4(a+b+c)(ab+bc+ca)=\sum a^3-\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b+3abc\ge0.$$

**3. Upper bound.** With $p=1$, part 2 gives $1+9r\ge4q$, i.e. $q\le\frac{1+9r}4$. By AM-GM $r\le\frac1{27}$, hence
$$q-2r\le\frac{1+9r}4-2r=\frac14+\frac r4\le\frac14+\frac1{108}=\frac7{27}.$$
Equality at $x=y=z=\frac13$.

**Lower bound.** Let $z$ be the smallest of the three, so $z\le\frac13$. Then
$$xy+yz+zx-2xyz=xy(1-2z)+z(x+y)\ge0.$$

**Remark.** Part 3 is IMO 1984 problem 1.

**Lesson.** When a symmetric degree-3 inequality has equality at $a=b=c$ *and* at $(1,1,0)$, think Schur. Schur is the "missing" inequality between $\sum a^3$ and $\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b$ that AM-GM cannot give.

## Statement

Prove that for all real numbers $a,b,c$,
$$(a^2+b^2+c^2)^2\ge3\left(a^3b+b^3c+c^3a\right).$$

## Hint

Try to write $2\Bigl[(a^2+b^2+c^2)^2-3(a^3b+b^3c+c^3a)\Bigr]$ as a sum of three squares of quadratic forms obtained from each other by the cyclic shift $a\to b\to c\to a$. The first square is $\left(a^2-2ab+bc-c^2+ca\right)^2$.

## Solution

**Idea.** For cyclic inequalities, AM-GM and Cauchy-Schwarz often fail because the equality cases are strange. A sum of squares (SOS) certificate proves the inequality for *all real* numbers at once.

**Identity.** Define
$$S_1=a^2-2ab+bc-c^2+ca,\quad S_2=b^2-2bc+ca-a^2+ab,\quad S_3=c^2-2ca+ab-b^2+bc.$$
Direct expansion (which we checked symbolically) gives
$$S_1^2+S_2^2+S_3^2=2\Bigl[(a^2+b^2+c^2)^2-3(a^3b+b^3c+c^3a)\Bigr].$$

**Conclusion.** The left side is a sum of squares, hence $\ge0$, so $(a^2+b^2+c^2)^2\ge3(a^3b+b^3c+c^3a)$.

**Equality.** Iff $S_1=S_2=S_3=0$. Besides $a=b=c$, there are other equality cases of the form $\left(\sin^2\frac{4\pi}7,\sin^2\frac{2\pi}7,\sin^2\frac\pi7\right)$ (up to scaling), which is why this inequality is famous and hard to prove by standard means.

**Remark.** This is Vasile Cîrtoaje's inequality. One does not "find" the squares by luck: one looks for the certificate with computer help (semidefinite programming), and then verifies it by expansion, which is all that is needed for a proof.

**Lesson.** For a polynomial inequality valid on all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, try to write the difference as a sum of squares (SOS). For three variables and degree $4$, this often works.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c\ge0$ with $a+b+c=1$. Prove that
$$a^2b+b^2c+c^2a\le\frac4{27},$$
and find all cases of equality.

## Hint

The sum is cyclic, not symmetric: you can rotate the variables but not swap them. Among $a,b,c$, one lies between the other two; rotate so that it is $b$. Show then that $a^2b+b^2c+c^2a+abc\le b(a+c)^2$.

## Solution

**Reduction.** The expression is invariant under the rotation $a\to b\to c\to a$. Among three numbers, one is between the other two; by rotating, we may assume $b$ lies between $a$ and $c$, so $(a-b)(b-c)\ge0$.

**Key identity.** Expanding,
$$b(a+c)^2-\left(a^2b+b^2c+c^2a+abc\right)=c(a-b)(b-c)\ge0.$$
(Indeed $b(a+c)^2=a^2b+2abc+bc^2$, and the difference is $abc+bc^2-b^2c-c^2a=c(ab+bc-b^2-ac)=c(a-b)(b-c)$.)

**Conclusion.** Since $abc\ge0$,
$$a^2b+b^2c+c^2a\le b(a+c)^2=4\cdot b\cdot\frac{a+c}2\cdot\frac{a+c}2\le4\left(\frac{b+\frac{a+c}2+\frac{a+c}2}3\right)^3=4\cdot\frac1{27}=\frac4{27}.$$

**Equality.** We need $abc=0$, $c(a-b)(b-c)=0$ and $b=\frac{a+c}2$ simultaneously. $b=\frac{a+c}2$ and $b=\frac13$ (from AM-GM equality) with $abc=0$ force $a=0$ or $c=0$. If $a=0$ then $c=\frac23$ and $c(a-b)(b-c)=\frac2{27}\ne0$, impossible. So $c=0$, $b=\frac a2$ and $a+b=1$: $(a,b,c)=\left(\frac23,\frac13,0\right)$ and its cyclic rotations $\left(0,\frac23,\frac13\right)$, $\left(\frac13,0,\frac23\right)$.

**Remark.** Notice that the maximum is *not* at $a=b=c$ (where the value is $\frac19=\frac3{27}$): it is on the boundary.

**Lesson.** Cyclic inequality with an equality case on the boundary: order the variables (carefully, only using rotations) and push the expression to a two-variable one.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$. Prove that
$$\frac a{\sqrt{a^2+8bc}}+\frac b{\sqrt{b^2+8ca}}+\frac c{\sqrt{c^2+8ab}}\ge1.$$

## Hint

Denote $X_a=a^2+8bc$, etc., and $s=a+b+c$. The function $t\mapsto t^{-1/2}$ is convex. Apply Jensen with the weights $\frac as,\frac bs,\frac cs$.

## Solution

**Weighted Jensen.** For a convex $f$ and weights $w_i\ge0$ with $\sum w_i=1$: $\sum w_if(t_i)\ge f\left(\sum w_it_i\right)$. Take $f(t)=t^{-1/2}$, which is convex on $(0,\infty)$ since $f''(t)=\frac34t^{-5/2}>0$, with $w_a=\frac as$ and $t_a=X_a$ (and cyclically):
$$\sum\frac as\cdot\frac1{\sqrt{X_a}}\ge\frac1{\sqrt{\sum\frac as X_a}}.$$
Multiplying by $s$,
$$\sum\frac a{\sqrt{X_a}}\ge\frac{s}{\sqrt{\frac1s\sum aX_a}}=\frac{s^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\sum aX_a}}.$$

**It suffices to prove** $s^3\ge\sum aX_a=a^3+b^3+c^3+24abc$. Now
$$s^3=\sum a^3+3\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b+6abc,$$
so
$$s^3-\left(\sum a^3+24abc\right)=3\left(\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b-6abc\right)\ge0,$$
because $\sum_{\rm sym}a^2b=a(b^2+c^2)+b(c^2+a^2)+c(a^2+b^2)\ge a\cdot2bc+b\cdot2ca+c\cdot2ab=6abc$.

**Equality** iff $a=b=c$.

**Remark.** The official solutions use Hölder's inequality or the bound $\frac a{\sqrt{a^2+8bc}}\ge\frac{a^{4/3}}{a^{4/3}+b^{4/3}+c^{4/3}}$. The weighted-Jensen route is shorter once the weights $\frac as$ are guessed.

**Lesson.** A sum $\sum\frac a{\sqrt{X_a}}$ with the *numerator also a weight* suggests Jensen with weights proportional to the numerators.

1. Let $x,y>0$ with $x+y=5$. Find the maximum of $x^2y^3$.
2. Prove that for $a,b>0$, $2a+3b\ge5\,a^{2/5}b^{3/5}$.

## Hint

You want AM-GM on $5$ numbers whose sum is the *constraint* and whose product is the target. Split $x$ into $2$ equal parts and $y$ into $3$ equal parts, but with the right scaling so that the equality case is reachable.

## Solution

**1.** Apply AM-GM to the five numbers $\frac x2,\frac x2,\frac y3,\frac y3,\frac y3$, whose sum is $x+y=5$:
$$\left(\frac x2\right)^2\left(\frac y3\right)^3\le\left(\frac{x+y}5\right)^5=1.$$
Hence $x^2y^3\le4\cdot27=108$. Equality iff all five numbers are equal: $\frac x2=\frac y3$, i.e. $x=2$, $y=3$. The maximum is $108$.

*(Why scale by $2$ and $3$?* Without scaling, equality would require $x=y$, which is incompatible with the aim of maximizing $x^2y^3$ — the scaling is chosen so that the equality case can be met.)

**2.** AM-GM on the five numbers $a,a,b,b,b$ gives
$$\frac{2a+3b}5\ge\sqrt[5]{a^2b^3}\iff2a+3b\ge5a^{2/5}b^{3/5}.$$
In general, for $\lambda\in(0,1)$: $\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b\ge a^\lambda b^{1-\lambda}$ (weighted AM-GM, a form of the concavity of $\ln$).

**Remark.** Compare with calculus: maximizing $x^2(5-x)^3$ by differentiation gives the same point, but AM-GM provides a proof in two lines and works in many variables.

**Lesson.** To maximize a product under a sum constraint (or minimize a sum under a product constraint), split the variables into equal parts proportional to their exponents, so that equality in AM-GM is attainable.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$ with $a+b+c=1$. Find the minimum of $\dfrac1a+\dfrac4b+\dfrac9c$ and the point where it is attained.

## Hint

Multiply the sum by $a+b+c=1$ and apply Cauchy-Schwarz to the vectors $\left(\frac1{\sqrt a},\frac2{\sqrt b},\frac3{\sqrt c}\right)$ and $\left(\sqrt a,\sqrt b,\sqrt c\right)$.

## Solution

**Cauchy-Schwarz.**
$$\left(\frac1a+\frac4b+\frac9c\right)(a+b+c)\ge\left(\sqrt{\tfrac1a\cdot a}+\sqrt{\tfrac4b\cdot b}+\sqrt{\tfrac9c\cdot c}\right)^2=(1+2+3)^2=36.$$
Since $a+b+c=1$, $\frac1a+\frac4b+\frac9c\ge36$.

**Equality.** In Cauchy-Schwarz the two vectors are proportional: $\frac1{\sqrt a}:\sqrt a=\frac2{\sqrt b}:\sqrt b=\frac3{\sqrt c}:\sqrt c$, i.e. $a:b:c=1:2:3$. With $a+b+c=1$: $(a,b,c)=\left(\frac16,\frac13,\frac12\right)$, and indeed $6+12+18=36$.

**Remark.** This is the Engel form: $\sum\frac{x_i^2}{y_i}\ge\frac{(\sum x_i)^2}{\sum y_i}$ with $x=(1,2,3)$ and $y=(a,b,c)$.

**Lesson.** To find an extremum with Cauchy-Schwarz, *first* guess the equality case ($a:b:c$), which tells you how to choose the vectors.
