## Statement

Let $a,b$ be positive integers. Prove that
$$\gcd(2^a-1,\,2^b-1)=2^{\gcd(a,b)}-1.$$
Deduce that $2^a-1$ divides $2^b-1$ if and only if $a$ divides $b$.

## Hint

Suppose $a>b$. Write $2^a-1=2^{a-b}(2^b-1)+(2^{a-b}-1)$ and compare with the Euclidean algorithm.

## Solution

**Idea.** The Euclidean algorithm on the exponents is mirrored by the Euclidean algorithm on the numbers $2^k-1$.

**Key step.** For $a>b$,
$$2^a-1=2^{a-b}\bigl(2^b-1\bigr)+\bigl(2^{a-b}-1\bigr),$$
so $\gcd(2^a-1,2^b-1)=\gcd(2^b-1,2^{a-b}-1)$, because $\gcd(u,v)=\gcd(v,u-qv)$ for any integer $q$. Thus the pair of exponents $(a,b)$ is replaced by $(b,a-b)$.

**Conclusion.** Repeating the subtraction step, the exponents follow the subtractive Euclidean algorithm and end at $(g,g)$ with $g=\gcd(a,b)$ (or at $(g,0)$ if one uses $\gcd(u,0)=u$, where $2^0-1=0$). Hence the gcd equals $2^g-1$.

**Divisibility.** $2^a-1\mid2^b-1\iff\gcd(2^a-1,2^b-1)=2^a-1\iff2^{\gcd(a,b)}-1=2^a-1\iff\gcd(a,b)=a\iff a\mid b$.

**Check.** $\gcd(2^{12}-1,2^{18}-1)=\gcd(4095,262143)=63=2^6-1$ ✓.

**Lesson.** When a gcd involves a parametrised expression, show that the Euclidean algorithm on the parameter reproduces the Euclidean algorithm on the values.

## Statement

Find all positive integers $n$ such that $n$ divides $2^n-1$.

## Hint

If $n>1$, look at the smallest prime divisor $p$ of $n$ and at the order of $2$ modulo $p$.

## Solution

**Answer.** Only $n=1$.

**Idea.** The order of $2$ modulo the smallest prime factor of $n$ must be a divisor of $n$ smaller than that prime.

**Proof.** Let $n>1$ with $n\mid2^n-1$ and let $p$ be the smallest prime divisor of $n$. Since $2^n-1$ is odd, $p$ is odd. Let $d$ be the order of $2$ modulo $p$, i.e. the least $d\ge1$ with $2^d\equiv1\pmod p$.

- From $2^n\equiv1\pmod p$ we get $d\mid n$.
- From Fermat's little theorem $2^{p-1}\equiv1$ we get $d\mid p-1$.

So $d$ divides both $n$ and $p-1$. Now $d\ne1$ because $2\not\equiv1\pmod p$. Hence $d>1$ has a prime divisor $q$ with $q\le d\le p-1<p$, and $q\mid d\mid n$. This contradicts the minimality of $p$.

**Check.** $n=1$ works trivially; for instance $n=3$ fails since $2^3-1=7$.

**Remark.** The same argument shows that $n\mid2^n-1$ cannot hold for $n>1$, but $n\mid2^n+1$ has infinitely many solutions (e.g. $n=3,9,27,\dots$).

**Lesson.** "Take the smallest prime divisor and use orders" is the standard way to show that a divisibility $n\mid a^n-1$ is impossible.

## Statement

Prove that the only integer solution of $x^2+y^2=3z^2$ is $x=y=z=0$.

## Hint

Squares modulo $3$ are $0$ or $1$. What does the equation say modulo $3$?

## Solution

**Idea.** Descent: from a non-zero solution build a strictly smaller one.

**Proof.** Suppose there is a solution other than $(0,0,0)$, and choose one with $|z|$ smallest positive (if $z=0$ then $x^2+y^2=0$ forces $x=y=0$).

**Modulo $3$.** $x^2+y^2\equiv0\pmod3$. Since squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $3$, the sum is $0$ only if both are $0$, so $3\mid x$ and $3\mid y$. Write $x=3x_1$, $y=3y_1$:
$$9(x_1^2+y_1^2)=3z^2\ \Longrightarrow\ z^2=3(x_1^2+y_1^2),$$
so $3\mid z$; write $z=3z_1$. Then $9z_1^2=3(x_1^2+y_1^2)$, i.e.
$$x_1^2+y_1^2=3z_1^2,$$
a solution with $|z_1|=|z|/3<|z|$ and $z_1\ne0$. Contradiction with minimality.

**Lesson.** Infinite descent: reduce modulo a well-chosen number, show all variables are divisible by it, divide, and get a smaller solution.

## Statement

Let $x,y,z$ be positive integers with $x^2+y^2=z^2$. Prove that $xyz$ is divisible by $60$.

## Hint

Since $60=3\cdot4\cdot5$, prove separately that $3$, $4$ and $5$ divide $xyz$, looking at the possible residues of squares modulo $3$, $8$ and $5$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Squares have very few residues, so each prime power can be handled by a short case check.

**Modulo $3$.** Squares are $0$ or $1$. If $3\nmid x$ and $3\nmid y$, then $z^2\equiv1+1=2\pmod3$, impossible. So $3\mid xy$.

**Modulo $5$.** Squares are $0,1,4\equiv-1$. If $5\nmid xyz$, then $x^2,y^2,z^2\in\{\pm1\}$ and $x^2+y^2\in\{-2,0,2\}$ is never $\equiv\pm1$. So $5\mid xyz$.

**Divisibility by $4$.** Squares modulo $4$ are $0,1$, so $x,y$ are not both odd (else $z^2\equiv2$).
- If $x,y$ are both even, then $4\mid xy$.
- Otherwise exactly one is even, say $x$, and $y,z$ are odd. Odd squares are $\equiv1\pmod8$, so $x^2=z^2-y^2\equiv0\pmod8$. Writing $x=2m$, $4m^2\equiv0\pmod8$ forces $m$ even, so $4\mid x$.

**Conclusion.** $3$, $4$ and $5$ are pairwise coprime and each divides $xyz$, so $60\mid xyz$.

**Check.** $(3,4,5)$: $60$. $(5,12,13)$: $780=13\cdot60$. $(8,15,17)$: $2040=34\cdot60$ ✓.

**Lesson.** Reduce to the list of squares modulo $3$, $4$ (or $8$) and $5$: most "which numbers divide..." questions about squares are closed by such a check.

## Statement

Prove that there are infinitely many prime numbers congruent to $3$ modulo $4$.

## Hint

Imitate Euclid. Assume there are finitely many, $p_1,\dots,p_r$ (including $3$), and consider $N=4p_1p_2\cdots p_r-1$.

## Solution

**Idea.** An integer $\equiv3\pmod4$ cannot be a product of primes all $\equiv1\pmod4$.

**Proof.** Suppose the primes $\equiv3\pmod4$ are exactly $p_1,\dots,p_r$, and let $N=4p_1\cdots p_r-1$. Then $N\ge11$, $N$ is odd, and $N\equiv3\pmod4$.

- If all prime factors of $N$ were $\equiv1\pmod4$, their product $N$ would be $\equiv1\pmod4$. Contradiction.
- So $N$ has a prime factor $q\equiv3\pmod4$, hence $q=p_i$ for some $i$.

But then $p_i$ divides both $N$ and $4p_1\cdots p_r$, hence divides their difference $1$. Contradiction.

**Remark.** The same trick fails for primes $\equiv1\pmod4$ (a product of primes $\equiv3$ can be $\equiv1$); a different argument (using $x^2+1$) is needed there. Dirichlet's theorem handles all progressions $a\bmod m$ with $\gcd(a,m)=1$.

**Lesson.** Euclid's construction works whenever one can arrange the residue of the auxiliary number so that some prime factor must lie in the class under study.

## Statement

1. Prove that for a prime $p$, $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$.
2. Prove that an integer $n>1$ is prime if and only if $n\mid(n-1)!+1$.

## Hint

In $\{2,\dots,p-2\}$ pair each number with its inverse modulo $p$. Which numbers are their own inverse?

## Solution

**1.** For $p=2,3$ the claim is checked directly ($1!=1\equiv-1\pmod2$, $2!=2\equiv-1\pmod3$). Let $p\ge5$. Each $a\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}$ has a unique inverse $a^{-1}$ in $\{1,\dots,p-1\}$ modulo $p$. We have $a=a^{-1}$ iff $a^2\equiv1$ iff $p\mid(a-1)(a+1)$ iff $a\equiv\pm1$, i.e. $a\in\{1,p-1\}$.

Hence the $p-3$ numbers $2,3,\dots,p-2$ split into pairs $\{a,a^{-1}\}$ with product $\equiv1$, so $2\cdot3\cdots(p-2)\equiv1$, and
$$(p-1)!\equiv1\cdot1\cdot(p-1)\equiv-1\pmod p.$$

**2.** ($\Rightarrow$) is part 1. ($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $n$ is composite, with a divisor $d$ such that $1<d<n$. Then $d\mid(n-1)!$, so $d\nmid(n-1)!+1$, and therefore $n\nmid(n-1)!+1$. Hence $n\mid(n-1)!+1$ forces $n$ prime.

**Remark.** Wilson's criterion is a perfect primality test in theory, but useless in practice because $(n-1)!$ is huge.

**Lesson.** In a group of residues, pair every element with its inverse: only the elements with $a^2=1$ survive.

title: Vieta jumping: (a² + b²)/(ab + 1) is a square
category: number

difficulty: 5
tags: Vieta jumping, descent, Diophantine equations, extremal principle

## Statement

Let $a,b$ be positive integers such that $k=\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{ab+1}$ is an integer. Prove that $k$ is a perfect square.

## Hint

Fix $k$ and look at the equation $a^2+b^2=k(ab+1)$ as a quadratic in $a$. If $(a,b)$ is a solution, so is the pair formed with the other root. Then take a solution with $a+b$ minimal.

## Solution

**Idea.** Assume $k$ is not a perfect square. Among all pairs of positive integers $(a,b)$ with $a^2+b^2=k(ab+1)$ pick one with $a+b$ minimal, and with $a\ge b$. We build a smaller pair.

**Case $a=b$.** Then $2a^2=k(a^2+1)$, so $k=\frac{2a^2}{a^2+1}<2$. As $k$ is a positive integer, $k=1$, a perfect square. So assume $a>b$.

**The jump.** Regard the equation as $x^2-kb\,x+(b^2-k)=0$ in the unknown $x$. It has the root $x=a$; by Vieta the other root is
$$a'=kb-a=\frac{b^2-k}{a},$$
an integer.

- $a'>0$: if $a'<0$ then $a'\le-1$, and from $a'^2+kb|a'|+b^2-k=0$ (the equation with $x=a'$) together with $kb|a'|\ge k$ we get $a'^2+b^2\le0$, impossible. If $a'=0$ then $b^2=k$ would be a square, excluded. So $a'\ge1$.
- $a'<b$: indeed $a'=\dfrac{b^2-k}a<\dfrac{b^2}a\le b$ since $a\ge b$.

Thus $(a',b)$ is another pair of positive integers satisfying the equation, with $a'+b<a+b$. This contradicts the minimality of $a+b$.

**Conclusion.** $k$ must be a perfect square.

**Check.** $(a,b)=(8,2)$: $\frac{64+4}{17}=4$; $(27,3)$: $\frac{738}{82}=9$; $(30,8)$: $\frac{964}{241}=4$ ✓.

**Remark.** This is IMO 1988 Problem 6, the most famous Vieta jumping problem.

**Lesson.** Vieta jumping: view the equation as a quadratic in one variable, use the second root to produce a smaller solution, and conclude by minimality.

## Statement

Let $v_3(m)$ denote the exponent of $3$ in the prime factorisation of $m$. Prove that for every $n\ge0$,
$$v_3\left(2^{3^n}+1\right)=n+1.$$

## Hint

Put $x_n=2^{3^n}$, so $x_{n+1}=x_n^3$, and factor $x^3+1=(x+1)(x^2-x+1)$. What is $x^2-x+1$ modulo $9$ when $x\equiv-1\pmod3$?

## Solution

**Idea.** Each cube multiplies the number $x+1$ by a factor having valuation exactly $1$.

**Lemma.** If $x\equiv-1\pmod3$, then $v_3(x^2-x+1)=1$. Indeed write $x=3t-1$: then
$$x^2-x+1=9t^2-6t+1-3t+1+1=9t^2-9t+3\equiv3\pmod9,$$
so $3\mid x^2-x+1$ but $9\nmid x^2-x+1$.

**Induction.** Let $x_n=2^{3^n}$. As $3^n$ is odd, $x_n\equiv(-1)^{3^n}=-1\pmod3$, so the lemma applies to $x_n$.
- $n=0$: $x_0+1=3$, so $v_3=1$.
- Step: $x_{n+1}+1=x_n^3+1=(x_n+1)(x_n^2-x_n+1)$, so
$$v_3(x_{n+1}+1)=v_3(x_n+1)+1.$$

Hence $v_3(x_n+1)=n+1$.

**Check.** $2^3+1=9$ ($v_3=2$), $2^9+1=513=27\cdot19$ ($v_3=3$), $2^{27}+1=134217729=81\cdot1657009$ ($v_3=4$).

**Remark.** This is the special case of the *Lifting The Exponent lemma*: for an odd prime $p$ with $p\mid a-b$, $p\nmid ab$: $v_p(a^n-b^n)=v_p(a-b)+v_p(n)$.

**Lesson.** To compute an exact $p$-adic valuation, factor with the cyclotomic identity ($x^p-y^p$ or $x^p+y^p$) and track how the valuation grows at each step.

## Statement

Recall Legendre's formula: $v_p(n!)=\sum_{k\ge1}\left\lfloor\frac n{p^k}\right\rfloor$.

1. How many zeros are at the end of the decimal expansion of $2024!$?
2. Prove that for all non-negative integers $m,n$, the number
$$\frac{(2m)!\,(2n)!}{m!\,n!\,(m+n)!}$$
is an integer.

## Hint

For part 2, prove $\lfloor2x\rfloor+\lfloor2y\rfloor\ge\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor+\lfloor x+y\rfloor$ for real $x,y$.

## Solution

**1.** The number of trailing zeros is $\min(v_2,v_5)$ of $2024!$, and $v_5\le v_2$ because $\lfloor 2024/5^k\rfloor\le\lfloor 2024/2^k\rfloor$ for every $k$. So
$$v_5(2024!)=\left\lfloor\frac{2024}5\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{2024}{25}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{2024}{125}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{2024}{625}\right\rfloor=404+80+16+3=503.$$

**2. Floor inequality.** Write $x=a+\alpha$, $y=b+\beta$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$, $\alpha,\beta\in[0,1)$. The inequality becomes
$$\lfloor2\alpha\rfloor+\lfloor2\beta\rfloor\ge\lfloor\alpha+\beta\rfloor.$$
If $\alpha+\beta<1$ the right side is $0$. If $\alpha+\beta\ge1$ then one of $\alpha,\beta$ is at least $\frac12$, so the left side is at least $1$, and the right side is exactly $1$ (since $\alpha+\beta<2$).

**Integrality.** For each prime $p$, by Legendre's formula,
$$v_p(\text{expression})=\sum_{k\ge1}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2m}{p^k}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{2n}{p^k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac m{p^k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac n{p^k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{m+n}{p^k}\right\rfloor\right)\ge0$$
by the inequality with $x=\frac m{p^k}$, $y=\frac n{p^k}$. All the valuations are non-negative, so the number is an integer.

**Check.** $(m,n)=(1,1)$: $\frac{2\cdot2}{1\cdot1\cdot2}=2$; $(2,3)$: $\frac{24\cdot720}{2\cdot6\cdot120}=12$ ✓.

**Remark.** These are the "super Catalan numbers". The case $m=n$ gives $\binom{2n}n$.

**Lesson.** To prove that a quotient of factorials is an integer, compare prime by prime using Legendre's formula, and reduce to an elementary inequality for floor functions.

## Statement

Prove that the only integer solution of $x^3+2y^3+4z^3=0$ is $(0,0,0)$. Deduce that $\sqrt[3]2$ is irrational.

## Hint

Show that $x$, then $y$, then $z$ must all be even.

## Solution

**Descent.** Suppose a non-zero solution exists, and choose one with $|x|+|y|+|z|$ minimal.

- $x^3=-2y^3-4z^3$ is even, so $x$ is even: $x=2x_1$. Then $8x_1^3+2y^3+4z^3=0$, i.e. $4x_1^3+y^3+2z^3=0$.
- So $y^3$ is even, $y=2y_1$: $4x_1^3+8y_1^3+2z^3=0$, i.e. $2x_1^3+4y_1^3+z^3=0$.
- So $z$ is even, $z=2z_1$: $2x_1^3+4y_1^3+8z_1^3=0$, i.e. $x_1^3+2y_1^3+4z_1^3=0$.

Thus $(x_1,y_1,z_1)=(x/2,y/2,z/2)$ is a smaller non-zero solution, contradiction.

**Irrationality.** If $\sqrt[3]2=\frac pq$ with $q\ne0$, then $p^3=2q^3$, i.e. $p^3-2q^3=0$. Setting $x=p$, $y=-q$, $z=0$ gives $x^3+2y^3+4z^3=p^3-2q^3=0$ with $(x,y,z)\ne0$, contradiction.

**Remark.** The same argument proves that $x^3+2y^3+4z^3=0$ has no non-trivial *rational* solutions, which is the statement that $1,\sqrt[3]2,\sqrt[3]4$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Lesson.** Descent by parity: when all variables are forced to be even, divide by $2$ and obtain a smaller solution.

## Statement

1. Let $p$ be a prime with $p\equiv3\pmod4$. Prove that if $p\mid a^2+b^2$ then $p\mid a$ and $p\mid b$.
2. Deduce that if $n=a^2+b^2$, every prime $p\equiv3\pmod4$ divides $n$ to an even power.
3. Show that $n\cdot m$ is a sum of two squares whenever $n$ and $m$ are.

## Hint

For part 1, if $p\nmid b$ use the inverse of $b$ to get $x^2\equiv-1\pmod p$, then apply Fermat's little theorem to $x^{p-1}$.

## Solution

**1.** Suppose $p\mid a^2+b^2$ and $p\nmid b$. Let $x\equiv ab^{-1}\pmod p$. Then $x^2\equiv-1\pmod p$ and $x\not\equiv0$. By Fermat's little theorem,
$$1\equiv x^{p-1}=(x^2)^{(p-1)/2}\equiv(-1)^{(p-1)/2}\pmod p.$$
Since $p\equiv3\pmod4$, $\frac{p-1}2$ is odd, so the right side is $-1$. So $1\equiv-1$, i.e. $p=2$, contradiction. Hence $p\mid b$, and then $p\mid a^2$, so $p\mid a$.

**2.** By strong induction on $n$. Let $n=a^2+b^2$ and $p\equiv3\pmod4$ with $p\mid n$. By part 1, $p\mid a,b$, so $n=p^2(a_1^2+b_1^2)$ where $a=pa_1$, $b=pb_1$, and $n/p^2$ is again a sum of two squares, smaller than $n$. By induction, the exponent of $p$ in $n/p^2$ is even, so the exponent in $n$ is even too. (If $p\nmid n$ the exponent is $0$.)

**3.** Brahmagupta's identity:
$$(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)=(ac-bd)^2+(ad+bc)^2.$$

**Examples.** $21=3\cdot7$ and $2021=43\cdot47$ are not sums of two squares (primes $\equiv3\pmod4$ with odd exponent); $45=9\cdot5=6^2+3^2$ is.

**Lesson.** To forbid a representation, find a prime for which the representation forces divisibility of both terms, then descend.

$$\left\lfloor\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\sqrt{4n+2}\right\rfloor.$$

## Hint

## Solution

**Idea.** The two numbers are very close; an integer between them would be a square near $4n+2$, and $4n+2$ is never a square.

**Upper comparison.** $(\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1})^2=2n+1+2\sqrt{n(n+1)}$, and $n(n+1)<\left(n+\frac12\right)^2$, so $2\sqrt{n(n+1)}<2n+1$ and
$$(\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1})^2<4n+2.$$

**Lower comparison.** $n(n+1)>n^2$ so $2\sqrt{n(n+1)}>2n$ and
$$(\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1})^2>4n+1.$$

**No integer in between.** Suppose an integer $k$ satisfies $\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1}<k\le\sqrt{4n+2}$. Squaring, $4n+1<k^2\le4n+2$, so $k^2=4n+2$. But squares are $\equiv0,1\pmod4$ while $4n+2\equiv2$. Contradiction.

**Conclusion.** The numbers $\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1}\le\sqrt{4n+2}$ have no integer in $(\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1},\sqrt{4n+2}]$, so their floors coincide.

**Check.** $n=1$: $1+1.414=2.414$, $\sqrt6=2.449$, both floors $2$.

## Statement

Prove that no integer of the form $8k+7$ is a sum of three squares of integers.

## Hint

What are the possible values of a square modulo $8$?

## Solution

**Squares modulo 8.** For $x$ odd, $x^2\equiv1\pmod8$; for $x\equiv2,6\pmod8$, $x^2\equiv4$; for $x\equiv0,4\pmod8$, $x^2\equiv0$. So squares are $0,1,4$ modulo $8$.

**Sums of three.** The sums of three elements of $\{0,1,4\}$ are:
$$0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 8\equiv0,\ 9\equiv1,\ 12\equiv4.$$
Precisely, the possible sums are the residues $0,1,2,3,4,5,6$ modulo $8$. The residue $7$ is never obtained (for instance $4+1+1=6$, $4+4+1=9\equiv1$, $1+1+1=3$).

**Conclusion.** No sum of three squares is $\equiv7\pmod8$.

**Remark.** Legendre's three-square theorem states the converse: a positive integer is a sum of three squares iff it is not of the form $4^a(8b+7)$. For example $15$, $23$, $28=4\cdot7$ are not sums of three squares.

**Lesson.** List the residues of squares modulo $8$ ($0,1,4$) and check which sums are attainable: the standard way to rule out representations.
