## Statement

At a party with $n\ge2$ people, friendship is mutual. Prove that two people have the same number of friends at the party.

## Hint

The number of friends of a person is an integer between $0$ and $n-1$. That gives $n$ possible values for $n$ people, which is one too many to conclude directly. Can the values $0$ and $n-1$ both occur?

## Solution

**Idea.** Count the possible values and show that there are really only $n-1$ of them.

**Values.** Each person has between $0$ and $n-1$ friends. Suppose some person $A$ has $0$ friends and some person $B$ has $n-1$ friends. Then $B$ is a friend of everybody, in particular of $A$, so $A$ has at least one friend: contradiction. Hence the values $0$ and $n-1$ do not occur together.

**Pigeonhole.**
- If nobody has $0$ friends, all the numbers of friends lie in $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$.
- If somebody has $0$ friends, then nobody has $n-1$ friends, and all the numbers lie in $\{0,\dots,n-2\}$.

In both cases the $n$ people take at most $n-1$ different values, so two people have the same number of friends.

**Remark.** The statement is false if we allow a person to be their own friend or friendships to be one-way. The mutuality is used exactly once, in the key step.

**Lesson.** Pigeonhole: count the boxes carefully, and look for a hidden constraint that removes one box.

## Statement

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n$ be integers. Prove that there exist $i\le j$ such that the sum $a_i+a_{i+1}+\dots+a_j$ is divisible by $n$.

## Hint

Consider the $n+1$ partial sums $S_0=0$, $S_1=a_1$, $S_2=a_1+a_2$, $\dots$, $S_n$ and their remainders modulo $n$.

## Solution

**Idea.** A block sum is a difference of two partial sums.

**Pigeonhole.** The $n+1$ numbers $S_0,S_1,\dots,S_n$ have only $n$ possible remainders modulo $n$, so two of them, say $S_p$ and $S_q$ with $p<q$, have the same remainder. Then $n$ divides
$$S_q-S_p=a_{p+1}+a_{p+2}+\dots+a_q.$$
Taking $i=p+1$ and $j=q$ gives the required block.

**Remark.** The result is sharp: for $n-1$ numbers it fails. Take $a_1=\dots=a_{n-1}=1$.

**Lesson.** Turn sums of blocks into differences of prefix sums, then apply the pigeonhole principle to remainders.

## Statement

Let $n\ge1$. Prove that every sequence of $n^2+1$ distinct real numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length $n+1$ or a decreasing subsequence of length $n+1$.

## Hint

To each term $a_i$ attach the pair $(x_i,y_i)$, where $x_i$ is the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending at $a_i$ and $y_i$ the length of the longest decreasing subsequence ending at $a_i$. Show that these pairs are pairwise distinct.

## Solution

**Idea.** Label every term by two numbers, and show that the labels never repeat.

**Distinct labels.** Let $i<j$.
- If $a_i<a_j$, any increasing subsequence ending at $a_i$ can be extended by $a_j$, so $x_j\ge x_i+1$.
- If $a_i>a_j$, any decreasing subsequence ending at $a_i$ can be extended by $a_j$, so $y_j\ge y_i+1$.

In both cases $(x_i,y_i)\ne(x_j,y_j)$.

**Counting.** Suppose, for contradiction, that all increasing and decreasing subsequences have length at most $n$. Then $1\le x_i,y_i\le n$ for all $i$, so there are at most $n^2$ possible pairs. But we have $n^2+1$ terms with pairwise distinct pairs: contradiction.

**Remark.** The bound $n^2+1$ is sharp.

**Lesson.** When pigeonhole directly fails, build a cleverly chosen label for each object so that distinct objects get distinct labels.

## Statement

Let $n\ge1$ and let $S$ be a set of $n+1$ distinct integers chosen from $\{1,2,\dots,2n\}$. Prove that $S$ contains two numbers such that one divides the other. Show that the statement fails for sets of $n$ numbers.

## Hint

Write every integer as $2^k\cdot m$ with $m$ odd. How many possible values of $m$ are there?

## Solution

**Idea.** Sort the numbers into boxes according to their odd part.

**Boxes.** Every $a\in\{1,\dots,2n\}$ can be written uniquely as $a=2^km$ with $k\ge0$ and $m$ odd. The odd part $m$ is one of the $n$ odd numbers $1,3,\dots,2n-1$.

**Pigeonhole.** The $n+1$ chosen numbers go into $n$ boxes, so two of them, $a=2^km$ and $b=2^lm$ with $a\ne b$, share the same odd part $m$. Then $k\ne l$, and if $k<l$ then $a$ divides $b$; otherwise $b$ divides $a$.

**Sharpness.** The $n$ numbers $n+1,n+2,\dots,2n$ contain no pair where one divides the other.

**Lesson.** Decompose objects into a canonical form and use one component as the pigeonhole box.

## Statement

The numbers $1,2,\dots,10$ are written on a board. A move consists of erasing two numbers $a$ and $b$ and writing $|a-b|$ instead. After nine moves, a single number remains. Prove that it is odd.

## Hint

How does the parity of the sum of all numbers on the board change after a move?

## Solution

**Idea.** Find a quantity that a move cannot change, at least modulo $2$.

**Invariant.** A move replaces the two numbers $a,b$ by $|a-b|$, so the sum $T$ of the numbers on the board changes by
$$-(a+b)+|a-b|=-2\min(a,b),$$
which is even. Hence the parity of $T$ never changes.

**Conclusion.** At the start $T=1+2+\dots+10=55$, which is odd. After nine moves, $T$ is the single remaining number, and it is odd.

**Remark.** The same argument works for $1,\dots,n$: the final number has the parity of $\frac{n(n+1)}2$.

**Lesson.** Invariant: find a quantity (often a parity) that every allowed move preserves.

## Statement

In a parliament, each member has at most $3$ enemies, and enmity is mutual. Prove that the members can be split into two houses so that every member has at most $1$ enemy in his own house.

## Hint

Start with an arbitrary split. If somebody has at least $2$ enemies in his own house, move him to the other house. Consider the number of pairs of enemies who sit in the same house.

## Solution

**Idea.** Use a monovariant: a nonnegative integer that strictly decreases at each step.

**Process.** Start with any split of the members into two houses. Let $E$ be the number of pairs of enemies belonging to the same house. If every member has at most one enemy in his own house, we are done. Otherwise, take a member $x$ with at least $2$ enemies in his own house.

**Effect of the move.** Since $x$ has at most $3$ enemies in total, he has at most $1$ enemy in the other house. Move $x$ to the other house.
- The pairs $\{x,y\}$ with $y$ an enemy in his former house are no longer in the same house: at least $2$ pairs are removed from the count.
- The pairs $\{x,y\}$ with $y$ an enemy in the other house now count: at most $1$ new pair.
- All other pairs are unaffected.

Hence $E$ decreases by at least $1$.

**Termination.** $E$ is a nonnegative integer, so the process cannot continue forever. When it stops, nobody has $2$ or more enemies in his own house.

**Lesson.** Monovariant: to prove a configuration with some property exists, start anywhere and improve a nonnegative integer quantity until it cannot be improved.

## Statement

Prove that there is no finite non-empty set $S$ of points in the plane such that every point of $S$ is the midpoint of two other points of $S$.

## Hint

Look at the point of $S$ that is largest in some sense: for instance, largest abscissa, and among those the largest ordinate.

## Solution

**Idea.** An extreme point can never be the midpoint of two other points.

**Extreme point.** Choose the point $P=(p,q)$ of $S$ with the largest abscissa $p$, and, among the points with abscissa $p$, the largest ordinate $q$.

**Contradiction.** Suppose $P$ is the midpoint of two other points $A=(a_1,a_2)$ and $B=(b_1,b_2)$ of $S$. Then $\frac{a_1+b_1}2=p$ with $a_1\le p$ and $b_1\le p$, so $a_1=b_1=p$. **Remark.** For an infinite set the statement is false: $\mathbb{Z}^2$ has this property.

**Lesson.** Extremal principle: look at a point that maximises or minimises some quantity.

title: Triangle-free graphs have at most n²/4 edges (Mantel)
category: combi/graphs
level: olympiad
difficulty: 4
tags: double counting, graphs, Cauchy–Schwarz, extremal graph theory
date: 2026-11-04

## Statement

A graph on $n$ vertices has no triangle. Prove that it has at most $\dfrac{n^2}4$ edges. When is equality possible?

## Hint

If $u$ and $v$ are adjacent, can they have a common neighbour? Deduce a bound on $d(u)+d(v)$, then sum over all edges.

## Solution

**Idea.** Count the pairs (vertex, edge containing it) in two ways.

**Local bound.** Let $uv$ be an edge. If a vertex $w$ were adjacent to both $u$ and $v$, then $uvw$ would be a triangle. So the neighbourhoods of $u$ and $v$ are disjoint, and
$$d(u)+d(v)\le n.$$

**Summation.** Let $e$ be the number of edges. Summing over all edges $uv$,
$$\sum_{uv\in E}\bigl(d(u)+d(v)\bigr)\le ne.$$
On the left, each vertex $v$ appears $d(v)$ times, each with the term $d(v)$. So the left side equals $\sum_vd(v)^2$:
$$\sum_vd(v)^2\le ne.$$

**Cauchy–Schwarz.** Since $\sum_vd(v)=2e$,
$$\sum_vd(v)^2\ge\frac{\left(\sum_vd(v)\right)^2}n=\frac{4e^2}n.$$

**Conclusion.** $\dfrac{4e^2}n\le ne$, so $e\le\dfrac{n^2}4$.

**Equality.** For $n$ even, the complete bipartite graph $K_{n/2,n/2}$ is triangle-free with $\frac{n^2}4$ edges. For $n$ odd, the bound is $\left\lfloor\frac{n^2}4\right\rfloor$ and is attained by $K_{(n-1)/2,(n+1)/2}$.

**Lesson.** Double counting: when a sum over edges involves degrees, rewrite it as a sum over vertices; combine with Cauchy–Schwarz.

2. Prove that if a tree has a vertex of degree $k$, then it has at least $k$ leaves.

$$2(n-1)=\sum_u\deg u\ge L+k+2(n-L-1).$$
Simplifying the right side: $2n-L-2+k$. Therefore $2n-2\ge2n-L-2+k$, i.e. $L\ge k$.

**Equality.** It holds when all vertices other than $v$ and the leaves have degree exactly $2$, that is when the tree is a "spider": paths glued at $v$.

## Statement

Two players take turns placing identical round coins on a circular table. Each coin must lie completely on the table and must not overlap the coins already placed (touching is allowed). The player who cannot place a coin loses. (A coin fits on the table.) Prove that the first player has a winning strategy.

## Hint

Use the centre of the table. The first player should have a reply to every possible move of the second one.

## Solution

**Idea.** A symmetry strategy: after the first move, always answer by the symmetric move.

**Strategy.** Let $O$ be the centre of the table and $r$ the radius of a coin. The first player puts his first coin with its centre at $O$. Afterwards, each time the opponent places a coin with centre $P$, the first player places a coin with centre $P'$, the reflection of $P$ in $O$.

**Invariant.** Just before each move of the second player, the position is symmetric with respect to $O$.

**The reply is legal.** Let the second player place a coin at $P$ (legal, so $P\ne O$).
- *On the table:* the table is symmetric about $O$, so the coin at $P'$ lies completely on the table.
- *No overlap with the coin at $P$:* two coins overlap iff their centres are at distance less than $2r$. Here $PP'=2\,OP\ge2r$ because the move at $P$ was legal and $O$ is occupied.
- *No overlap with the other coins:* by symmetry, if the coin at $P'$ overlapped some coin $C$, then the coin at $P$ would overlap the reflection of $C$: impossible.

**Conclusion.** The first player always has a legal reply. Since the table has finite area, the game ends after finitely many moves, and the player who cannot move is the second one.

**Lesson.** Symmetry strategy: if the board has a central symmetry, occupy the centre and mirror every move of the opponent.

## Statement

1. A pile contains $n$ stones. Two players alternately remove between $1$ and $5$ stones. The player who takes the last stone wins. For which $n$ does the first player have a winning strategy?
2. There are two piles with the same number $m\ge1$ of stones. Two players alternately remove any positive number of stones from one pile of their choice. The player who takes the last stone wins. Who wins?

For part 1, find the positions that are lost for the player to move, starting from small $n$. For part 2, think of a pairing strategy.

## Solution

**1. Winning positions.** Positions $n=1,\dots,5$ are winning for the player to move. The position $n=6$ is losing. We claim the first player loses if and only if $6\mid n$.

- *If $6\mid n$:* the second player answers each move "remove $k$" by "remove $6-k$", which is legal. After each of his moves the number of stones is a multiple of $6$. The first player can never take the last stone.
- *If $6\nmid n$:* the first player removes $r=n\bmod6\in\{1,\dots,5\}$ stones, leaving a multiple of $6$ and then plays the second player's strategy above.

**Answer 1.** The first player wins iff $n$ is not a multiple of $6$.

**2. Pairing.** The second player wins. After each move of the first player, he removes the same number of stones from the other pile. Then the piles are equal after each of his moves. The first player can never take the last stone; the second player finishes by taking the last stones of the second pile.

**Remark.** These are the simplest cases of impartial game theory (Sprague–Grundy); the general sum-of-piles result is Bouton's solution of Nim.

**Lesson.** Compute the losing positions from the end of the game, then find a complement strategy.

## Hint

For part 1, split according to whether $n$ belongs to the subset. For part 2, subtract $i-1$ from the $i$-th smallest element.

## Solution

**1. Recurrence.** Take $n\ge2$ and split the admissible subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ into two classes.
- Those not containing $n$: they are exactly the admissible subsets of $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$, so there are $a_{n-1}$.
- Those containing $n$: they do not contain $n-1$, and removing $n$ leaves an admissible subset of $\{1,\dots,n-2\}$; conversely adding $n$ to such a subset is admissible. So there are $a_{n-2}$.

Hence $a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}$. The initial values are $a_0=1$ and $a_1=2$, which are $F_2$ and $F_3$. By induction $a_n=F_{n+2}$.

**2. Bijection.** Let $x_1<x_2<\dots<x_k$ be an admissible subset, so $x_{i+1}\ge x_i+2$. Define $y_i=x_i-(i-1)$. Then $y_{i+1}-y_i\ge1$, so $y_1<\dots<y_k$, and $1\le y_1$, $y_k\le n-k+1$. Conversely, from any $k$-subset $y_1<\dots<y_k$ of $\{1,\dots,n-k+1\}$, setting $x_i=y_i+(i-1)$ gives an admissible subset. These maps are inverse, so the count is $\binom{n-k+1}k$.

**Check.** Summing over $k$ gives $\sum_k\binom{n-k+1}k=F_{n+2}$.

**Lesson.** Recurrence by conditioning on the last element, and bijections that "squeeze out" a constraint.

## Statement

A derangement of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ is a permutation with no fixed point. Let $D_n$ be their number.

1. Prove that $D_n=n!\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$.
2. Compute $D_4$, and show that for $n\ge2$, $D_n$ is the integer closest to $\dfrac{n!}e$.

## Hint

Let $A_i$ be the set of permutations fixing $i$. Apply inclusion–exclusion to $A_1\cup\dots\cup A_n$.

## Solution

**1. Inclusion–exclusion.** $D_n=n!-\left|A_1\cup\dots\cup A_n\right|$. For a set $I$ of $k$ indices, the permutations fixing every element of $I$ number $(n-k)!$. There are $\binom nk$ such sets. Therefore
$$D_n=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nk(n-k)!=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\frac{n!}{k!}=n!\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}.$$

**2. Values.** $D_4=24-24+12-4+1=9$.

**Closest integer.** Since $e^{-1}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$,
$$\frac{n!}e-D_n=n!\sum_{k>n}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}.$$
The series is alternating with decreasing terms, so its absolute value is at most the first term: $\left|\frac{n!}e-D_n\right|\le\frac{n!}{(n+1)!}=\frac1{n+1}\le\frac13<\frac12$ for $n\ge2$. So $D_n$ is the integer closest to $\frac{n!}e$.

**Consequence.** The probability that a random permutation has no fixed point tends to $\frac1e$.

**Lesson.** Inclusion–exclusion: when the intersections of the bad events all have the same size depending only on the number of events, the sum collapses to a clean formula.

## Statement

A path of length $2n$ starts at height $0$ and at each step goes up by $1$ or down by $1$. Prove that the number of such paths that end at height $0$ and never go below height $0$ is
$$C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}n.$$

## Hint

Count all paths from $0$ to $0$, then subtract the bad ones. For a bad path, reflect the part before the first visit to height $-1$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The reflection principle: bad paths are in bijection with paths that are easy to count.

**All paths.** A path from $0$ to $0$ with $2n$ steps has $n$ up-steps and $n$ down-steps, so there are $\binom{2n}n$ of them.

**Bad paths.** A path from $0$ to $0$ is bad if it reaches height $-1$ at some moment. Consider the first such moment $t$, and reflect the portion of the path before $t$ about the line $y=-1$. The new path starts at $-2$, still reaches $-1$ first at time $t$, and ends at $0$.

Conversely, any path from $-2$ to $0$ with $2n$ steps must cross $-1$; reflecting the part before the first visit to $-1$ gives a bad path from $0$ to $0$. These operations are inverse, so bad paths are in bijection with all paths from $-2$ to $0$.

**Counting those.** A path from $-2$ to $0$ in $2n$ steps has $u$ up-steps and $d$ down-steps with $u+d=2n$ and $u-d=2$: $u=n+1$, $d=n-1$. So there are $\binom{2n}{n+1}$ of them.

**Conclusion.**
$$C_n=\binom{2n}n-\binom{2n}{n+1}=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}n.$$
The first values are $1,2,5,14,42,132,\dots$

**Remark.** The same numbers count well-formed sequences of $n$ pairs of brackets and triangulations of a convex $(n+2)$-gon.

**Lesson.** Reflection principle: reflect an initial segment to transform a constrained count into an unconstrained one.

## Statement

The squares of an $n\times n$ board ($n\ge2$) are all white. A move chooses one row or one column and changes the colour of every square in it. Prove that it is impossible to obtain a board with exactly one black square.

## Hint

Look at a $2\times2$ block formed by two rows and two columns, and count its black squares modulo $2$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Find a quantity that no move can change.

**Invariant.** Fix two rows $r\ne r'$ and two columns $c\ne c'$, and consider the four squares $(r,c),(r,c'),(r',c),(r',c')$. Look at the parity of the number of black squares among these four.
- A move on row $r$ (or $r'$) changes exactly two of them.
- A move on column $c$ (or $c'$) likewise changes exactly two.
- A move on any other row or column changes none of them.

Changing two squares changes their black count by $-2$, $0$ or $+2$: the parity of the black count is invariant.

**Conclusion.** At the start this number is $0$, so it stays even forever. Now suppose a board with a single black square $(a,b)$ could be reached. Choose any other row $r'\ne a$ and column $c'\ne b$ and the block of the rows $a,r'$ and columns $b,c'$: it contains exactly one black square, an odd number. Contradiction.

**Remark.** The reachable boards are exactly those in which every $2\times2$ block has an even number of black squares.

**Lesson.** Invariant: restrict attention to a small sub-configuration on which each move acts in a controlled way.

## Statement

Among $N$ coins, exactly one is heavier than the others, which all have the same weight. You may use a balance scale (comparing the weights of two groups, with three possible outcomes: left heavier, right heavier, balance).

1. Prove that if $N=3^k$, the heavy coin can always be found in $k$ weighings.
2. Prove that if $N>3^k$, it is not always possible to find it in $k$ weighings.

## Hint

For part 1, divide the coins into three equal groups. For part 2, count the possible sequences of outcomes.

## Solution

**1. Strategy.** Induction on $k$. For $k=0$ there is a single coin. For $k\ge1$, divide the $3^k$ coins into three groups $A,B,C$ of $3^{k-1}$ coins and weigh $A$ against $B$.
- If $A$ is heavier, the heavy coin is in $A$; if $B$ is heavier, it is in $B$.
- If the scale balances, it is in $C$.

In each case, the heavy coin is among $3^{k-1}$ coins, which can be found in $k-1$ further weighings by the induction hypothesis. In total $k$ weighings suffice.

**2. Lower bound.** Fix a strategy using at most $k$ weighings. Each possible heavy coin gives a sequence of at most $k$ outcomes, each outcome being one of $3$ values. There are at most $3^k$ such sequences.

If two different coins gave the same sequence, the strategy could not tell them apart. So a strategy that always finds the coin must give distinct sequences to distinct coins, hence $N\le3^k$. Therefore $N>3^k$ coins cannot be handled in $k$ weighings.

**Remark.** An information-theoretic argument: each weighing provides at most $\log_23$ bits of information. The harder variant with a coin that may be heavier or lighter is solved by the same principle: $12$ coins in $3$ weighings.

**Lesson.** Optimal algorithm problems come in two halves: an explicit strategy, and a counting argument on outcomes showing that nothing better is possible.
