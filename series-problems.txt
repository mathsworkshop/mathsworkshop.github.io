## Statement

Study the nature of the following series:

1. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{(\ln n)^n}$;
2. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{H_n}{\ln(n!)}$ where $H_n=1+\frac12+\cdots+\frac1n$;
3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1n\ln(n)\ln\!\left(1+\frac1n\right)$;
4. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{(\ln n)^{\ln n}}$.

## Hint

1. Cauchy's root test. 2. Compare $\ln(n!)$ with $n\ln n$ and $H_n$ with $\ln n$. 3. Use $\ln(1+1/n)\sim 1/n$, then compare $\ln n/n^2$ with $1/n^{3/2}$. 4. Write $(\ln n)^{\ln n}=n^{\ln\ln n}$ and compare with $1/n^2$.

## Solution

**(1) $\sum\frac{1}{(\ln n)^n}$.** By Cauchy's root test:
$$\sqrt[n]{\frac{1}{(\ln n)^n}}=\frac{1}{\ln n}\longrightarrow0<1.$$
The series **converges**.

**(2) $\sum\frac{H_n}{\ln(n!)}$.** First compare $\ln(n!)$ with $n\ln n$: for $1\le k\le n$, $\ln k\le\ln n$, hence
$$\ln(n!)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ln k\le n\ln n.$$
Therefore
$$\frac{H_n}{\ln(n!)}\ge\frac{H_n}{n\ln n}.$$
Moreover $H_n\ge\ln n$ (comparison of the harmonic sum with $\int_1^n\frac{dx}{x}$), so
$$\frac{H_n}{n\ln n}\ge\frac{\ln n}{n\ln n}=\frac1n.$$
Since $\sum\frac1n$ diverges, by comparison the series **diverges**.

**(3) $\sum\frac1n\ln(n)\ln(1+1/n)$.** Since $\ln(1+1/n)\sim\frac1n$ as $n\to\infty$, the general term satisfies
$$u_n=\frac{\ln n}{n}\cdot\ln\!\left(1+\frac1n\right)\sim\frac{\ln n}{n^2}.$$
Now $\frac{\ln n}{n^2}=o\!\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\right)$ (since $\ln n/\sqrt n\to0$), and $\sum n^{-3/2}$ converges (Riemann, $\alpha=3/2>1$). By comparison, the series **converges**.

**(4) $\sum\frac{1}{(\ln n)^{\ln n}}$.** Rewrite
$$\frac{1}{(\ln n)^{\ln n}}=\frac{1}{\exp(\ln n\cdot\ln\ln n)}=\frac{1}{n^{\ln\ln n}}.$$
Since $x\mapsto\ln\ln x$ is increasing and tends to $+\infty$ on $[e,+\infty[$, there exists $N$ with $\ln\ln n\ge2$ for all $n\ge N$. For such $n$,
$$u_n\le\frac{1}{n^2},$$
the general term of a convergent Riemann series. By comparison, the series **converges**.

## Statement

Let $(u_n)_{n\ge0}$ be a sequence with $u_n\ge0$ for all $n$.

1. Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}u_n$ converges if and only if $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\ln(1+u_n)$ converges.
2. Study the convergence of the series with general term
$$v_n=\ln\!\left(1+e^{-n}\frac{n!}{n^n}\right).$$

## Hint

For 1., use $\ln(1+u_n)\sim u_n$ as $u_n\to0$. For 2., apply 1. with $u_n=e^{-n}\frac{n!}{n^n}$, and compute the ratio $u_{n+1}/u_n$ to use d'Alembert's test.

## Solution

**1. Equivalence of the two series.** Both series have nonnegative terms, so we can use the equivalence test.

- Suppose $\sum u_n$ converges. Then $u_n\to0$, hence $\ln(1+u_n)\sim u_n$. Since the terms are nonnegative and equivalent, $\sum\ln(1+u_n)$ converges.

- Conversely, suppose $\sum\ln(1+u_n)$ converges. Then $\ln(1+u_n)\to0$, hence $u_n\to0$ (as $u_n=e^{\ln(1+u_n)}-1\to0$). Therefore $\ln(1+u_n)\sim u_n$, and by the equivalence test $\sum u_n$ converges.


**2. Convergence of $\sum v_n$.** By 1., it suffices to study the series with nonnegative term $u_n=e^{-n}\dfrac{n!}{n^n}$. Compute the ratio:
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=e^{-(n+1)}\frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\cdot e^{n}\frac{n^n}{n!}=e^{-1}\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n=e^{-1}\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{-n}.$$
Since $\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n\to e$, we get
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}\longrightarrow e^{-1}\cdot e^{-1}=e^{-2}<1.$$
By d'Alembert's ratio test, $\sum u_n$ converges. By 1., $\sum v_n$ **converges**.

## Statement

Show that the following numerical series converge and compute their sums:

1. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{2^{n+1}}{3^n}$;
2. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\ln\!\left(1-\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\right)$;
3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\arctan\!\left(\frac{1}{n^2+n+1}\right)$.

## Hint

1. Recognize a geometric series with ratio $\frac23$, and apply d'Alembert's ratio test. 2. Expand $\ln\frac{(n-1)(n+2)}{n(n+1)}$ and telescope. 3. Use $\arctan x+\arctan y=\arctan\frac{x+y}{1-xy}$ with $x=-n$, $y=n+1$.

## Solution

**(1) Geometric series.** For $n\ge1$,
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\frac{2^{n+2}}{3^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{3^n}{2^{n+1}}=\frac{2}{3}<1,$$
so by d'Alembert's ratio test the series **converges**. We recognize
$$\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{2^{n+1}}{3^n}=2\sum_{n\ge1}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^n,$$
a geometric series with ratio $\frac23$. Its partial sums are
$$S_n=2\sum_{k=1}^n\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^k=2\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1-(2/3)^n}{1-2/3}.$$
Taking $n\to+\infty$:
$$S=2\cdot\frac{2/3}{1/3}=2\cdot 2=4.$$

**(2) Logarithmic telescoping series.** For $n\ge1$,
$$1-\frac{2}{n(n+1)}=\frac{(n-1)(n+2)}{n(n+1)},$$
hence
$$\ln\!\left(1-\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\right)=\ln(n-1)+\ln(n+2)-\ln n-\ln(n+1).$$
Regroup the terms as
$$\ln(n+2)-\ln(n+1)-\bigl(\ln n-\ln(n-1)\bigr).$$
The partial sum of order $n$ telescopes:
$$S_n=\ln(n+2)-\ln n-\ln 3=\ln\!\left(1+\frac2n\right)-\ln 3.$$
Since $\ln(1+2/n)\to0$, we get $S_n\to-\ln 3$. The series **converges** with sum $-\ln 3$.

**(3) Arctangent telescoping series.** Using $\arctan x+\arctan y=\arctan\frac{x+y}{1-xy}$ with $x=-n$ and $y=n+1$:
$$\arctan(-n)+\arctan(n+1)=\arctan\frac{1}{1+n(n+1)}=\arctan\!\left(\frac{1}{n^2+n+1}\right).$$
Since $\arctan(-n)=-\arctan n$,
$$\arctan\!\left(\frac{1}{n^2+n+1}\right)=\arctan(n+1)-\arctan n.$$
The partial sum telescopes:
$$S_n=\arctan(n+1)-\arctan(1)=\arctan(n+1)-\frac{\pi}{4}.$$
As $n\to+\infty$, $\arctan(n+1)\to\frac{\pi}{2}$, so $S_n\to\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\pi}{4}$. The series **converges** with sum $\frac{\pi}{4}$.

## Statement

Study the nature and, in case of convergence, compute the sum of the following series:

1. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{2^{n+1}}{3^n}$;
2. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\ln\!\left(1-\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\right)$;
3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\arctan\!\left(\frac{1}{n^2+n+1}\right)$.

## Hint

For 1., factor as a geometric series. For 2., expand $\ln\frac{(n-1)(n+2)}{n(n+1)}$ and telescope. For 3., use $\arctan a+\arctan b=\arctan\frac{a+b}{1-ab}$ with $x=-n$, $y=n+1$.

## Solution

**(1)** For $n\ge1$,
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\frac{2^{n+2}}{3^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{3^n}{2^{n+1}}=\frac{2}{3}<1,$$
so by d'Alembert's ratio test the series **converges**. Moreover,
$$\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{2^{n+1}}{3^n}=2\sum_{n\ge1}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^n=2\cdot\frac{2/3}{1-2/3}=2\cdot2=4.$$
Hence the series converges with sum $4$.

**(2)** For $n\ge1$,
$$1-\frac{2}{n(n+1)}=\frac{(n-1)(n+2)}{n(n+1)},$$
so
$$\ln\!\left(1-\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\right)=\ln(n-1)+\ln(n+2)-\ln n-\ln(n+1).$$
Regroup as
$$\ln(n+2)-\ln(n+1)-\bigl(\ln n-\ln(n-1)\bigr).$$
The partial sum telescopes:
$$S_n=\ln(n+2)-\ln n-\ln 3.$$
As $n\to+\infty$, $\ln(n+2)-\ln n=\ln\!\left(1+\frac2n\right)\to0$, so $S_n\to-\ln3$.
The series **converges** with sum $-\ln 3$.

**(3)** Using $\arctan a+\arctan b=\arctan\frac{a+b}{1-ab}$ with $x=-n$ and $y=n+1$:
$$\arctan(-n)+\arctan(n+1)=\arctan\frac{1}{1+n(n+1)}=\arctan\!\left(\frac{1}{n^2+n+1}\right).$$
Since $\arctan(-n)=-\arctan(n)$,
$$\arctan\!\left(\frac{1}{n^2+n+1}\right)=\arctan(n+1)-\arctan(n).$$
The partial sum telescopes:
$$S_n=\arctan(n+1)-\arctan(1)=\arctan(n+1)-\frac{\pi}{4}.$$
As $n\to+\infty$, $\arctan(n+1)\to\pi/2$. Hence $S_n\to\frac{\pi}{4}$: the series **converges** with sum $\pi/4$.

**Conclusion.** (1) converges, sum $4$; (2) converges, sum $-\ln3$; (3) converges, sum $\pi/4$.

## Statement

Study the nature of the following numeric series:

1. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n!}{n^n}$;
2. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n!}{a^n}$ with $a>0$;
3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{2n}{n+2^n}$;
4. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{n\ln n\,(\ln\ln n)^\beta}$, $\beta\in\mathbb{R}$;
5. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n^{\ln n}}{(\ln n)^n}$;
6. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\cos^2 n}{\sqrt n}$.

## Hint

1. Ratio test. 2. Ratio test, discuss $a$. 3. Compare with $2^{-n}$. 4. Bertrand series with substitution. 5. Rewrite $n^{\ln n}=e^{(\ln n)^2}$ and $(\ln n)^n=e^{n\ln\ln n}$, compare to $1/n^2$. 6. Use $\cos^2 n\ge0$ and comparison with harmonic.

## Solution

**(1) $\sum\frac{n!}{n^n}$.** Ratio:
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{n^n}{n!}=\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n=\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{-n}\to\frac1e<1.$$
By d'Alembert, the series **converges**.

**(2) $\sum\frac{n!}{a^n}$, $a>0$.** Ratio:
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\frac{(n+1)!}{a^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{a^n}{n!}=\frac{n+1}{a}\to+\infty.$$
For every fixed $a>0$, the ratio eventually exceeds $1$, so $u_n\not\to0$: the series **diverges**.

**(3) $\sum\frac{2n}{n+2^n}$.** For $n\ge1$,
$$0\le\frac{2n}{n+2^n}\le\frac{2n}{2^n}=2n\cdot2^{-n}.$$
The series $\sum 2n\cdot2^{-n}$ converges (ratio test: $(n+1)/2n\to1/2<1$). By comparison, the series **converges**.

**(4) $\sum\frac{1}{n\ln n(\ln\ln n)^\beta}$.** Use the substitution $u=\ln\ln t$; the integral $\int\frac{dt}{t\ln t(\ln\ln t)^\beta}=\int\frac{du}{u^\beta}$ converges iff $\beta>1$. Hence the series **converges if $\beta>1$** and **diverges if $\beta\le1$**.

**(5) $\sum\frac{n^{\ln n}}{(\ln n)^n}$.** Write
$$n^{\ln n}=\exp((\ln n)^2),\qquad (\ln n)^n=\exp(n\ln\ln n),$$
so
$$u_n=\exp\bigl((\ln n)^2-n\ln\ln n\bigr).$$
Since $n\ln\ln n$ dominates $(\ln n)^2$, one has $u_n=o(n^{-2})$. By comparison with $\sum n^{-2}$, the series **converges**.

**(6) $\sum\frac{\cos^2 n}{\sqrt n}$.** Since $\cos^2 n\ge0$ and $\cos^2 n\ge\frac12$ along a positive density of integers (e.g. $n\equiv0\pmod{2\pi}$ approximation), one gets $\frac{\cos^2 n}{\sqrt n}\not\to0$ fast enough. More directly: $\cos^2 n=\frac{1+\cos 2n}{2}$, so
$$\frac{\cos^2 n}{\sqrt n}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt n}+\frac{\cos 2n}{2\sqrt n}.$$
The first term gives a divergent series ($\sum n^{-1/2}$), while the second converges by Dirichlet's test. Hence the series **diverges** (to $+\infty$).

## Statement

Study the nature of the following series and, in case of convergence, compute their sum:
$$u_n=\ln\!\left(1+\frac1n\right),\qquad v_n=\ln\!\left(1-\frac1n\right),\qquad w_n=\ln\!\left(1+\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\right).$$

## Hint

Use $\ln(1+x)=\ln a-\ln b$ when $1+x=a/b$, and write partial sums to observe telescoping.

## Solution

**1. Series $\sum u_n$.** For $n\ge1$,
$$u_n=\ln\!\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)=\ln(n+1)-\ln n.$$
The partial sum from $n=1$ to $N$ telescopes:
$$S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\ln(n+1)-\ln n)=\ln(N+1)-\ln 1=\ln(N+1).$$
As $N\to+\infty$, $S_N\to+\infty$: the series **diverges**.

**2. Series $\sum v_n$.** The term $v_n$ is defined for $n\ge2$. For $n\ge2$,
$$v_n=\ln\!\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)=\ln(n-1)-\ln n.$$
The partial sum from $n=2$ to $N$ telescopes:
$$T_N=\sum_{n=2}^{N}(\ln(n-1)-\ln n)=\ln 1-\ln N=-\ln N.$$
As $N\to+\infty$, $T_N\to-\infty$: the series **diverges** (to $-\infty$).

**3. Series $\sum w_n$.** The term $w_n$ is defined for $n\ge2$. Split the partial sum into blocks:
$$w_{2p}=\ln\!\left(\frac{2p+1}{2p}\right),\qquad w_{2p+1}=\ln\!\left(\frac{2p}{2p+1}\right).$$
Note that $w_{2p}+w_{2p+1}=0$ for every $p\ge1$, since $\frac{2p+1}{2p}\cdot\frac{2p}{2p+1}=1$.

**Odd partial sums.** For $N\ge1$:
$$Z_{2N+1}=\sum_{n=2}^{2N+1}w_n=\sum_{p=1}^{N}(w_{2p}+w_{2p+1})=0.$$

**Even partial sums.** For $N\ge1$:
$$Z_{2N}=\sum_{n=2}^{2N}w_n=\ln\!\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)+\sum_{p=2}^{N}(w_{2p-1}+w_{2p}).$$
But for $p\ge2$, $w_{2p-1}+w_{2p}=\ln\!\left(\frac{2p-1}{2p}\right)+\ln\!\left(\frac{2p+1}{2p}\right)$ — the cancellation now leaves $\ln\frac{2N+1}{2N}$:
$$Z_{2N}=\ln\frac{3}{2}+\ln\frac{3}{4}+\ln\frac{5}{4}+\cdots+\ln\frac{2N+1}{2N}=\ln\frac{2N+1}{2N}.$$
Indeed the product telescopes:
$$\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{5}{4}\cdot\frac{5}{6}\cdots\frac{2N+1}{2N}=\frac{2N+1}{2N}.$$
As $N\to+\infty$, $Z_{2N}=\ln\!\left(1+\frac{1}{2N}\right)\to0$.

**Conclusion.** Both $Z_{2N}$ and $Z_{2N+1}$ tend to $0$, so the sequence of partial sums $(Z_N)$ converges to $0$. Hence the series $\sum w_n$ **converges** with sum $0$.

Compute

Decompose into partial fractions and look at the partial sums.

## Solution

$$S_N=\frac12\sum_{n=1}^N\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+2}\right)=\frac12\left(1+\frac12-\frac1{N+1}-\frac1{N+2}\right)\longrightarrow\frac34.$$

## Statement

1. Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty\ln\left(1+\frac1n\right)$ diverges although its terms tend to $0$.
2. Compute $\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^\infty\ln\left(1-\frac1{n^2}\right)$.

## Hint

Use $\ln\frac ab=\ln a-\ln b$ so that the partial sums telescope.

## Solution

**1.** $\ln\left(1+\frac1n\right)=\ln(n+1)-\ln n$, so $S_N=\ln(N+1)\to+\infty$. The series diverges, even though $\ln(1+\frac1n)\to0$: the terms tend to $0$ too slowly (they behave like $\frac1n$).

**2.** $1-\frac1{n^2}=\frac{(n-1)(n+1)}{n^2}$, so
$$\begin{aligned} 
\ln\left(1-\frac1{n^2}\right)&=\ln(n-1)-2\ln n+\ln(n+1)\\ &=\bigl[\ln(n+1)-\ln n\bigr]-\bigl[\ln n-\ln(n-1)\bigr].
\end{aligned}$$
Summing from $2$ to $N$, the brackets telescope:
$$S_N=\ln(N+1)-\ln N-\bigl(\ln2-\ln1\bigr)\longrightarrow-\ln2.$$

**Remark.** Exponentiating gives the infinite product $\prod_{n\ge2}\left(1-\frac1{n^2}\right)=\frac12$.

## Statement

Show that the following series diverge.

1. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\cos\frac1n$
2. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n$
3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^n\frac{n}{n+1}$

## Hint

If $\sum u_n$ converges, then $u_n\to0$ (because $u_n=S_n-S_{n-1}$).

## Solution

**The principle.** If $S_n = \sum_{n=1}^k u_n$ converges to $S$, then $u_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\to S-S=0$. So if $u_n\not\to0$, the series diverges.

**1.** $\cos\frac1n\to\cos0=1\ne0$.

**2.** $\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n\to e\ne0$.

**3.** $\left|(-1)^n\frac n{n+1}\right|\to1$, so the terms do not tend to $0$.

**The converse is false.** $u_n=\frac1n\to0$ but $\sum\frac1n$ diverges. Tending to zero is *necessary* for convergence, never sufficient.

## Statement

Let $S=1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\dots=\ln2$. Show that the rearranged series
$$1+\frac13-\frac12+\frac15+\frac17-\frac14+\frac19+\frac1{11}-\frac16+\cdots$$
(two odd reciprocals, then one even one) converges to $\frac32\ln2$, not $\ln2$.

## Hint

Write $\frac12S=\frac12-\frac14+\frac16-\dots$ and add it to $S$ term by term, keeping the zeros in place.

## Solution

**Idea.** Add the series $S$ to the series $\frac S2$, in which the term $\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{2k}$ is placed at position $2k$ and zeros elsewhere:
$$\frac S2=0+\frac12+0-\frac14+0+\frac16+0-\frac18+\cdots$$
Two convergent series can be added term by term.

**Addition.**
- position $1$: $1+0=1$
- position $2$: $-\frac12+\frac12=0$
- position $3$: $\frac13$
- position $4$: $-\frac14-\frac14=-\frac12$
- position $5$: $\frac15$
- position $6$: $-\frac16+\frac16=0$
- position $7$: $\frac17$
- position $8$: $-\frac18-\frac18=-\frac14$

In general the even positions alternate between $0$ and $-\frac1{2k}$ for $k$ even, and the odd positions are $\frac1{2k+1}$.

**Removing zeros.** Deleting zero terms from a convergent series does not change its sum. We obtain
$$\frac32S=1+\frac13-\frac12+\frac15+\frac17-\frac14+\frac19+\frac1{11}-\frac16+\cdots$$
which is exactly the rearrangement (every positive term $\frac1{2k+1}$ and every negative term $-\frac1{2k}$ appears once).

**Conclusion.** The rearranged series sums to $\frac32\ln2\approx1.0397\ne\ln2\approx0.6931$.

**Remark.** This is an instance of *Riemann's rearrangement theorem*: a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to **any** real number, or to diverge. Only absolutely convergent series are safe to rearrange.

## Statement

## Statement

## Statement

$$\sum_{n\ge2}\frac1{n^\alpha(\ln n)^\beta}.$$

## Hint

Split into $\alpha>1$, $\alpha<1$ and $\alpha=1$. A power of $n$, however small, beats every power of $\ln n$.

## Solution

**$\alpha>1$.** Choose $\alpha'\in(1,\alpha)$. Whatever the sign of $\beta$, $(\ln n)^{-\beta}\le n^{\alpha-\alpha'}$ for large $n$ (a power of $\ln n$ is negligible against a positive power of $n$). So $u_n\le\frac1{n^{\alpha'}}$, and the series **converges**.

**$\alpha<1$.** Choose $\alpha'\in(\alpha,1)$. Whatever the sign of $\beta$, $(\ln n)^\beta\le n^{\alpha'-\alpha}$ for large $n$. So $u_n\ge\frac1{n^{\alpha'}}$, and the series **diverges**.

**$\alpha=1$.** For $\beta>0$ the function $\frac1{x(\ln x)^\beta}$ is positive and decreasing. With $u=\ln x$,
$$\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^\beta}=\int_{\ln2}^\infty\frac{du}{u^\beta},$$
finite iff $\beta>1$. Fix an integer $n\geq 2$. On $[n:n+1]$, we have
$$ \frac1{(n+1)^\alpha(\ln (n+1))^\beta} \leq f \leq \frac1{n^\alpha(\ln n)^\beta}. $$

$$ \frac1{(n+1)^\alpha(\ln (n+1))^\beta} \leq \int_n^{n+1} f(x) dx \leq \frac1{n^\alpha(\ln n)^\beta}. $$

$$ \sum_{n\geq 2} \frac1{(n+1)^\alpha(\ln (n+1))^\beta} \leq \int_2^{=\infty} f(x) dx \leq \sum_{n=2}^{+\infty} \frac1{n^\alpha(\ln n)^\beta}. $$
Thus the serie $\sum_{n\geq 2} \frac1{(n+1)^\alpha(\ln (n+1))^\beta}$ and the integral $\int_2^{+\infty} f(x) dx$ have the same nature.  

For $\beta\le0$, $\frac1{n(\ln n)^\beta}\ge\frac1n$ for $n\ge3$: divergence.

**Conclusion.** The series converges iff $\alpha>1$, or $\alpha=1$ and $\beta>1$.

**1.** For $N\ge3$ the partial sums telescope with a step of $2$:
$$S_N=\frac12\sum_{n=2}^N\left(\frac1{n-1}-\frac1{n+1}\right)=\frac12\left(1+\frac12-\frac1N-\frac1{N+1}\right)\longrightarrow\frac34.$$

**2.** For $n\ge2$, $\frac1{n^2}<\frac1{n^2-1}$. So the partial sums of $\sum\frac1{n^2}$ are increasing and bounded by $1+\frac34=\frac74$: the series converges, with sum less than $\frac74$.

**Remark.** The true value is $\frac{\pi^2}6\approx1.645$, and $\frac74=1.75$: a crude bound obtained with no calculus at all.

## Statement

For $x\ge0$ let $u_n(x)=\dfrac x{n(1+nx)}$. Show that $\sum u_n(x)$ converges for every $x\ge0$ and that the convergence is **normal** on $[0,+\infty)$. What can you say about the sum $f(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}u_n(x)$?

## Hint

Bound $u_n(x)$ by $\frac1{n^2}$ uniformly in $x$.

## Solution

**Pointwise.** For $x=0$, all terms vanish. For $x>0$, $1+nx\ge nx$, so
$$0\le u_n(x)=\frac1n\cdot\frac x{1+nx}\le\frac1n\cdot\frac1n=\frac1{n^2}.$$
The series converges.

**Normally.** The bound $0\le u_n(x)\le\frac1{n^2}$ holds for all $x\ge0$, and $\sum\frac1{n^2}<\infty$. So $\sum\sup_x|u_n|<\infty$: the convergence is normal, hence uniform, on $[0,+\infty)$.

**Consequence.** Each $u_n$ is continuous on $[0,+\infty)$, and a uniformly convergent series of continuous functions has a continuous sum. So $f$ is continuous on $[0,+\infty)$, with $f(0)=0$.

**Remark.** One can check that $f$ is not differentiable at $0$ from the right: $\frac{f(x)}x=\sum\frac1{n(1+nx)}\to\sum\frac1n=\infty$ as $x\to0^+$, a classic example where the derivative of a uniformly convergent series cannot be computed term by term.
