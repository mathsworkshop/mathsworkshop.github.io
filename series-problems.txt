## Statement

Determine whether each series converges, and if so compute its sum:
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{2^k},\qquad
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{a}{1+a}\right)^k,\qquad
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(1-x^2)^k.
\]

## Hint

All are geometric series $\sum r^k$; converge iff $|r|<1$, sum
$\frac{1}{1-r}$.

## Solution

**Idea.** A geometric series is the simplest series: we know exactly when it converges and what it sums to. The only work is to identify the ratio and check the condition $|r|<1$.

**First series.** Here $r=-\frac12$, and $|r|=\frac12<1$, so the series converges with sum
$$\frac{1}{1-(-1/2)}=\frac{1}{3/2}=\frac23.$$

**Second series.** Here $r=\frac{a}{1+a}$. The convergence condition $|r|<1$ means
$$\left|\frac{a}{1+a}\right|<1.$$
Since $1+a$ must be nonzero (otherwise $r$ is undefined), we solve: $|a|<|1+a|$. Squaring both sides, $a^2<(1+a)^2=1+2a+a^2$, so $0<1+2a$, i.e. $a>-\frac12$. Under this condition the sum is
$$\frac{1}{1-\frac{a}{1+a}}=1+a.$$

**Third series.** Here $r=1-x^2$. Convergence requires $|1-x^2|<1$, i.e. $-1<1-x^2<1$. The left inequality gives $x^2<2$, the right gives $x^2>0$. So the series converges exactly for $x\in\,]0,\sqrt2[\,\cup\,]-\sqrt2,0[$. Its sum is
$$\frac{1}{1-(1-x^2)}=\frac{1}{x^2}.$$

**Remark.** In all three cases the answer is a *function* of the parameter: the sum of a geometric series depends continuously on $r$ on the interval $|r|<1$, and blows up as $r\to1^-$.

## Statement

Show that the series $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{k(k+p)}$ converges (for fixed $p\in\mathbb{N}^*$) and compute its sum.

## Hint

Use partial fractions: $\frac{1}{k(k+p)}=\frac1p\left(\frac1k-\frac1{k+p}\right)$. Write the partial sums and observe the cancellation.

## Solution

**Idea.** A *telescoping* series is one where consecutive terms cancel. The partial sum collapses to a handful of terms, and the limit is then easy to read off.

**Convergence.** For every $k\ge1$, $\frac{1}{k(k+p)}\le\frac{1}{k^2}$, and $\sum\frac1{k^2}$ converges (Riemann with $\alpha=2>1$). By comparison, the series converges.

**Partial sums.** Decompose:
$$\frac{1}{k(k+p)}=\frac1p\left(\frac1k-\frac1{k+p}\right).$$
Therefore
$$S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k(k+p)}=\frac1p\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac1k-\frac1{k+p}\right).$$
Writing out the terms, everything cancels except the first $p$ terms and the last $p$ terms:
$$S_n=\frac1p\left(\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac1k-\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+p}\frac1k\right).$$

**Limit.** As $n\to+\infty$, each of the $p$ terms $\frac{1}{n+1},\ldots,\frac{1}{n+p}$ tends to $0$. Hence
$$\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{k(k+p)}=\frac1p\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac1k.}$$

**Check.** For $p=1$: $\sum\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=1$, which is correct since $\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}$ and the sum telescopes to $1$.

@@@ divergence-test-first-examples.md
---
title: The divergence test and three divergent series
category: calculus/single-variable/series
level: lycee
difficulty: 1
tags: divergence test, necessary condition, counterexamples
date: 2026-11-02
---

## Statement

Show that if $\sum u_n$ converges then $u_n\to0$. Deduce that $\sum\frac{n}{n+1}$, $\sum(-1)^n$, and $\sum\cos\frac1n$ diverge.

## Hint

The partial sums $S_n$ converge, hence $u_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\to0$. Check the contrapositive.

## Solution

**The theorem.** Let $S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_k$. If $\sum u_n$ converges, then $S_n\to S$ for some real $S$. Since $S_n-S_{n-1}=u_n$ and both $S_n$ and $S_{n-1}$ tend to the same limit $S$, we get
$$u_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\longrightarrow S-S=0.$$

**The contrapositive.** If $u_n$ does not tend to $0$, the series *cannot* converge. This is the **divergence test**, and it is always the first thing to check.

**Application to the three series.**
- $\frac{n}{n+1}=\frac{1}{1+1/n}\to1\ne0$, so $\sum\frac{n}{n+1}$ diverges.
- $(-1)^n$ takes the values $1,-1,1,-1,\ldots$ and has no limit, so $\sum(-1)^n$ diverges.
- $\cos\frac1n\to\cos0=1\ne0$, so $\sum\cos\frac1n$ diverges.

**Warning.** The converse is false: $u_n\to0$ does **not** imply that $\sum u_n$ converges. The harmonic series $\sum\frac1n$ has $u_n\to0$ but diverges. The divergence test is only a one-way tool: it can prove divergence, never convergence.

## Statement

Compute $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{3^n+2^n}{6^n}$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{2n+1}{3^n}$.

## Hint

Split into geometric series. For the second, use $\sum_{n\ge0} n r^n=\frac{r}{(1-r)^2}$ for $|r|<1$.

## Solution

**First sum.** Split the fraction:
$$\frac{3^n+2^n}{6^n}=\frac{3^n}{6^n}+\frac{2^n}{6^n}=\frac{1}{2^n}+\frac{1}{3^n}.$$
Both are geometric with ratio $\frac12$ and $\frac13$, and
$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2^n}=1,\qquad \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{3^n}=\frac{1/3}{1-1/3}=\frac12.$$
Hence the first sum equals $1+\frac12=\frac32$.

**Second sum.** Write $2n+1=2n+1$ and split:
$$\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{2n+1}{3^n}=2\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{n}{3^n}+\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{3^n}.$$
The second is geometric: $\frac{1}{1-1/3}=\frac32$. For the first, use the formula $\sum_{n\ge0}nr^n=\frac{r}{(1-r)^2}$ with $r=\frac13$:
$$\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{n}{3^n}=\frac{1/3}{(2/3)^2}=\frac{1/3}{4/9}=\frac34.$$
Therefore the second sum equals $2\cdot\frac34+\frac32=\frac32+\frac32=3$.

**Remark.** The identity $\sum n r^n=\frac{r}{(1-r)^2}$ is obtained by differentiating $\sum r^n=\frac{1}{1-r}$ term by term (legitimate for $|r|<1$).

## Statement

Study $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\left(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n\right)$.

## Hint

Rationalize: $\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}$. Alternatively, compute the partial sum directly.

## Solution

**Direct telescoping.** The partial sum is
$$S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n\right)=\sqrt{N+1}-\sqrt1=\sqrt{N+1}-1.$$
As $N\to+\infty$, $\sqrt{N+1}\to+\infty$, so $S_N\to+\infty$. The series **diverges**.

**Comparison viewpoint.** Rationalizing gives
$$\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n=\frac{(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n)(\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n)}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}.$$
Since $\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n\sim 2\sqrt n$, the general term is $\sim\frac{1}{2\sqrt n}$, which is comparable to a Riemann series with $\alpha=\frac12\le1$. This confirms divergence.

**Remark.** The two methods agree, but the direct telescoping is more informative: it shows that the partial sums grow like $\sqrt N$. This is the same growth rate as the integral $\int_1^N\frac{dt}{2\sqrt t}=\sqrt N-1$.

@@@ comparison-test-basic.md

title: The comparison test on three series
category: calculus/single-variable/series
level: l1
difficulty: 2
tags: comparison test, equivalences, trigonometric limits
date: 2026-11-02
---

## Statement

Determine the nature of
\[
\text{(a) }\sum_{n\ge1}\left(1-\cos\frac{\pi}{n}\right),\qquad
\text{(b) }\sum_{n\ge1}\ln\left(1+\frac1n\right),\qquad
\text{(c) }\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n+n}{n^2+1}.
\]

## Hint

(a) Use $1-\cos x\sim\frac{x^2}{2}$ as $x\to0$. (b) Use $\ln(1+x)\sim x$ as $x\to0$. (c) Find an equivalent of the general term.

## Solution

**General principle.** For positive-term series, if $u_n\sim v_n$ (i.e. $u_n/v_n\to1$), then $\sum u_n$ and $\sum v_n$ have the same nature. This is the **equivalence test**, a consequence of the comparison test.

**(a)** As $n\to+\infty$, $\frac{\pi}{n}\to0$, so
$$1-\cos\frac{\pi}{n}\sim\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)^2=\frac{\pi^2}{2n^2}.$$
The series $\sum\frac{1}{n^2}$ converges (Riemann, $\alpha=2>1$), so the given series **converges**.

**(b)** As $n\to+\infty$, $\frac1n\to0$, so
$$\ln\left(1+\frac1n\right)\sim\frac1n.$$
The harmonic series diverges, so the given series **diverges**.

**(c)** The general term is positive for $n\ge2$ (the numerator $n+(-1)^n$ is positive). Moreover
$$\frac{(-1)^n+n}{n^2+1}\sim\frac{n}{n^2}=\frac1n.$$
The harmonic series diverges, so the given series **diverges**.

**Remark.** In (c), the $(-1)^n$ in the numerator is negligible compared with $n$. Do not be misled by the alternating-looking sign: the series is *not* alternating in the usual sense, and it diverges.

@@@ riemann-series-recognition.md

## Statement

Study the nature of $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1}{n^2+n+1}$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}ne^{-\sqrt n}$.

## Hint

First: find an equivalent of the general term. Second: compare with $\frac{1}{n^2}$ using the fact that exponential growth dominates polynomial growth.

## Solution

**First series.** The denominator behaves like $n^2$:
$$n^2+n+1=n^2\left(1+\frac1n+\frac1{n^2}\right)\sim n^2.$$
Hence
$$\frac{1}{n^2+n+1}\sim\frac{1}{n^2}.$$
Since $\sum\frac{1}{n^2}$ converges (Riemann, $\alpha=2>1$), the series **converges**.

**Second series.** Write
$$ne^{-\sqrt n}=\exp(\ln n-\sqrt n).$$
For large $n$, $\frac{\ln n}{\sqrt n}\to0$, so $\ln n-\sqrt n\sim-\sqrt n\to-\infty$. In particular, for large $n$, $\ln n-\sqrt n\le-2\ln n$, i.e.
$$ne^{-\sqrt n}\le e^{-2\ln n}=\frac{1}{n^2}.$$
Comparison with the convergent Riemann series $\sum\frac{1}{n^2}$ shows that the series **converges**.

**Remark.** The hierarchy $n^a\ll e^{n^b}$ (for $a,b>0$) means that any polynomial factor is crushed by the exponential. Here the exponent is only $\sqrt n$, but it is enough to beat the factor $n$.

@@@ comparison-with-harmonic.md

title: The logarithm is smaller than the identity
category: calculus/single-variable/series
level: l1
difficulty: 2
tags: comparison test, logarithm, harmonic series
date: 2026-11-02
---

## Statement

Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{\ln n}$ diverges.

## Hint

Compare with $\frac1n$: for large $n$, $\ln n\le n$, hence $\frac{1}{\ln n}\ge\frac1n$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The logarithm grows, but very slowly: it is eventually smaller than the identity. Hence its reciprocal is eventually *larger* than $\frac1n$, and the harmonic series pushes everything to divergence.

**Computation.** For $n\ge2$, we have $\ln n\le n$ (this follows from $\ln t\le t-1$ for $t>0$, applied at $t=n$). Therefore
$$\frac{1}{\ln n}\ge\frac1n.$$
Since $\sum_{n\ge2}\frac1n$ diverges (harmonic series), the comparison test gives that $\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{\ln n}$ **diverges**.

**Remark.** This is a good illustration of how weak the divergence of the harmonic series is: even replacing $n$ by the much smaller quantity $\ln n$ does not save the series. To get convergence with a logarithmic factor, one needs $\sum\frac{1}{n(\ln n)^\beta}$ with $\beta>1$.

@@@ integral-test-for-logarithmic-series.md

title: The integral test on two logarithmic series
category: calculus/single-variable/series
level: l1
difficulty: 3
tags: integral test, logarithm, Bertrand series
date: 2026-11-02
---

## Statement

Study $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{n(\ln n)^2}$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{n\ln n}$.

## Hint

Use the integral test with $f(x)=\frac{1}{x(\ln x)^\beta}$: substitute $u=\ln x$.

## Solution

**The integral test.** If $f:[1,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ is positive, continuous, and decreasing, then $\sum f(n)$ converges if and only if $\int_1^{+\infty}f(x)\,dx$ converges. Here $f(x)=\frac{1}{x(\ln x)^\beta}$ satisfies these hypotheses for $x$ large enough (the function is positive and decreasing on $[2,+\infty)$).

**Substitution.** Let $u=\ln x$, so $du=\frac{dx}{x}$. Then
$$\int_2^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^\beta}=\int_{\ln2}^{+\infty}\frac{du}{u^\beta}.$$
This is a Riemann integral at $+\infty$ with exponent $\beta$. It converges if and only if $\beta>1$.

**Conclusion.**
- For $\beta=2>1$: $\sum\frac{1}{n(\ln n)^2}$ **converges**.
- For $\beta=1$: $\sum\frac{1}{n\ln n}$ **diverges**.

**Remark.** For $\beta=1$, one can also compute the partial sums explicitly: $\sum_{n=2}^{N}\frac{1}{n\ln n}\approx\ln(\ln N)$, which tends to $+\infty$.

## Statement

Study $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\left(\frac1n-\sin\frac1n\right)$.

## Hint

Use the Taylor expansion $\sin x=x-\frac{x^3}{6}+o(x^3)$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The two terms $\frac1n$ and $\sin\frac1n$ almost cancel: their difference is much smaller than either term. The Taylor expansion reveals the true order of magnitude.

**Computation.** As $n\to+\infty$, $x=\frac1n\to0$, and
$$\sin x=x-\frac{x^3}{6}+o(x^3).$$
Therefore
$$\frac1n-\sin\frac1n=\frac1n-\left(\frac1n-\frac{1}{6n^3}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^3}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{6n^3}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^3}\right).$$
So the general term is positive for large $n$ and
$$\frac1n-\sin\frac1n\sim\frac{1}{6n^3}.$$

**Conclusion.** Since $\sum\frac{1}{n^3}$ converges (Riemann, $\alpha=3>1$), the given series **converges**.

**Remark.** Without the Taylor expansion, one might incorrectly conclude divergence by comparing $\frac1n-\sin\frac1n$ with $\frac1n$. The cancellation is the whole point: it makes the series converge much faster than the harmonic series.

## Statement

Compute $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}$.

## Hint

Partial fractions: $\frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{n(n+1)}-\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\right)$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The decomposition is designed to make the series telescope *twice*: each term is a difference of consecutive terms of a simpler sequence.

**Decomposition.** We claim
$$\frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{n(n+1)}-\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\right).$$
Verification: the right-hand side equals
$$\frac12\cdot\frac{(n+2)-n}{n(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac12\cdot\frac{2}{n(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}.$$

**Partial sums.** With $v_n=\frac{1}{n(n+1)}$, the general term is $\frac12(v_n-v_{n+1})$, so
$$S_N=\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{N}(v_n-v_{n+1})=\frac12(v_1-v_{N+1}).$$
Since $v_1=\frac12$ and $v_{N+1}=\frac{1}{(N+1)(N+2)}\to0$, we get
$$\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14.}$$

**Remark.** This is the case $p=1$ of the general decomposition into two consecutive terms. One could also decompose directly into $\frac{1/2}{n}-\frac{1}{n+1}+\frac{1/2}{n+2}$.

Study the nature of $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\sqrt n}{n^2+1}$.

@@@ ratio-test-dalembert.md
---
title: d'Alembert's ratio test with a parameter
category: calculus/single-variable/series
level: l2
difficulty: 2
tags: ratio test, factorials, parameter, number e
date: 2026-11-02
---

## Statement

Study, depending on $a>0$, the series $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n!\,a^n}{n^n}$.

## Hint

Compute $\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}$ and find its limit.

## Solution

**Ratio computation.** With $u_n=\frac{n!\,a^n}{n^n}$,
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\frac{(n+1)!\,a^{n+1}}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{n^n}{n!\,a^n}=a\cdot\frac{(n+1)!}{n!}\cdot\frac{n^n}{(n+1)^{n+1}}=a\cdot(n+1)\cdot\frac{n^n}{(n+1)^{n+1}}.$$
Simplify: $(n+1)\cdot n^n/(n+1)^{n+1}=n^n/(n+1)^n=(n/(n+1))^n=(1+1/n)^{-n}$. Hence
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=a\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{-n}\longrightarrow a\cdot e^{-1}=\frac{a}{e}.$$

**Conclusion (d'Alembert's test).** If $\frac{a}{e}<1$, i.e. $a<e$, the series converges. If $\frac{a}{e}>1$, i.e. $a>e$, the series diverges.

**The critical case $a=e$.** The ratio test is inconclusive. Stirling's formula gives $n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n$, hence
$$u_n=\frac{n!\,e^n}{n^n}\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\longrightarrow+\infty,$$
so the series diverges.

**Conclusion.** The series converges if and only if $0<a<e$.

@@@ ratio-test-with-central-binomial.md
---
title: A ratio test with central binomial coefficients

tags: ratio test, factorials, Stirling formula, Wallis
date: 2026-11-02

## Statement

Study $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2\,4^n}$.

## Hint

Compute $\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}$. The ratio test is inconclusive; use Stirling's formula to find an equivalent of $u_n$.

## Solution

**Ratio.** With $u_n=\frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2\,4^n}$,
$$\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\frac{(2n+2)!}{((n+1)!)^2\,4^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{(n!)^2\,4^n}{(2n)!}=\frac{(2n+2)(2n+1)}{4(n+1)^2}=\frac{(2n+1)}{2(n+1)}.$$
As $n\to+\infty$, this ratio tends to $1$. d'Alembert's test is inconclusive.

**Stirling's formula.** $n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n$. Then
$$(2n)!\sim\sqrt{4\pi n}\left(\frac{2n}{e}\right)^{2n},\qquad (n!)^2\sim 2\pi n\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{2n}.$$
Hence
$$u_n=\frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2\,4^n}\sim\frac{\sqrt{4\pi n}\,(2n/e)^{2n}}{2\pi n\,(n/e)^{2n}\,4^n}=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi n}}{2\pi n}\cdot\frac{4^n}{4^n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}.$$

**Conclusion.** Since $u_n\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$, the series is comparable to the harmonic series, so it **diverges**.

**Remark.** This is a classic: the central binomial coefficient $\binom{2n}{n}\sim\frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$, so $u_n=\binom{2n}{n}/4^n\sim 1/\sqrt{\pi n}$.

## Statement

Study $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\left(\frac{n}{2n+1}\right)^n$.

## Hint

$(u_n)^{1/n}=\frac{n}{2n+1}\to\frac12$.

## Solution

**Computation.** With $u_n=\left(\frac{n}{2n+1}\right)^n$, the $n$-th root is
$$\sqrt[n]{u_n}=\frac{n}{2n+1}=\frac{1}{2+1/n}\longrightarrow\frac12.$$

## Statement

Study $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{(\ln n)^{\ln n}}$.

## Hint

$(\ln n)^{\ln n}=n^{\ln\ln n}$.

## Solution

**Rewrite.** Using $a^b=e^{b\ln a}$ with $a=\ln n$ and $b=\ln n$:
$$(\ln n)^{\ln n}=\exp(\ln n\cdot\ln\ln n)=n^{\ln\ln n}.$$
The general term is therefore
$$u_n=\frac{1}{n^{\ln\ln n}}.$$

**Remark.** This is a Bertrand-type series in disguise: the exponent $\ln\ln n$ is not constant, but it is eventually larger than any fixed exponent $>1$. Here it suffices that it eventually exceeds $2$.

## Statement

Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{(-1)^n}{\ln n}$ converges.

## Hint

The alternating series test applies: $\frac{1}{\ln n}$ is positive, decreasing for $n\ge2$, and tends to $0$.

## Solution

**The alternating series test (AST).** If $(b_n)$ is positive, decreasing, and tends to $0$, then $\sum(-1)^n b_n$ converges.

**Verification of the hypotheses.** With $b_n=\frac{1}{\ln n}$:
- $b_n>0$ for $n\ge2$.
- $\ln$ is increasing, so $\ln n$ is increasing, so $b_n$ is decreasing for $n\ge2$.
- $b_n\to0$ since $\ln n\to+\infty$.

By the AST, the series **converges**.

## Statement

Determine the nature of $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{n^\alpha(\ln n)^\beta}$ depending on the real parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

## Hint

Split into the cases $\alpha>1$, $\alpha<1$, and $\alpha=1$. In the last case, use the integral test.

## Solution

**Case $\alpha>1$.** Choose $\alpha'$ with $1<\alpha'<\alpha$. Since $\frac{\ln n}{n^{\alpha-\alpha'}}\to0$, we have $(\ln n)^\beta\ge1$ for large $n$, hence
$$\frac{1}{n^\alpha(\ln n)^\beta}\le\frac{1}{n^\alpha}\le\frac{1}{n^{\alpha'}}$$
for large $n$. Since $\sum\frac{1}{n^{\alpha'}}$ converges (Riemann, $\alpha'>1$), the series **converges**.

**Case $\alpha<1$.** Choose $\alpha'$ with $\alpha<\alpha'<1$. For large $n$, $(\ln n)^\beta\le n^{\alpha'-\alpha}$ (the logarithm grows slower than any positive power), hence
$$\frac{1}{n^\alpha(\ln n)^\beta}\ge\frac{1}{n^{\alpha'}}.$$
Since $\sum\frac{1}{n^{\alpha'}}$ diverges (Riemann, $\alpha'\le1$), the series **diverges**.

**Case $\alpha=1$.** The series becomes $\sum\frac{1}{n(\ln n)^\beta}$. Apply the integral test to $f(x)=\frac{1}{x(\ln x)^\beta}$, which is positive and decreasing for $x$ large enough. With $u=\ln x$:
$$\int_2^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^\beta}=\int_{\ln2}^{+\infty}\frac{du}{u^\beta}.$$
This converges iff $\beta>1$. So the series **converges iff $\beta>1$**, and **diverges iff $\beta\le1$**.

**Conclusion.** The Bertrand series converges if and only if
$$\alpha>1,\quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(\alpha=1\text{ and }\beta>1\bigr).$$
It diverges in all other cases.

## Statement

Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\sin n}{n}$ converges.

## Hint

The partial sums of $\sin n$ are bounded; $\frac1n$ decreases to $0$. Apply Dirichlet's test (Abel summation).

## Solution

**Bounded partial sums.** For every $n\ge1$,
$$\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sin k=\operatorname{Im}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{ik}\right)=\operatorname{Im}\left(e^{i}\frac{1-e^{in}}{1-e^{i}}\right).$$
Therefore
$$\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sin k\right|\le\frac{2}{|1-e^{i}|}=\frac{1}{\sin(1/2)}.$$
So the partial sums of $(\sin n)$ are bounded by $M=\frac{1}{\sin(1/2)}$.

By Dirichlet's test (a consequence of Abel summation), the series $\sum u_nv_n$ converges whenever the partial sums of $(u_n)$ are bounded and $(v_n)$ decreases to $0$. $$\left|\frac{\sin n}{n}\right|\ge\frac{\sin^2 n}{n}=\frac{1-\cos 2n}{2n}.$$

## Statement

Show that the series
$$\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1}{n}$$
restricted to the integers $n$ whose decimal expansion contains no digit $9$ converges, while the full harmonic series diverges.

## Hint

Count how many integers with $k$ digits contain no digit $9$, and bound the sum over that block.

## Solution

**Counting.** Among the $k$-digit integers (from $10^{k-1}$ to $10^k-1$), those whose decimal expansion contains no digit $9$ are formed by choosing the first digit among $1,\ldots,8$ (8 choices) and each of the remaining $k-1$ digits among $0,\ldots,8$ (9 choices each). So there are $8\cdot9^{k-1}$ such integers.

**Bounding the block.** Every $k$-digit integer is $\ge10^{k-1}$, so for each such integer $n$,
$$\frac1n\le\frac{1}{10^{k-1}}.$$
The sum over the $k$-digit integers without digit $9$ is therefore at most
$$8\cdot9^{k-1}\cdot\frac{1}{10^{k-1}}=8\left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^{k-1}.$$

**Summing over blocks.** The total sum $S$ over all integers without digit $9$ satisfies
$$S\le\sum_{k\ge1}8\left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^{k-1}=8\cdot\frac{1}{1-9/10}=80.$$
So the restricted series **converges**.

**Contrast.** The full harmonic series diverges (by comparison with $\ln n$). So removing the integers that contain a $9$ is enough to make the series converge: the "density" of integers without a $9$ decays exponentially in the number of digits, which kills the harmonic divergence.

**Remark.** The same argument works with any forbidden digit, or with any finite set of forbidden digits. This is a classical illustration of the subtlety of the harmonic series: even a very sparse subseries can diverge or converge depending on the density of the selected indices.
