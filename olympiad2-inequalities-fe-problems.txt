## Statement

Let $a_i,x_i,u_i\ge0$ for $i=1,\dots,n$. Prove Hölder's inequality
$$\left(\sum a_i^3\right)\left(\sum x_i^3\right)\left(\sum u_i^3\right)\ge\left(\sum a_ix_iu_i\right)^3.$$
Deduce that $(a^3+b^3+c^3)\cdot3\cdot3\ge(a+b+c)^3$ for $a,b,c\ge0$.

## Hint

Divide each sum by its total so that the three sums become $1$. Then apply AM-GM to $a_i^3,x_i^3,u_i^3$ (after normalisation) for each $i$ and add.

## Solution

**Idea.** Normalise, then use AM-GM on each triple.

**Normalisation.** Let $A=\sum a_i^3$, $X=\sum x_i^3$, $U=\sum u_i^3$. If one of them is $0$, the corresponding sequence is $0$ and both sides vanish. Otherwise, for each $i$ AM-GM on three non-negative numbers gives
$$\frac{a_i^3}A+\frac{x_i^3}X+\frac{u_i^3}U\ \ge\ 3\,\frac{a_ix_iu_i}{(AXU)^{1/3}}.$$

**Summation.** Summing over $i$, the left side is $1+1+1=3$, so
$$3\ge3\,\frac{\sum a_ix_iu_i}{(AXU)^{1/3}}\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum a_ix_iu_i\le(AXU)^{1/3},$$
which is the claim after cubing.

**Consequence.** Take $x_i=u_i=1$ and $n=3$: $(a^3+b^3+c^3)\cdot3\cdot3\ge(a+b+c)^3$, i.e. $a^3+b^3+c^3\ge\frac{(a+b+c)^3}9$.

**Remark.** Equality holds iff the three sequences $(a_i^3),(x_i^3),(u_i^3)$ are proportional.

**Lesson.** To prove a product-of-sums inequality, normalise every factor to $1$ and let AM-GM do the work term by term.

title: A symmetrisation trick: Σ a³/(a² + ab + b²)

## Statement

For $a,b,c>0$ prove that
$$\frac{a^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}+\frac{b^3}{b^2+bc+c^2}+\frac{c^3}{c^2+ca+a^2}\ \ge\ \frac{a+b+c}3.$$

## Hint

Compare the sum with the "twin" sum $\sum\frac{b^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}$ and look at the difference of the two.

## Solution

**Idea.** Let $S=\sum\frac{a^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}$ and $T=\sum\frac{b^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}$, both over the cyclic pairs $(a,b),(b,c),(c,a)$.

**The difference vanishes.** Since $a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)$,
$$S-T=\sum(a-b)=(a-b)+(b-c)+(c-a)=0.$$
So $S=T=\dfrac{S+T}2$ and
$$S=\frac12\sum_{\rm cyc}\frac{a^3+b^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}.$$

**A pair estimate.** For $a,b>0$,
$$\frac{a^3+b^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}\ge\frac{a+b}3\iff3(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)\ge(a+b)(a^2+ab+b^2)\iff2(a-b)^2\ge0.$$

**Conclusion.** Summing the three pair estimates, $S\ge\frac12\cdot\frac{2(a+b+c)}3=\frac{a+b+c}3$, with equality iff $a=b=c$.

**Lesson.** When a cyclic sum has numerators and denominators that differ by a nice factor, add the "mirror" sum: the difference telescopes and the symmetrised sum is easier to bound.

title: Muirhead's idea with weighted AM-GM

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$. Prove that
$$a^5+b^5+c^5\ \ge\ abc\,(a^2+b^2+c^2).$$

## Hint

Write $a^3bc=(a^5)^{3/5}(b^5)^{1/5}(c^5)^{1/5}$ and use weighted AM-GM.

## Solution

**Idea.** The exponent vector $(5,0,0)$ majorises $(3,1,1)$: each term $a^3bc$ is a weighted geometric mean of $a^5,b^5,c^5$.

**Weighted AM-GM.** With weights $\frac35,\frac15,\frac15$,
$$a^3bc=(a^5)^{3/5}(b^5)^{1/5}(c^5)^{1/5}\le\frac{3a^5+b^5+c^5}5.$$
The same holds for $b^3ca$ and $c^3ab$.

**Summation.**
$$abc(a^2+b^2+c^2)=a^3bc+b^3ca+c^3ab\le\frac{5(a^5+b^5+c^5)}5=a^5+b^5+c^5.$$
Equality iff $a=b=c$.

**Remark.** Muirhead's theorem says that $\sum_{\rm sym}a^{\alpha_1}b^{\alpha_2}c^{\alpha_3}\ge\sum_{\rm sym}a^{\beta_1}b^{\beta_2}c^{\beta_3}$ whenever $\alpha$ majorises $\beta$ (same total, partial sums of the sorted $\alpha$ are at least those of $\beta$). Each instance can be proved exactly as above.

**Lesson.** An inequality between symmetric sums of monomials of the same degree is an application of weighted AM-GM: find the weights that turn the smaller monomial into a geometric mean of the larger ones.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c\ge0$ with $a+b+c=3$. Prove that
$$a^2b+b^2c+c^2a+abc\le4.$$
When does equality hold?

## Hint

The expression is only cyclic, so you cannot assume $a\ge b\ge c$, but you can assume that $b$ is the middle value. Compare with $b(a+c)^2$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Choose the variable in the middle and merge the other two.

**Reduction.** The expression is invariant under the cyclic shift $(a,b,c)\to(b,c,a)$, and one of the three numbers is between the other two, so we may assume $b$ is between $a$ and $c$. Then $(a-b)(b-c)\ge0$.

**Key identity.** Expanding,
$$b(a+c)^2-\left(a^2b+b^2c+c^2a+abc\right)=abc+bc^2-b^2c-c^2a=c(a-b)(b-c)\ \ge0.$$
(To check the factorisation: $(a-b)(b-c)=ab-ac-b^2+bc$, and multiplying by $c$ gives $abc-ac^2-b^2c+bc^2$.)

**AM-GM.** Hence the expression is at most $b(a+c)^2=4\cdot b\cdot\frac{a+c}2\cdot\frac{a+c}2\le4\left(\frac{b+(a+c)}3\right)^3=4$, since $b+(a+c)=3$.

**Equality.** We need $c(a-b)(b-c)=0$ and $b=\frac{a+c}2=1$. This gives $a=b=c=1$ or $c=0$, $b=1$, $a=2$ (and the cyclic shifts of the latter).

**Lesson.** For cyclic inequalities, "middle variable" arguments replace the symmetry you do not have: the sign of $(a-b)(b-c)$ gives the one extra inequality you need.

## Statement

Let $x,y,z\ge0$ with $x+y+z=1$. Prove that
$$0\le xy+yz+zx-2xyz\le\frac7{27}.$$

## Hint

Expand $(1-2x)(1-2y)(1-2z)$ and compare with $xy+yz+zx-2xyz$.

## Solution

**Notation.** Let $q=xy+yz+zx$ and $r=xyz$.

**Lower bound.** Since $x,y,z\le1$,
$$q-2r=\bigl[xy(1-z)+yz(1-x)+zx(1-y)\bigr]+r\ge0,$$
because $xy(1-z)+yz(1-x)+zx(1-y)=q-3r$.

**Upper bound.** Expanding, using $x+y+z=1$,
$$(1-2x)(1-2y)(1-2z)=1-2+4q-8r=-1+4q-8r,$$
so $q-2r=\dfrac{1+P}4$ with $P=(1-2x)(1-2y)(1-2z)$. We must show $P\le\frac1{27}$.
- Two of the factors cannot be negative: $1-2x<0$ and $1-2y<0$ would give $x+y>1$, so $z<0$.
- If exactly one factor is negative, the others are non-negative and $P\le0$.
- If none is negative, their sum is $3-2=1$ and AM-GM gives $P\le\left(\frac13\right)^3=\frac1{27}$.

In all cases $P\le\frac1{27}$, hence $q-2r\le\frac{28}{27\cdot4}=\frac7{27}$.

**Equality.** Upper bound: $x=y=z=\frac13$. Lower bound: e.g. $(1,0,0)$.

**Lesson.** Rewriting a symmetric expression through $p=x+y+z$, $q$, $r$ (the pqr method) can turn it into a product whose sign analysis and AM-GM finish the job.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c$ be the sides of a triangle. Prove that
1. $abc\ge(a+b-c)(b+c-a)(c+a-b)$,
2. $\dfrac a{b+c-a}+\dfrac b{c+a-b}+\dfrac c{a+b-c}\ge3$.

## Hint

Set $x=\frac{b+c-a}2$, $y=\frac{c+a-b}2$, $z=\frac{a+b-c}2$. What do you know about $x,y,z$?

## Solution

**Ravi substitution.** The triangle inequalities say exactly that $x,y,z>0$, and conversely $a=y+z$, $b=z+x$, $c=x+y$. So the sides of a triangle correspond to arbitrary positive numbers.

**1.** In terms of $x,y,z$ the inequality reads
$$(x+y)(y+z)(z+x)\ge(2z)(2x)(2y)=8xyz,$$
which is true by AM-GM on each factor: $x+y\ge2\sqrt{xy}$, etc. Equality iff $x=y=z$, i.e. the triangle is equilateral.

**2.** Since $b+c-a=2x$, etc.,
$$\sum\frac a{b+c-a}=\frac{y+z}{2x}+\frac{z+x}{2y}+\frac{x+y}{2z}=\frac12\left[\left(\frac xy+\frac yx\right)+\left(\frac yz+\frac zy\right)+\left(\frac zx+\frac xz\right)\right]\ge\frac12\cdot6=3.$$

**Lesson.** A triangle condition is a hidden positivity condition: the Ravi substitution replaces it by three free positive variables, which makes many geometric inequalities routine.

title: Minkowski's inequality and a sum of two square roots

## Statement

1. Prove that for real numbers $p_1,q_1,p_2,q_2$,
$$\sqrt{p_1^2+q_1^2}+\sqrt{p_2^2+q_2^2}\ \ge\ \sqrt{(p_1+p_2)^2+(q_1+q_2)^2}.$$
2. Find the minimum over $x\in\mathbb{R}$ of $\sqrt{x^2+4}+\sqrt{(6-x)^2+9}$ and the $x$ where it is reached.

## Hint

For part 1, interpret $(p_i,q_i)$ as vectors and expand the square of the right-hand side. For part 2, apply part 1 with well-chosen $p_i,q_i$.

## Solution

**1.** Let $u=(p_1,q_1)$ and $v=(p_2,q_2)$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $u\cdot v\le|u||v|$, so
$$|u+v|^2=|u|^2+2u\cdot v+|v|^2\le|u|^2+2|u||v|+|v|^2=(|u|+|v|)^2.$$
Taking square roots gives the inequality. Equality iff $u,v$ are parallel with the same direction.

**2.** Write the expression as $\sqrt{x^2+2^2}+\sqrt{(6-x)^2+3^2}$ and apply part 1 with $(p_1,q_1)=(x,2)$ and $(p_2,q_2)=(6-x,3)$:
$$\ge\sqrt{6^2+5^2}=\sqrt{61}.$$

**Equality.** The vectors $(x,2)$ and $(6-x,3)$ must be parallel with the same direction: $3x=2(6-x)$, i.e. $x=\frac{12}5>0$ (and both vectors point up-right). So the minimum is $\sqrt{61}$, reached at $x=\frac{12}5$.

**Remark.** Geometrically, the expression is the length of the broken line from $(0,2)$ to $(x,0)$ to $(6,-3)$; it is shortest when the line is straight, of length $\sqrt{6^2+5^2}$. The Minkowski form avoids drawing the figure.

**Lesson.** A sum of square roots of sums of two squares is a sum of lengths: think "triangle inequality", and choose the vectors so that the unknown cancels in the sum.

## Statement

Prove that for all real numbers $a,b,c$,
$$a^4+b^4+c^4\ \ge\ abc\,(a+b+c).$$

## Hint

Show the chain $a^4+b^4+c^4\ge a^2b^2+b^2c^2+c^2a^2\ge abc(a+b+c)$, each step being a sum of squares.

## Solution

**Idea.** Prove a stronger intermediate inequality twice with $x^2+y^2+z^2\ge xy+yz+zx$.

**Basic fact.** For all real $x,y,z$,
$$x^2+y^2+z^2-xy-yz-zx=\frac12\left[(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2\right]\ge0.$$

**Step 1.** With $x=a^2$, $y=b^2$, $z=c^2$:
$$a^4+b^4+c^4\ge a^2b^2+b^2c^2+c^2a^2.$$

**Step 2.** With $x=ab$, $y=bc$, $z=ca$: $xy=ab^2c$, $yz=bc^2a$, $zx=ca^2b$, so
$$a^2b^2+b^2c^2+c^2a^2\ge ab^2c+abc^2+a^2bc=abc(a+b+c).$$

**Equality.** Step 1 is an equality iff $a^2=b^2=c^2$, and step 2 iff $ab=bc=ca$. Together, $|a|=|b|=|c|$ and the signs agree, so $a=b=c$.

**Lesson.** Look for a chain of simpler true inequalities, each of which is "square $\ge0$" after a substitution such as $x=ab$.

## Statement

Let $A,B,C$ be the angles of a triangle. Prove that
$$\sin A\sin B\sin C\le\frac{3\sqrt3}8.$$

## Hint

Take logarithms and show that $\ln\sin$ is concave on $(0,\pi)$.

## Solution

**Concavity.** On $(0,\pi)$ the function $g=\ln\sin$ has $g'=\cot$ and $g''=-\dfrac1{\sin^2}<0$, so $g$ is strictly concave.

**Jensen.** For the three angles with equal weights,
$$\frac{g(A)+g(B)+g(C)}3\le g\!\left(\frac{A+B+C}3\right)=g\!\left(\frac\pi3\right)=\ln\frac{\sqrt3}2.$$
Hence $\ln(\sin A\sin B\sin C)\le3\ln\frac{\sqrt3}2$, i.e.
$$\sin A\sin B\sin C\le\left(\frac{\sqrt3}2\right)^3=\frac{3\sqrt3}8.$$

**Equality.** By strict concavity, iff $A=B=C=\frac\pi3$.

**Remark.** The same method gives $\sin\frac A2\sin\frac B2\sin\frac C2\le\frac18$ (use concavity of $\ln\sin$ at $\frac\pi6$).

**Lesson.** A product under a fixed-sum constraint is a sum after taking logarithms: check the concavity of $\ln f$ and use Jensen.

title: Two inequalities by induction on the number of variables
category: olympiad/oly-inequalities
level: olympiad
difficulty: 4
tags: induction, AM-GM, Bernoulli, smoothing
date: 2026-11-05

## Statement

1. If $x_1,\dots,x_n>0$ and $x_1x_2\cdots x_n=1$, then $x_1+\dots+x_n\ge n$.
2. If $x_1,\dots,x_n>-1$ all have the same sign, then $(1+x_1)\cdots(1+x_n)\ge1+x_1+\dots+x_n$.

Deduce AM-GM from part 1.

## Hint

For part 1, among $n+1$ numbers with product $1$, one is $\ge1$ and one is $\le1$: merge them. For part 2, multiply the inductive inequality by $1+x_{n+1}>0$.

## Solution

**1.** For $n=1$, $x_1=1$. Assume it holds for $n$ numbers and take $x_1,\dots,x_{n+1}$ with product $1$. Not all are $<1$ and not all $>1$; reorder so that $x_n\ge1\ge x_{n+1}$. The $n$ numbers $x_1,\dots,x_{n-1},x_nx_{n+1}$ have product $1$, so by induction
$$x_1+\dots+x_{n-1}+x_nx_{n+1}\ge n.$$
Therefore
$$\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_i\ \ge\ n+x_n+x_{n+1}-x_nx_{n+1}=n+1+(x_n-1)(1-x_{n+1})\ \ge\ n+1.$$

**2.** For $n=1$ it is an equality. Suppose it holds for $n$, and let $S_n=x_1+\dots+x_n$. Since $1+x_{n+1}>0$,
$$\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}(1+x_i)\ge(1+x_{n+1})(1+S_n)=1+S_{n+1}+x_{n+1}S_n\ge1+S_{n+1},$$
because $x_{n+1}$ and $S_n$ have the same sign, so $x_{n+1}S_n\ge0$.

**AM-GM.** For positive $a_1,\dots,a_n$ with geometric mean $g$, set $x_i=a_i/g$: the product of the $x_i$ is $1$, so $\sum a_i/g\ge n$, i.e. $\frac{a_1+\dots+a_n}n\ge g$.

**Lesson.** Induction on the number of variables works when two variables can be merged into one while keeping the constraint: choose the pair on opposite sides of $1$.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$ with $abc=1$. Prove that
$$\frac1{a^2+a+1}+\frac1{b^2+b+1}+\frac1{c^2+c+1}\ge1.$$

## Hint

Clear the denominators and use $abc=1$ to simplify the many terms containing $abc$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The constraint kills most terms after expansion.

**Clearing denominators.** Let $D=\prod(a^2+a+1)$. The inequality is
$$\sum_{\rm cyc}(b^2+b+1)(c^2+c+1)-D\ge0.$$

**Expansion.** Expanding the left-hand side mechanically (checked with a computer algebra system) gives
$$-a^2b^2c^2-a^2b^2c-a^2bc^2-a^2bc+a^2-ab^2c^2-ab^2c-abc^2-abc+a+b^2+b+c^2+c+2.$$
Now substitute $abc=1$:
- $-a^2b^2c^2=-1$,
- $-a^2b^2c-a^2bc^2-ab^2c^2=-ab-ac-bc$,
- $-a^2bc-ab^2c-abc^2=-a-b-c$,
- $-abc=-1$.

Summing everything with the positive terms $a^2+b^2+c^2+a+b+c+2$:
$$a^2+b^2+c^2+a+b+c+2-1-(ab+bc+ca)-(a+b+c)-1=a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca\ \ge0.$$

**Equality.** Iff $a=b=c=1$.

**Lesson.** With a multiplicative constraint, expand fully and use $abc=1$ to cancel: often the inequality collapses to a classical one such as $a^2+b^2+c^2\ge ab+bc+ca$.

title: The cube substitution: Σ 1/(a + b + 1) ≤ 1

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$ with $abc=1$. Prove that
$$\frac1{a+b+1}+\frac1{b+c+1}+\frac1{c+a+1}\le1.$$

## Hint

Write $a=x^3$, $b=y^3$, $c=z^3$ with $xyz=1$, and use $x^3+y^3\ge xy(x+y)$.

## Solution

**Substitution.** Let $x=a^{1/3}$, $y=b^{1/3}$, $z=c^{1/3}$, so $xyz=1$.

**Key lemma.** For $x,y>0$, $x^3+y^3-xy(x+y)=(x+y)(x-y)^2\ge0$.

**Estimate.** Using $1=xyz$,
$$a+b+1=x^3+y^3+xyz\ge xy(x+y)+xyz=xy(x+y+z).$$
Since $xy=\frac1z$,
$$\frac1{a+b+1}\le\frac1{xy(x+y+z)}=\frac z{x+y+z}.$$

**Summation.** The same holds for the other two terms, with $x$ and $y$ in place of $z$, and adding gives
$$\sum\frac1{a+b+1}\le\frac{z+x+y}{x+y+z}=1.$$

**Equality.** Iff $x=y=z=1$.

**Lesson.** The constraint $abc=1$ with cubes in the lemma $x^3+y^3\ge xy(x+y)$ suggests the substitution $a=x^3$: the "1" becomes $xyz$ and the denominators become homogeneous.
