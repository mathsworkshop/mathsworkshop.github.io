## Statement

What is the last digit of $7^{2026}$?

## Hint

Write out the last digits of the first few powers of $7$ and look for a cycle.

## Solution

The last digits of $7^1,7^2,7^3,7^4$ are $7,9,3,1$, and $7^4\equiv1\pmod{10}$, so the pattern repeats with period $4$.

Since $2026=4\cdot506+2$,
$$7^{2026}=\left(7^4\right)^{506}\cdot7^2\equiv49\equiv9\pmod{10}.$$

The last digit is $9$.

## Statement

Show that no integer of the form $4k+3$ is a sum of two squares of integers.

## Hint

Find the possible remainders of a square modulo $4$.

## Solution

Let $m$ be an integer. If $m=2j$, then $m^2=4j^2\equiv0\pmod4$. If $m=2j+1$, then $m^2=4j(j+1)+1\equiv1\pmod4$. So a square is $\equiv0$ or $1\pmod4$.

A sum of two squares is therefore congruent to $0+0=0$, $0+1=1$ or $1+1=2$ modulo $4$, never to $3$. Hence $4k+3$ is never a sum of two squares.

## Statement

Prove that $\sqrt2$ is irrational.

## Hint

Suppose $\sqrt2=p/q$ in lowest terms. Square and use parity.

## Solution

**Contradiction.** Suppose $\sqrt2=p/q$ with $p,q\in\mathbb{N}^*$ coprime.

**Square.** $p^2=2q^2$, so $p^2$ is even, hence $p$ is even: $p=2k$.

**Substitute.** $4k^2=2q^2$, so $q^2=2k^2$, hence $q$ is even too.

**Contradiction.** $p$ and $q$ both even contradicts $\gcd(p,q)=1$.

**Conclusion.** $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb{Q}$.

## Statement

Prove that $\sqrt3$ is irrational.

Same structure as $\sqrt2$, but use divisibility by $3$ instead of parity.

## Solution

**Contradiction.** Suppose $\sqrt3=p/q$ with $p,q$ coprime.

**Square.** $p^2=3q^2$, so $3\mid p^2$, hence $3\mid p$ (since $3$ is prime). Write $p=3k$.

**Substitute.** $9k^2=3q^2$, so $q^2=3k^2$, hence $3\mid q$.

**Contradiction.** $3\mid p$ and $3\mid q$ contradict $\gcd(p,q)=1$.

**Conclusion.** $\sqrt3$ is irrational.

## Statement

Let $p$ be prime. Prove that $\sqrt p$ is irrational.

## Hint

Same argument as $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt3$, using Euclid's lemma: $p\mid n^2\Rightarrow p\mid n$.

## Solution

**Contradiction.** Suppose $\sqrt p=a/b$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$.

**Square.** $a^2=pb^2$, so $p\mid a^2$, hence $p\mid a$ (Euclid's lemma since $p$ is prime). Write $a=pk$.

**Substitute.** $p^2k^2=pb^2$, so $b^2=pk^2$, hence $p\mid b^2$, so $p\mid b$.

**Contradiction.** $\gcd(a,b)\ge p>1$, contradiction.

**Conclusion.** $\sqrt p$ is irrational.

## Statement

Let $n\in\mathbb{N}^*$ be a non-square. Prove that $\sqrt n$ is irrational.

## Hint

Use the prime factorization of $n$: some prime has an odd exponent. Apply the same argument as for $\sqrt p$.

## Solution

**Prime factorization.** $n=p_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}$. Since $n$ is not a square, some $\alpha_i$ is odd. Let $p=p_i$.

**Contradiction.** Suppose $\sqrt n=a/b$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$.

**Square.** $a^2=nb^2$, so $p\mid a^2$, hence $p\mid a$. Write $a=pk$.

**Substitute.** $p^2k^2=nb^2$, so $p^2\mid nb^2$, and $v_p(a^2)=2v_p(a)$ is even while $v_p(nb^2)=v_p(n)+2v_p(b)$ has parity $v_p(n)$ odd. Contradiction.

**Conclusion.** $\sqrt n$ is irrational.

## Statement

Show that $\log_{10}2$ is irrational.

## Hint

If $\log_{10}2=p/q$, then $10^p=2^q$, i.e. $2^p\cdot5^p=2^q$.

## Solution

**Assume.** $\log_{10}2=p/q$ with $p,q\in\mathbb{N}^*$, i.e. $2^{p/q}=10$, so $2^p=10^q=2^q\cdot5^q$.

## Statement

Prove that $e=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n!}$ is irrational.

## Hint

Suppose $e=p/q$. Multiply by $q!$ and bound the tail.

## Solution

**Assume.** $e=p/q$ with $p,q\in\mathbb{N}^*$.

**Multiply.** $q!e=q!p/q\in\mathbb{Z}$. Also $q!\sum_{n=0}^{q}\frac{1}{n!}\in\mathbb{Z}$ (each term is an integer multiple).

**Tail.** $q!e-q!\sum_{n=0}^{q}\frac{1}{n!}=\sum_{n=q+1}^{+\infty}\frac{q!}{n!}=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(q+1)\cdots(q+k)}$.

**Bound.** This tail is $<\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(q+1)^k}=\frac{1}{q}$.

**Contradiction.** The left side is a nonzero integer (since it's an integer minus an integer, and the tail is $>0$), but it is $<1/q\le1$. Contradiction.

**Conclusion.** $e$ is irrational.

## Statement

Show that $e^r$ is irrational for every nonzero rational $r$.

## Hint

Adapt the proof of irrationality of $e$ to $e^r=\sum r^n/n!$.

## Solution

**Setup.** Let $r=p/q$ with $p,q\in\mathbb{Z}$, $q>0$, $p\neq0$. Suppose $e^r=s/t$ with $s,t\in\mathbb{N}^*$.

**Multiply.** Compute $e^r$ as $\sum (p/q)^n/n!$. Multiply by $(qN)!/t$ or similar carefully chosen denominator to clear denominators.

**Standard result.** The proof (due to Lambert) shows that if $r\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$ and $e^r\in\mathbb{Q}$, then $r=0$. Since $r\neq0$, $e^r\notin\mathbb{Q}$.

**Conclusion.** $e^r$ is irrational for all nonzero rational $r$, in particular $e^2$ is irrational.

## Statement

State Lambert's theorem and deduce that $\pi$ is irrational.

## Hint

Lambert: if $x\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$ then $\tan x\notin\mathbb{Q}$. Since $\tan(\pi/4)=1\in\mathbb{Q}$, $\pi/4$ is not rational.

**Lambert's theorem.** For $x\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}$, $\tan x$ is irrational.

## Statement

Let $P(X)=a_nX^n+\cdots+a_0\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $a_n\neq0$. Show that any rational root of $P$ is of the form $p/q$ with $p\mid a_0$ and $q\mid a_n$.

## Hint

Write $P(p/q)=0$ and multiply by $q^n$; reduce modulo $p$ and modulo $q$.

## Solution

**Setup.** $P(p/q)=0$, $\gcd(p,q)=1$.

**Multiply.** $a_np^n+a_{n-1}p^{n-1}q+\cdots+a_0q^n=0$.

**$p\mid a_0$.** Mod $p$: $a_0q^n\equiv0\pmod p$. Since $\gcd(p,q)=1$, $p\mid a_0$.

**$q\mid a_n$.** Mod $q$: $a_np^n\equiv0\pmod q$. Since $\gcd(p,q)=1$, $q\mid a_n$.

**Conclusion.** Any rational root has the stated form.

**Corollary.** If $P$ is monic ($a_n=1$), any rational root is an integer dividing $a_0$.

Prove that $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is irrational.

## Solution

**Assume.** $p^3=2q^3$ with $\gcd(p,q)=1$.

## Statement

Show that the set $\{n\sqrt2\bmod1:n\in\mathbb{N}\}$ is dense in $[0,1]$.

## Hint

Use the pigeonhole principle: for any $N$, the fractional parts of $0,\sqrt2,\ldots,N\sqrt2$ are $N+1$ points in $[0,1)$, so two are within $1/N$.

## Solution

**Pigeonhole.** Among $\{0\},\{1\sqrt2\},\ldots,\{N\sqrt2\}$ (which are $N+1$ points in $[0,1)$), two differ by at most $1/N$ in fractional part. Say $\{k\sqrt2\}$ and $\{\ell\sqrt2\}$ with $0<\delta=\{(\ell-k)\sqrt2\}<1/N$ (taking absolute difference).

**Density.** Since $\sqrt2$ is irrational, $\delta\neq0$, and multiples of $\delta$ mod $1$ form a $\delta$-dense set in $[0,1)$.

**Conclusion.** For any $N$, we get $\delta<1/N$, so the set is dense.

## Statement

Explain why it is unknown whether $e+\pi$ and $e\pi$ are irrational.

## Hint

If one of them were rational, then $e$ and $\pi$ would be roots of a quadratic, but no such relation is known.

## Solution

**Known.** $e$ and $\pi$ are both irrational (indeed transcendental).

**If $e+\pi\in\mathbb{Q}$.** Then $e,\pi$ are roots of $X^2-sX+p=0$ where $s=e+\pi$ and $p=e\pi$. If both $s,p\in\mathbb{Q}$, then $e,\pi$ are algebraic — contradiction with transcendence.

**But only one is known?** Actually, if $e+\pi\in\mathbb{Q}$, then $e$ and $\pi$ are roots of $X^2-sX+p=0$ for some $p$. But we do not know $p=e\pi$ is rational.

**Open problem.** Whether $e+\pi$ and $e\pi$ are irrational is open. (However, at most one of them can be rational, since their sum and product being rational would force both to be algebraic.)

**Conclusion.** These are famous open problems in transcendence theory.

## Statement

Show that $L=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}10^{-n!}$ is irrational (and in fact transcendental).

## Hint

Rational numbers have eventually periodic decimals; $L$ has isolated $1$s at positions $n!$.

## Solution

**Structure.** $L=0.110001000000000000000001\ldots$ — $1$s at positions $1,2,6,24,120,\ldots$ and $0$s elsewhere.

**Not eventually periodic.** The gaps between $1$s increase without bound (the positions are factorials), so the decimal is not eventually periodic.

**Conclusion.** $L$ is irrational (indeed transcendental, by Liouville's approximation theorem).

**Liouville's theorem.** If $\alpha$ is algebraic of degree $d$, then $|\alpha-p/q|\ge c/q^d$ for all rationals $p/q$ (with $c$ depending only on $\alpha$). But $L$ can be approximated by truncated sums $p_N/q_N$ with $q_N=10^{N!}$ and error $<10^{-(N+1)!}<1/q_N^N$ for any $N$, so $L$ is transcendental.

Show that the continued fraction expansion of $\sqrt2$ is $[1;2,2,2,\ldots]$, and deduce that $\sqrt2$ is irrational.

## Statement

Show that a real number is rational iff its decimal expansion is eventually periodic.

## Hint

Forward: long division produces periodic remainders. Converse: compute $0.\overline{a_1\cdots a_k}$ as a geometric series.

## Solution

**Forward.** For $x=p/q$, long division: remainders mod $q$ are among $0,\ldots,q-1$, so by pigeonhole some remainder repeats within $q$ steps. From that point the decimal is periodic. (If the remainder $0$ is hit, the decimal is finite, considered periodic with period $0$.)

**Converse.** Up to adding an integer to $x$, we can assume $x=0.\overline{a_1\cdots a_k}$. Multiplying $x$ by $10^k$ and subtracting $x$ gives $x(10^k-1)=\overline{a_1\cdots a_k} \in \mathbb{N}$, so $x=\frac{\overline{a_1\cdots a_k}}{10^k-1}\in\mathbb{Q}$.

**Conclusion.** Rationals are exactly the eventually periodic decimals.

## Statement

Let $\varphi$ be Euler's totient function ($\varphi(d)$ is the number of integers $a$ with $1\le a\le d$ and $\gcd(a,d)=1$). Prove that for every positive integer $n$,
$$\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n.$$

## Hint

Look at the $n$ fractions $\frac1n,\frac2n,\dots,\frac nn$ and write each in lowest terms. What can you say about the denominators?

## Solution

**Idea.** Count the same set of $n$ objects in two ways: all together, and grouped according to the denominator in lowest terms.

**Grouping.** Take $k\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ and write $\frac kn=\frac ad$ in lowest terms, i.e. $\gcd(a,d)=1$ and $1\le a\le d$. The denominator $d$ divides $n$ (because $\frac kn=\frac ad$ gives $kd=an$, and $\gcd(a,d)=1$ forces $d\mid n$).

**Counting a group.** Fix a divisor $d$ of $n$. A fraction $\frac kn$ has lowest-terms denominator $d$ exactly when it equals $\frac ad$ with $1\le a\le d$ and $\gcd(a,d)=1$. Each such $a$ gives exactly one $k=a\cdot\frac nd$, and distinct $a$ give distinct fractions. Hence exactly $\varphi(d)$ of the fractions $\frac kn$ have denominator $d$.

**Conclusion.** Every one of the $n$ fractions has exactly one lowest-terms denominator $d\mid n$, so
$$n=\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d).$$

## Statement

Let $a,b$ be coprime positive integers. Prove that $ab-a-b$ cannot be written as $ax+by$ with integers $x,y\ge0$, but every integer $n>ab-a-b$ can.

## Hint

Given $n$, choose the representation $n=ax+by$ in which $0\le x\le b-1$. When is $y\ge0$?

## Solution

**Normalising.** Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$, $a$ is invertible modulo $b$, so for each integer $n$ there is a unique $x_0\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}$ with $ax_0\equiv n\pmod b$. Put $y_0=\frac{n-ax_0}b\in\mathbb Z$. Every integer solution of $ax+by=n$ is $(x_0+tb,\ y_0-ta)$ for $t\in\mathbb Z$.

$$y_0=\frac{n-ax_0}b\ge\frac{n-a(b-1)}b>\frac{ab-a-b-ab+a}b=-1,$$
so $y_0>-1$, and being an integer, $y_0\ge0$.

**Example.** For $a=5$, $b=7$ the largest amount that cannot be paid is $35-12=23$.

**Lesson.** Reduce one variable modulo the other coefficient to get a canonical representative, then the sign of the other variable decides everything.

## Statement

Let $p$ be a prime and $a,b$ integers with $a^p\equiv b^p\pmod p$. Prove that $a^p\equiv b^p\pmod{p^2}$.

## Hint

By Fermat's little theorem $a\equiv b\pmod p$. Write $a=b+kp$ and expand $(b+kp)^p$.

## Solution

**Step 1.** By Fermat's little theorem $a^p\equiv a$ and $b^p\equiv b\pmod p$. The hypothesis gives $a\equiv b\pmod p$, so $a=b+kp$ for some integer $k$.

**Step 2: binomial expansion.**
$$a^p=(b+kp)^p=b^p+\binom p1b^{p-1}kp+\sum_{j\ge2}\binom pjb^{p-j}(kp)^j.$$
The second term is $p\cdot b^{p-1}\cdot kp$, a multiple of $p^2$. For $j\ge2$ the factor $(kp)^j$ is a multiple of $p^2$. Hence $a^p\equiv b^p\pmod{p^2}$.

**Example.** $17\equiv2\pmod5$, and indeed $17^5-2^5=1419825=25\cdot56793$.

**Remark.** First step of "lifting the exponent": $v_p(a^p-b^p)=v_p(a-b)+1$ when $p\mid a-b$ and $p$ is odd.

**Lesson.** When two numbers are congruent modulo $p$, write one as the other plus a multiple of $p$; the binomial expansion then shows the $p$-th powers agree one power of $p$ higher.

## Statement

Let $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$, $F_{k+2}=F_{k+1}+F_k$. Prove that for all positive integers $m,n$,
$$\gcd(F_m,F_n)=F_{\gcd(m,n)}.$$

## Hint

Prove first the addition formula $F_{a+b}=F_aF_{b+1}+F_{a-1}F_b$, and that consecutive Fibonacci numbers are coprime. Then imitate the Euclidean algorithm.

## Solution

**Step 1: addition formula.** For $a\ge1$ and $b\ge0$: $F_{a+b}=F_aF_{b+1}+F_{a-1}F_b$. Induction on $b$.

**Step 2: consecutive terms are coprime.** $\gcd(F_{k+1},F_k)=\gcd(F_{k-1},F_k)=\dots=\gcd(F_1,F_0)=1$.

**Step 3: subtractive Euclid.** Let $m>n\ge1$. Apply the formula with $a=m-n\ge1$, $b=n$:
$$F_m=F_{m-n}F_{n+1}+F_{m-n-1}F_n.$$
Hence $\gcd(F_m,F_n)=\gcd(F_{m-n}F_{n+1},F_n)$. Since $\gcd(F_{n+1},F_n)=1$ (Step 2), any common divisor of $F_{m-n}F_{n+1}$ and $F_n$ is a common divisor of $F_{m-n}$ and $F_n$, so
$$\gcd(F_m,F_n)=\gcd(F_{m-n},F_n).$$

**Step 4: conclusion.** Applying Step 3 repeatedly mimics the subtractive Euclidean algorithm on the indices; it ends at $(d,d)$ with $d=\gcd(m,n)$, and $\gcd(F_d,F_d)=F_d$.

**Check.** $\gcd(F_{12},F_{18})=\gcd(144,2584)=8=F_6$.

**Remark.** Consequently, if $F_n$ is prime then $n$ is prime, or $n=4$.

**Lesson.** A sequence with the "addition formula" structure inherits the Euclidean algorithm on its indices.

## Statement

Prove that for every integer $k\ge0$, $3^{k+1}\mid2^{3^k}+1$. Deduce that $n=3^k$ satisfies $n\mid2^n+1$, so there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ with $n\mid2^n+1$.

## Hint

Use induction and the factorisation $x^3+1=(x+1)(x^2-x+1)$ with $x=2^{3^k}$.

## Solution

**Base case.** $k=0$: $3^1\mid2^1+1=3$ ✓.

**Induction step.** Assume $3^{k+1}\mid2^{3^k}+1$, and let $x=2^{3^k}$. Then
$$2^{3^{k+1}}+1=x^3+1=(x+1)(x^2-x+1).$$
The first factor is divisible by $3^{k+1}$. For the second, $x\equiv-1\pmod3$, so $x^2-x+1\equiv1+1+1=3\equiv0\pmod3$. Therefore $3^{k+2}$ divides the product.

**Conclusion.** So $3^{k+1}\mid2^{3^k}+1$, in particular $3^k\mid2^{3^k}+1$: with $n=3^k$, $n\mid2^n+1$. There are infinitely many such $n$.

**Check.** $k=2$: $2^9+1=513=27\cdot19$ ✓.

**Lesson.** To gain one more power of a prime, factor $x^p\pm1$ and show that the cofactor is divisible by $p$ exactly once.

## Statement

Prove that there are infinitely many primes $p\equiv1\pmod4$.

## Hint

Take a prime divisor $q$ of $(n!)^2+1$. What is the order of $n!$ modulo $q$?

## Solution

**Construction.** For $n\ge2$ let $N=(n!)^2+1$ and let $q$ be any prime divisor of $N$.

**$q>n$.** If $q\le n$, then $q\mid n!$, so $N\equiv1\pmod q$, contradicting $q\mid N$.

**$q$ is odd.** For $n\ge2$, $n!$ is even, so $N$ is odd.

**The order of $x=n!$ modulo $q$.** We have $x^2\equiv-1\not\equiv1\pmod q$ ($q$ odd), and $x^4\equiv1$. So the order of $x$ is exactly $4$. By Fermat's little theorem $x^{q-1}\equiv1$, hence $4\mid q-1$, i.e. $q\equiv1\pmod4$.

**Conclusion.** For every $n\ge2$ there is a prime $q\equiv1\pmod4$ with $q>n$. So there are infinitely many.

**Check.** $n=3$: $37=36+1$, and $37\equiv1\pmod4$. $n=4$: $577$ is prime.

**Lesson.** If $x^2\equiv-1\pmod q$, then $x$ has order $4$ and $4\mid q-1$.

## Statement

Let $a\ge2$ and $n\ge1$ be integers. Prove that $n\mid\varphi(a^n-1)$.

## Hint

Let $N=a^n-1$. Compute the order of $a$ modulo $N$ exactly.

## Solution

**Setup.** Put $N=a^n-1\ge1$. If $N=1$ then $\varphi(1)=1$ and $1\mid1$, fine. Assume $N\ge2$.

**Coprimality.** $a\cdot a^{n-1}=a^n\equiv1\pmod N$, so $a$ is invertible modulo $N$.

**The order is $n$.** $a^n\equiv1\pmod N$. If $1\le k<n$, then $0<a^k-1<a^n-1=N$, so $N\nmid a^k-1$, i.e. $a^k\not\equiv1\pmod N$. Hence the order of $a$ modulo $N$ is exactly $n$.

**Euler.** $a^{\varphi(N)}\equiv1\pmod N$, so the order $n$ divides $\varphi(N)=\varphi(a^n-1)$.

**Check.** $a=2$, $n=4$: $N=15$, $\varphi(15)=8$, $4\mid8$ ✓. $a=3$, $n=3$: $N=26$, $\varphi(26)=12$, $3\mid12$ ✓.

**Lesson.** To show $n\mid\varphi(N)$, exhibit an element of order exactly $n$ modulo $N$.

## Statement

Let $p$ be a prime. Prove that there exists an integer $g$ whose multiplicative order modulo $p$ is $p-1$ (a **primitive root**).

## Hint

For $d\mid p-1$ let $\psi(d)$ be the number of residues of order $d$. Show $\psi(d)\le\varphi(d)$ using that a polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots modulo $p$, and compare $\sum\psi(d)$ with $\sum\varphi(d)$.

## Solution

**Setup.** Work in the field $\mathbb F_p$. Every nonzero residue has an order dividing $p-1$ (Fermat), so
$$\sum_{d\mid p-1}\psi(d)=p-1.$$

**Claim: $\psi(d)\le\varphi(d)$ for all $d\mid p-1$.** If $\psi(d)=0$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise let $g$ have order $d$. The $d$ numbers $1,g,\dots,g^{d-1}$ are pairwise distinct and all satisfy $x^d=1$. A polynomial of degree $d$ over a field has at most $d$ roots, so these are *all* the roots of $x^d-1$. Any element of order $d$ is a root of $x^d-1$, hence of the form $g^j$ with $0\le j<d$. The order of $g^j$ is $\frac d{\gcd(j,d)}$, which equals $d$ iff $\gcd(j,d)=1$. So $\psi(d)=\varphi(d)$ in this case.

**Comparison.** We know $\sum_{d\mid p-1}\varphi(d)=p-1$ (the sum of $\varphi$ over the divisors of $p-1$). Combined with $\psi(d)\le\varphi(d)$ and $\sum\psi(d)=p-1$, equality must hold for every $d$:
$$\psi(d)=\varphi(d)\quad\text{for all }d\mid p-1.$$
In particular $\psi(p-1)=\varphi(p-1)\ge1$: a primitive root exists.

**Example.** $p=7$: $3$ is a primitive root ($3,2,6,4,5,1$), and there are $\varphi(6)=2$ of them ($3$ and $5$).

**Lesson.** Count elements of each order, bound each count by a polynomial-root argument, and use $\sum_{d\mid m}\varphi(d)=m$ to force equality.

## Statement

Let $p$ be an odd prime and $q$ a prime dividing $2^p-1$.

1. Prove that $q\equiv1\pmod{2p}$.
2. Use this to show that $8191=2^{13}-1$ is prime.

## Hint

The order of $2$ modulo $q$ divides $p$. It cannot be $1$.

## Solution

**1.** $q$ is odd. From $2^p\equiv1\pmod q$, the order $d$ of $2$ modulo $q$ divides $p$, so $d\in\{1,p\}$. If $d=1$ then $2\equiv1\pmod q$, impossible. So $d=p$. By Fermat's little theorem $2^{q-1}\equiv1$, hence $p\mid q-1$. Since $q-1$ is even and $p$ is odd, $2p\mid q-1$.

**Another check.** $2^{11}-1=2047=23\cdot89$, and $23=1+2\cdot11$, $89=1+8\cdot11$ ✓.

**Lesson.** The multiplicative order of $a$ modulo a prime $q\mid a^p-1$ is $p$, so $q\equiv1\pmod p$.

## Statement

Let $p\equiv3\pmod4$ be a prime and $m=\frac{p-1}2$. Prove that $m!\equiv\pm1\pmod p$.

## Hint

Pair $k$ with $p-k$ in Wilson's product $(p-1)!$.

## Solution

**Pairing.** Wilson's theorem: $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$. Write
$$(p-1)!=\prod_{k=1}^m k\cdot\prod_{k=1}^m(p-k)\equiv\prod_{k=1}^mk\cdot\prod_{k=1}^m(-k)=(-1)^m(m!)^2\pmod p.$$

**Parity of $m$.** Since $p\equiv3\pmod4$, $m=\frac{p-1}2$ is odd, so $(-1)^m=-1$.

**Conclusion.** $-1\equiv-(m!)^2$, i.e. $(m!)^2\equiv1\pmod p$. Since $p$ is prime, $m!\equiv\pm1\pmod p$.

**Check.** $p=7$: $3!=6\equiv-1$. $p=11$: $5!=120\equiv-1$. $p=23$: $11!\equiv1\pmod{23}$.

**Remark.** For $p\equiv1\pmod4$ the same computation gives $(m!)^2\equiv-1$, which is the key to writing such a prime as a sum of two squares.

**Lesson.** Wilson's theorem plus the pairing $k\leftrightarrow p-k$ turns $(p-1)!$ into a square (up to sign) of half the product.

## Statement

Let $a,b$ be positive integers. Prove that
$$\gcd(2^a-1,\,2^b-1)=2^{\gcd(a,b)}-1. $$
Deduce that $2^a-1$ divides $2^b-1$ if and only if $a$ divides $b$.

## Hint

Suppose $a>b$. Write $2^a-1=2^{a-b}(2^b-1)+(2^{a-b}-1)$ and compare with the Euclidean algorithm.

## Solution

**Idea.** The Euclidean algorithm on the exponents is mirrored by the Euclidean algorithm on the numbers $2^k-1$.

**Key step.** For $a>b$,
$$2^a-1=2^{a-b}\bigl(2^b-1\bigr)+\bigl(2^{a-b}-1\bigr),$$
so $\gcd(2^a-1,2^b-1)=\gcd(2^b-1,2^{a-b}-1)$, because $\gcd(u,v)=\gcd(v,u-qv)$ for any integer $q$. Thus the pair of exponents $(a,b)$ is replaced by $(b,a-b)$.

**Conclusion.** Repeating the subtraction step, the exponents follow the subtractive Euclidean algorithm and end at $(g,g)$ with $g=\gcd(a,b)$ (or at $(g,0)$ if one uses $\gcd(u,0)=u$, where $2^0-1=0$). Hence the gcd equals $2^g-1$.

**Divisibility.** $2^a-1\mid2^b-1\iff\gcd(2^a-1,2^b-1)=2^a-1\iff2^{\gcd(a,b)}-1=2^a-1\iff\gcd(a,b)=a\iff a\mid b$.

**Check.** $\gcd(2^{12}-1,2^{18}-1)=\gcd(4095,262143)=63=2^6-1$ ✓.

**Lesson.** When a gcd involves a parametrised expression, show that the Euclidean algorithm on the parameter reproduces the Euclidean algorithm on the values.

## Statement

Find all positive integers $n$ such that $n$ divides $2^n-1$.

## Hint

If $n>1$, look at the smallest prime divisor $p$ of $n$ and at the order of $2$ modulo $p$.

## Solution

**Answer.** Only $n=1$.

**Idea.** The order of $2$ modulo the smallest prime factor of $n$ must be a divisor of $n$ smaller than that prime.

**Proof.** Let $n>1$ with $n\mid2^n-1$ and let $p$ be the smallest prime divisor of $n$. Since $2^n-1$ is odd, $p$ is odd. Let $d$ be the order of $2$ modulo $p$, i.e. the least $d\ge1$ with $2^d\equiv1\pmod p$.

- From $2^n\equiv1\pmod p$ we get $d\mid n$.
- From Fermat's little theorem $2^{p-1}\equiv1$ we get $d\mid p-1$.

So $d$ divides both $n$ and $p-1$. Now $d\ne1$ because $2\not\equiv1\pmod p$. Hence $d>1$ has a prime divisor $q$ with $q\le d\le p-1<p$, and $q\mid d\mid n$. This contradicts the minimality of $p$.

**Check.** $n=1$ works trivially; for instance $n=3$ fails since $2^3-1=7$.

**Remark.** The same argument shows that $n\mid2^n-1$ cannot hold for $n>1$, but $n\mid2^n+1$ has infinitely many solutions (e.g. $n=3,9,27,\dots$).

**Lesson.** "Take the smallest prime divisor and use orders" is the standard way to show that a divisibility $n\mid a^n-1$ is impossible.

## Statement

Prove that the only integer solution of $x^2+y^2=3z^2$ is $x=y=z=0$.

## Hint

Squares modulo $3$ are $0$ or $1$. What does the equation say modulo $3$?

## Solution

**Idea.** Descent: from a non-zero solution build a strictly smaller one.

**Proof.** Suppose there is a solution other than $(0,0,0)$, and choose one with $|z|$ smallest positive (if $z=0$ then $x^2+y^2=0$ forces $x=y=0$).

**Modulo $3$.** $x^2+y^2\equiv0\pmod3$. Since squares are $0$ or $1$ modulo $3$, the sum is $0$ only if both are $0$, so $3\mid x$ and $3\mid y$. Write $x=3x_1$, $y=3y_1$:
$$9(x_1^2+y_1^2)=3z^2\ \Longrightarrow\ z^2=3(x_1^2+y_1^2),$$
so $3\mid z$; write $z=3z_1$. Then $9z_1^2=3(x_1^2+y_1^2)$, i.e.
$$x_1^2+y_1^2=3z_1^2,$$
a solution with $|z_1|=|z|/3<|z|$ and $z_1\ne0$. Contradiction with minimality.

**Lesson.** Infinite descent: reduce modulo a well-chosen number, show all variables are divisible by it, divide, and get a smaller solution.

## Statement

Let $x,y,z$ be positive integers with $x^2+y^2=z^2$. Prove that $xyz$ is divisible by $60$.

## Hint

Since $60=3\cdot4\cdot5$, prove separately that $3$, $4$ and $5$ divide $xyz$, looking at the possible residues of squares modulo $3$, $8$ and $5$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Squares have very few residues, so each prime power can be handled by a short case check.

**Modulo $3$.** Squares are $0$ or $1$. If $3\nmid x$ and $3\nmid y$, then $z^2\equiv1+1=2\pmod3$, impossible. So $3\mid xy$.

**Modulo $5$.** Squares are $0,1,4\equiv-1$. If $5\nmid xyz$, then $x^2,y^2,z^2\in\{\pm1\}$ and $x^2+y^2\in\{-2,0,2\}$ is never $\equiv\pm1$. So $5\mid xyz$.

- If $x,y$ are both even, then $4\mid xy$.
- Otherwise exactly one is even, say $x$, and $y,z$ are odd. Odd squares are $\equiv1\pmod8$, so $x^2=z^2-y^2\equiv0\pmod8$. Writing $x=2m$, $4m^2\equiv0\pmod8$ forces $m$ even, so $4\mid x$.

**Conclusion.** $3$, $4$ and $5$ are pairwise coprime and each divides $xyz$, so $60\mid xyz$.

**Check.** $(3,4,5)$: $60$. $(5,12,13)$: $780=13\cdot60$. $(8,15,17)$: $2040=34\cdot60$ ✓.

**Lesson.** Reduce to the list of squares modulo $3$, $4$ (or $8$) and $5$: most "which numbers divide..." questions about squares are closed by such a check.

## Statement

Prove that there are infinitely many prime numbers congruent to $3$ modulo $4$.

## Hint

Imitate Euclid. Assume there are finitely many, $p_1,\dots,p_r$ (including $3$), and consider $N=4p_1p_2\cdots p_r-1$.

**Idea.** An integer $\equiv3\pmod4$ cannot be a product of primes all $\equiv1\pmod4$.

**Proof.** Suppose the primes $\equiv3\pmod4$ are exactly $p_1,\dots,p_r$, and let $N=4p_1\cdots p_r-1$. Then $N\ge11$, $N$ is odd, and $N\equiv3\pmod4$.

**Remark.** The same trick fails for primes $\equiv1\pmod4$ (a product of primes $\equiv3$ can be $\equiv1$); a different argument (using $x^2+1$) is needed there. Dirichlet's theorem handles all progressions $a\bmod m$ with $\gcd(a,m)=1$.

## Statement

1. Prove that for a prime $p$, $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$.
2. Prove that an integer $n>1$ is prime if and only if $n\mid(n-1)!+1$.

## Hint

In $\{2,\dots,p-2\}$ pair each number with its inverse modulo $p$. Which numbers are their own inverse?

## Solution

**1.** For $p=2,3$ the claim is checked directly ($1!=1\equiv-1\pmod2$, $2!=2\equiv-1\pmod3$). Let $p\ge5$. Each $a\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}$ has a unique inverse $a^{-1}$ in $\{1,\dots,p-1\}$ modulo $p$. We have $a=a^{-1}$ iff $a^2\equiv1$ iff $p\mid(a-1)(a+1)$ iff $a\equiv\pm1$, i.e. $a\in\{1,p-1\}$.

Hence the $p-3$ numbers $2,3,\dots,p-2$ split into pairs $\{a,a^{-1}\}$ with product $\equiv1$, so $2\cdot3\cdots(p-2)\equiv1$, and
$$(p-1)!\equiv1\cdot1\cdot(p-1)\equiv-1\pmod p.$$

**2.** ($\Rightarrow$) is part 1. ($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $n$ is composite, with a divisor $d$ such that $1<d<n$. Then $d\mid(n-1)!$, so $d\nmid(n-1)!+1$, and therefore $n\nmid(n-1)!+1$. Hence $n\mid(n-1)!+1$ forces $n$ prime.

**Remark.** Wilson's criterion is a perfect primality test in theory, but useless in practice because $(n-1)!$ is huge.

**Lesson.** In a group of residues, pair every element with its inverse: only the elements with $a^2=1$ survive.

**Idea.** Assume $k$ is not a perfect square. Among all pairs of positive integers $(a,b)$ with $a^2+b^2=k(ab+1)$ pick one with $a+b$ minimal, and with $a\ge b$. We build a smaller pair.

**Case $a=b$.** Then $2a^2=k(a^2+1)$, so $k=\frac{2a^2}{a^2+1}<2$. As $k$ is a positive integer, $k=1$, a perfect square. So assume $a>b$.

**The jump.** Regard the equation as $x^2-kb\,x+(b^2-k)=0$ in the unknown $x$. It has the root $x=a$; by Vieta the other root is
$$a'=kb-a=\frac{b^2-k}{a},$$
an integer.

- $a'>0$: if $a'<0$ then $a'\le-1$, and from $a'^2+kb|a'|+b^2-k=0$ (the equation with $x=a'$) together with $kb|a'|\ge k$ we get $a'^2+b^2\le0$, impossible. If $a'=0$ then $b^2=k$ would be a square, excluded. So $a'\ge1$.
- $a'<b$: indeed $a'=\dfrac{b^2-k}a<\dfrac{b^2}a\le b$ since $a\ge b$.

Thus $(a',b)$ is another pair of positive integers satisfying the equation, with $a'+b<a+b$. This contradicts the minimality of $a+b$.

**Conclusion.** $k$ must be a perfect square.

**Check.** $(a,b)=(8,2)$: $\frac{64+4}{17}=4$; $(27,3)$: $\frac{738}{82}=9$; $(30,8)$: $\frac{964}{241}=4$ ✓.

**Remark.** This is IMO 1988 Problem 6, the most famous Vieta jumping problem.

**Lesson.** Vieta jumping: view the equation as a quadratic in one variable, use the second root to produce a smaller solution, and conclude by minimality.

## Solution

**Idea.** The two numbers are very close; an integer between them would be a square near $4n+2$, and $4n+2$ is never a square.

**Upper comparison.** $(\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1})^2=2n+1+2\sqrt{n(n+1)}$, and $n(n+1)<\left(n+\frac12\right)^2$, so $2\sqrt{n(n+1)}<2n+1$ and
$$(\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1})^2<4n+2.$$

**Lower comparison.** $n(n+1)>n^2$ so $2\sqrt{n(n+1)}>2n$ and
$$(\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1})^2>4n+1.$$

**No integer in between.** Suppose an integer $k$ satisfies $\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1}<k\le\sqrt{4n+2}$. Squaring, $4n+1<k^2\le4n+2$, so $k^2=4n+2$. But squares are $\equiv0,1\pmod4$ while $4n+2\equiv2$. Contradiction.

**Conclusion.** The numbers $\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1}\le\sqrt{4n+2}$ have no integer in $(\sqrt n+\sqrt{n+1},\sqrt{4n+2}]$, so their floors coincide.

**Check.** $n=1$: $1+1.414=2.414$, $\sqrt6=2.449$, both floors $2$.

**Lesson.** To compare floors of two close irrational expressions, show that no integer (or the square of an integer) can separate them, often with a congruence argument.

## Statement

Prove that for every positive integer $n$, $n!+1$ and $(n+1)!+1$ are coprime.

## Hint

Let $d$ divide both. Express $(n+1)!+1$ in terms of $n!$ and reduce modulo $d$.

## Solution

**Proof.** Let $d$ be a common divisor. Then $n!\equiv-1\pmod d$. Since $(n+1)!=(n+1)\,n!$,
$$0\equiv(n+1)!+1\equiv-(n+1)+1=-n\pmod d,$$
so $d\mid n$. But $n\mid n!$, hence $d\mid n!$. Together with $d\mid n!+1$ this gives $d\mid1$.

**Check.** $n=3$: $\gcd(7,25)=1$; $n=4$: $\gcd(25,121)=1$ ✓.

**Remark.** Equivalently, $\gcd(n!+1,(n+1)!+1)=\gcd(n!+1,n)=1$ by the Euclidean algorithm.

**Lesson.** To prove coprimality, assume a common divisor $d$, reduce one expression modulo $d$ using the other, and show that $d$ divides $1$.

## Statement

Prove that no integer of the form $8k+7$ is a sum of three squares of integers.

## Hint

What are the possible values of a square modulo $8$?

## Solution

**Squares modulo 8.** For $x$ odd, $x^2\equiv1\pmod8$; for $x\equiv2,6\pmod8$, $x^2\equiv4$; for $x\equiv0,4\pmod8$, $x^2\equiv0$. So squares are $0,1,4$ modulo $8$.

**Sums of three.** The sums of three elements of $\{0,1,4\}$ are:
$$0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 8\equiv0,\ 9\equiv1,\ 12\equiv4.$$
Precisely, the possible sums are the residues $0,1,2,3,4,5,6$ modulo $8$. The residue $7$ is never obtained (for instance $4+1+1=6$, $4+4+1=9\equiv1$, $1+1+1=3$).

**Conclusion.** No sum of three squares is $\equiv7\pmod8$.

**Remark.** Legendre's three-square theorem states the converse: a positive integer is a sum of three squares iff it is not of the form $4^a(8b+7)$. For example $15$, $23$, $28=4\cdot7$ are not sums of three squares.

**Lesson.** List the residues of squares modulo $8$ ($0,1,4$) and check which sums are attainable: the standard way to rule out representations.
