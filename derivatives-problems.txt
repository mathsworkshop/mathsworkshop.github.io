## Statement

Let $f(x)=5x^3+7x^2-4x+1$. Compute $f'(x)$ and find the equation of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point with abscissa $a=2$.

## Hint

Use $(x^n)'=nx^{n-1}$ and the tangent line equation $y=f'(a)(x-a)+f(a)$.

## Solution

**Derivative.** Differentiating term by term, $f'(x)=15x^2+14x-4$.

**At $a=2$.**
$$f'(2)=60+28-4=84,\qquad f(2)=40+28-8+1=61.$$

**Tangent line.** $y=84(x-2)+61$, that is
$$y=84x-107.$$

**Meaning.** Near $x=2$ the curve is almost indistinguishable from this line: $f(2+h)\approx61+84h$ for small $h$. Derivatives are, above all, slopes of best linear approximations.

## Statement

Let $f(x)=\dfrac{1-x}{4+x}$. Compute $f'(x)$ and give its domain of definition.

## Hint

Use $\left(\dfrac uv\right)'=\dfrac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$ with $u=1-x$ and $v=4+x$.

## Solution

We have $u'=-1$ and $v'=1$, so
$$f'(x)=\frac{(-1)(4+x)-(1-x)(1)}{(4+x)^2}=\frac{-4-x-1+x}{(4+x)^2}=\frac{-5}{(4+x)^2}.$$
The function $f$ is defined on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-4\}$, and $f'$ is defined on the same set.

**Remark.** Since $f'<0$ everywhere on its domain, $f$ is decreasing on each of the intervals $(-\infty,-4)$ and $(-4,+\infty)$. Be careful: it is *not* decreasing on the union, as $f(-5)=-6$ is smaller than $f(0)=\frac14$.

## Statement

Find all tangent lines to the parabola $y=x^2$ that pass through the point $A=(1,-3)$.

## Hint

Write the tangent at an unknown abscissa $a$, then require that it passes through $A$. The unknown is $a$, not the slope.

## Solution

**Tangent at $a$.** For $f(x)=x^2$, $f'(a)=2a$, so the tangent at the point of abscissa $a$ is
$$y=a^2+2a(x-a)=2ax-a^2.$$

**Passing through $A$.** We need $-3=2a-a^2$, that is $a^2-2a-3=0$, i.e. $(a-3)(a+1)=0$. Hence $a=3$ or $a=-1$.

**The two tangents.**
- $a=3$: $y=6x-9$,
- $a=-1$: $y=-2x-1$.

## Statement

Find $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}$ such that
$$f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2-x+3&\text{if }x\le1,\\ \alpha x+\beta&\text{if }x>1\end{cases}$$
is differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

## Hint

Differentiability at $1$ requires first continuity at $1$, then equality of the left and right derivatives.

## Solution

**Continuity at $1$.** The left limit is $1-1+3=3=f(1)$ and the right limit is $\alpha+\beta$. So $\alpha+\beta=3$. (1)

**One-sided derivatives at $1$.**
$$f'_-(1)=\lim_{x\to1^-}\frac{x^2-x+3-3}{x-1}=\lim_{x\to1^-}\frac{x(x-1)}{x-1}=1,$$
$$f'_+(1)=\lim_{x\to1^+}\frac{\alpha x+\beta-3}{x-1}=\lim_{x\to1^+}\frac{\alpha(x-1)}{x-1}=\alpha,$$
where we used $\beta-3=-\alpha$ from (1). So $\alpha=1$, and then $\beta=2$.

**Answer.** $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=2$.

**Remark.** Geometrically, the line $y=x+2$ is the tangent to the parabola at the point $(1,3)$: we have glued the parabola to its own tangent.

## Statement

Using only the definition $f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h$:

1. compute the derivative of $f(x)=\dfrac1x$ at $a\ne0$,
2. compute the derivative of $g(x)=\sqrt x$ at $a>0$,
3. explain what goes wrong for $g$ at $a=0$.

## Hint

For part 1, put the numerator over a common denominator. For part 2, multiply by the conjugate.

## Solution

**1.**
$$\frac{\frac1{a+h}-\frac1a}h=\frac{a-(a+h)}{h\,a(a+h)}=\frac{-1}{a(a+h)}\xrightarrow[h\to0]{}-\frac1{a^2}.$$

**2.**
$$\frac{\sqrt{a+h}-\sqrt a}h=\frac{(a+h)-a}{h\left(\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt a\right)}=\frac1{\sqrt{a+h}+\sqrt a}\xrightarrow[h\to0]{}\frac1{2\sqrt a}.$$

**3.** At $a=0$ the difference quotient is $\dfrac{\sqrt h}h=\dfrac1{\sqrt h}$ for $h>0$, which tends to $+\infty$. There is no finite limit: $\sqrt x$ is not differentiable at $0$. Its graph has a **vertical tangent** there.

## Statement

Let $f(x)=|x|+e^x$. Determine where $f$ is differentiable and compute $f'$ there.

## Hint

The absolute value is not differentiable at $0$. Treat $x>0$ and $x<0$ separately, then examine $0$ with the difference quotient.

## Solution

**For $x>0$:** $f(x)=x+e^x$, so $f'(x)=1+e^x$.

**For $x<0$:** $f(x)=-x+e^x$, so $f'(x)=-1+e^x$.

**At $0$.** The difference quotient is $\dfrac{|h|+e^h-1}h=\dfrac{|h|}h+\dfrac{e^h-1}h$. The second term tends to $1$. The first term is $+1$ for $h>0$ and $-1$ for $h<0$. So the right derivative is $1+1=2$ and the left derivative is $-1+1=0$. They differ, so $f$ is not differentiable at $0$.

**Answer.** $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}^*$, with $f'(x)=e^x+1$ for $x>0$ and $f'(x)=e^x-1$ for $x<0$.

**Remark.** The sum of a differentiable function and a function with a corner has a corner at the same place: the exponential only changes the slopes on both sides.

## Statement

Each of the following functions is continuous at $0$. Show that none is differentiable at $0$, and explain why in each case.

1. $f_1(x)=|x|$
2. $f_2(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}$
3. $f_3(x)=x\sin\dfrac1x$ for $x\ne0$, $f_3(0)=0$

## Hint

Write the difference quotient $\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}h$ in each case and study its behaviour as $h\to0$.

## Solution

**1. A corner.** The quotient is $\frac{|h|}h$, equal to $1$ for $h>0$ and $-1$ for $h<0$. The two one-sided derivatives exist but differ.

**2. A vertical tangent.** The quotient is $\dfrac{h^{1/3}}h=h^{-2/3}\to+\infty$ as $h\to0$ (from both sides). The slope of the secants becomes infinite.

**3. Wild oscillations.** The quotient is $\dfrac{h\sin(1/h)}h=\sin\dfrac1h$, which has no limit as $h\to0$: it takes the values $0$ and $1$ along suitable sequences $h\to0$. The secants through $(0,0)$ keep oscillating in slope between $-1$ and $1$.

**Moral.** Differentiability is a strictly stronger property than continuity, and it can fail in three different ways: left and right slopes differ, the slope blows up, or the slope has no limit at all.

## Statement

Let $f(x)=x^2\sin\dfrac1x$ for $x\ne0$ and $f(0)=0$.

1. Show that $f$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ and compute $f'$.
2. Show that $f'$ is not continuous at $0$.

## Hint

Away from $0$ use the usual rules. At $0$ you must use the definition, with the squeeze theorem.

## Solution

**1. For $x\ne0$,** by the product and chain rules,
$$f'(x)=2x\sin\frac1x-\cos\frac1x.$$
**At $0$,** the difference quotient is
$$\frac{f(h)-f(0)}h=h\sin\frac1h,$$
and $|h\sin\frac1h|\le|h|\to0$. So $f'(0)=0$ and $f$ is differentiable everywhere.

**2.** As $x\to0$, the term $2x\sin\frac1x\to0$, but $\cos\frac1x$ has no limit: it equals $1$ along $x_n=\frac1{2n\pi}$ and $-1$ along $y_n=\frac1{(2n+1)\pi}$. So $f'(x)$ has no limit at $0$, and in particular $f'$ is not continuous at $0$, although $f'(0)$ exists.

**Remark.** A derivative can be discontinuous. It cannot however have a jump discontinuity (see the problem on the limit of the derivative): its discontinuities are always of this oscillating kind.

## Statement

Compute the derivative of $f(x)=\ln\!\left(x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\right)$ on $\mathbb{R}$.

## Hint

Apply the chain rule to $\ln u$, then simplify. Notice that $u'=1+\frac x{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$ is related to $u$.

## Solution

**Domain.** Since $\sqrt{1+x^2}>|x|\ge-x$, we have $u(x)=x+\sqrt{1+x^2}>0$ for every $x$, so $f$ is well defined and differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Derivative of $u$.** $u'(x)=1+\dfrac x{\sqrt{1+x^2}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{1+x^2}+x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}=\dfrac{u(x)}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$.

**Chain rule.**
$$f'(x)=\frac{u'(x)}{u(x)}=\frac1{\sqrt{1+x^2}}.$$

**Remark.** This function is the inverse hyperbolic sine $\operatorname{arsinh}$, and the result explains why $\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}=\ln\left(x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\right)$.

## Statement

Compute the $n$-th derivative of $f(x)=x^2e^x$.

## Hint

Use the Leibniz formula $(uv)^{(n)}=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk u^{(k)}v^{(n-k)}$. Most of the terms vanish because $u=x^2$ is a polynomial of degree $2$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The Leibniz formula is the "binomial theorem for derivatives". With $u=x^2$ we have $u'=2x$, $u''=2$ and $u^{(k)}=0$ for $k\ge3$. Also $v=e^x$ satisfies $v^{(j)}=e^x$ for all $j$.

**Computation.** Only $k=0,1,2$ survive:
$$f^{(n)}(x)=\binom n0x^2e^x+\binom n1\,2x\,e^x+\binom n2\,2\,e^x=e^x\left(x^2+2nx+n(n-1)\right).$$

**Check.** For $n=1$: $e^x(x^2+2x)$, which is the product rule. For $n=2$: $e^x(x^2+4x+2)$.

**Remark.** The same method shows that the $n$-th derivative of $P(x)e^x$, with $P$ a polynomial, is $e^x$ times a polynomial of the same degree.

## Statement

Let $n\ge1$ and $Q_k=\dfrac{d^k}{dx^k}\bigl((x^2-1)^n\bigr)$. Prove that $Q_n$ (the polynomial that defines the Legendre polynomial $P_n$ up to a constant) has $n$ distinct real roots, all in $(-1,1)$.

## Hint

Show by induction on $k\le n$ that $Q_k=(x^2-1)^{n-k}R_k$ where $R_k$ has $k$ distinct roots in $(-1,1)$. Rolle's theorem creates one new root between two consecutive roots.

## Solution

**Claim.** For $0\le k\le n$, $Q_k=(x^2-1)^{n-k}R_k$, where $R_k$ is a polynomial of degree $k$ with $k$ distinct roots in $(-1,1)$.

**Degree check.** $Q_k$ has degree $2n-k$, so $\deg R_k=2n-k-2(n-k)=k$. Hence the $k$ roots are all the roots of $R_k$, and they are simple.

**Start.** $k=0$: $Q_0=(x^2-1)^n$ and $R_0=1$ has $0$ roots.

**Inductive step.** Suppose the claim holds for some $k<n$. Then $Q_k=(x-1)^{n-k}(x+1)^{n-k}R_k$ vanishes at the $k+2$ distinct points
$$-1<r_1<\dots<r_k<1,$$
because $n-k\ge1$. By Rolle's theorem applied to $Q_k$ on each of the $k+1$ intervals between consecutive points, $Q_{k+1}=Q_k'$ vanishes at some point in each of them: that gives $k+1$ distinct roots in $(-1,1)$.

Moreover, differentiating a product $(x^2-1)^{m}R$ with $m=n-k\ge1$ leaves a factor $(x^2-1)^{m-1}$, so $Q_{k+1}=(x^2-1)^{n-k-1}R_{k+1}$ for a polynomial $R_{k+1}$. By the degree count, $R_{k+1}$ has degree $k+1$, so the $k+1$ roots found are all its roots.

**Conclusion.** For $k=n$, $Q_n=R_n$ has $n$ distinct roots in $(-1,1)$.

**Remark.** A degree $n$ polynomial cannot have more than $n$ roots, so these are all of them. This is the key property of the Legendre polynomials in numerical integration (Gauss quadrature).

## Statement

Using the mean value theorem on $\arctan$, prove that
$$\forall t>0,\qquad\arctan t>\frac t{1+t^2}.$$

## Hint

Apply the theorem on $[0,t]$. For $c\in(0,t)$ compare $1+c^2$ with $1+t^2$.

## Solution

Fix $t>0$. The function $\arctan$ is continuous on $[0,t]$ and differentiable on $(0,t)$ with derivative $\frac1{1+x^2}$. By the mean value theorem there exists $c\in(0,t)$ with
$$\arctan t-\arctan0=\frac1{1+c^2}(t-0),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad\arctan t=\frac t{1+c^2}.$$
Since $0<c<t$, we have $1+c^2<1+t^2$, so $\frac1{1+c^2}>\frac1{1+t^2}$. Multiplying by $t>0$,
$$\arctan t=\frac t{1+c^2}>\frac t{1+t^2}.$$

**Remark.** The inequality is *strict* precisely because $c<t$ strictly. The mean value theorem is the standard tool for turning information on $f'$ (here: $f'$ is decreasing in $|x|$) into information on $f$.

## Solution

**2.** First, $f(0)=1$: choose $x_0$ with $f(x_0)\ne0$; then $f(x_0)=f(x_0+0)=f(x_0)f(0)$ gives $f(0)=1$.

Fix $y$ and differentiate $f(x+y)=f(x)f(y)$ in $x$:
$$f'(x+y)=f'(x)f(y).$$
Taking $x=0$: $f'(y)=f'(0)f(y)$. Let $a=f'(0)$. Then $f'=af$.

Apply part 1 to $x\mapsto f(x/a)$ if $a\ne0$, or directly: $g(x)=f(x)e^{-ax}$ has $g'=af e^{-ax}-af e^{-ax}=0$, so $g$ is constant, equal to $g(0)=1$. Hence $f(x)=e^{ax}$. (If $a=0$, $f'=0$ and $f=f(0)=1=e^{0\cdot x}$.)

**Remark.** The exponential is characterised by $f'=f$, $f(0)=1$, or by the functional equation: three faces of the same function.

## Statement

Let $f:I\to\mathbb{R}$ be convex and differentiable on an interval $I$. Prove that $f'$ is continuous.

## Hint

Use that $f'$ is non-decreasing for a convex function, and Darboux's theorem: a derivative takes all the intermediate values. Show that a monotone function cannot have a jump if it takes all intermediate values.

## Solution

**Tools.**
- If $f$ is convex and differentiable, $f'$ is non-decreasing.
- **Darboux's theorem:** the derivative of a differentiable function satisfies the intermediate value property, even if it is not continuous.

**Proof.** Suppose $f'$ is discontinuous at some point $x_0\in I$. Since $f'$ is monotone, it has one-sided limits at $x_0$, with
$$f'(x_0^-)\le f'(x_0)\le f'(x_0^+),$$
and at least one of the two inequalities is strict. Say $f'(x_0)<f'(x_0^+)$ (the other case is symmetric). Pick $y$ with $f'(x_0)<y<f'(x_0^+)$.

- For $x>x_0$ we have $f'(x)\ge f'(x_0^+)>y$ (monotone and $f'(x_0^+)$ is the infimum of the values to the right).
- For $x\le x_0$ we have $f'(x)\le f'(x_0)<y$.

So $f'$ never takes the value $y$. But Darboux's theorem, applied on $[x_0,x_1]$ with $x_1>x_0$ close enough that $f'(x_1)>y$, says that $y$, which lies between $f'(x_0)$ and $f'(x_1)$, is attained. Contradiction.

**Conclusion.** $f'$ is continuous on $I$.

**Remark.** So a convex differentiable function is automatically $C^1$. In general, derivatives can be discontinuous (see $x^2\sin\frac1x$), but never with jumps.

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be convex. Assume $\dfrac{f(x)-f(0)}x\to\ell\in\mathbb{R}$ as $x\to+\infty$. Show that $\dfrac{f(x)}x\to\ell$ and that $x\mapsto f(x)-\ell x$ is non-increasing.

## Hint

For a convex function, the slope $\dfrac{f(z)-f(y)}{z-y}$ is non-decreasing in $z$ for fixed $y$. Let $z\to+\infty$.

## Solution

**First claim.**
$$\frac{f(x)}x=\frac{f(x)-f(0)}x+\frac{f(0)}x\longrightarrow\ell+0=\ell.$$

**Slopes at infinity.** Fix $y\in\mathbb{R}$. For $z>y$,
$$\frac{f(z)-f(y)}{z-y}=\frac z{z-y}\cdot\frac{f(z)}z-\frac{f(y)}{z-y}\longrightarrow1\cdot\ell-0=\ell\quad(z\to+\infty).$$

**Convexity.** For fixed $y$, the slope $z\mapsto\frac{f(z)-f(y)}{z-y}$ is non-decreasing on $(y,+\infty)$, so every slope is at most its limit $\ell$. Therefore for $x>y$,
$$\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y}\le\ell\ \Longrightarrow\ f(x)-\ell x\le f(y)-\ell y.$$

**Conclusion.** $x\mapsto f(x)-\ell x$ is non-increasing.
