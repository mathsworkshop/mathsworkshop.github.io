## Statement

Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-2&3\end{pmatrix}$. Find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors, then compute $A^n$ for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

## Hint

The characteristic polynomial is $X^2-\textrm{tr}(A)X+\det A$.

## Solution

$\textrm{tr}A=3$ and $\det A=2$, so $\chi_A(X)=X^2-3X+2=(X-1)(X-2)$. The eigenvalues are $1$ and $2$.

- For $\lambda=1$: $(A-I)v=0$ gives $-x+y=0$, so $v_1=(1,1)$.
- For $\lambda=2$: $(A-2I)v=0$ gives $-2x+y=0$, so $v_2=(1,2)$.

Let $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $D=\textrm{diag}(1,2)$. Then $\det P=1$ and $P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$, and $A=PDP^{-1}$. Hence
$$A^n=PD^nP^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2^n\\1&2^{n+1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2-2^n&2^n-1\\2-2^{n+1}&2^{n+1}-1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Check: $n=0$ gives the identity and $n=1$ gives $A$.

## Statement

Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Compute $A^n$ for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

## Hint

Find the eigenvalues of $A$ and a basis of eigenvectors.

## Solution

The characteristic polynomial is $(X-2)^2-1=(X-1)(X-3)$. The vector $(1,-1)$ is an eigenvector for $1$ and $(1,1)$ is one for $3$.

## Statement

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Is $A$ diagonalizable?

## Hint

The characteristic polynomial has a double root at $2$. Compute the eigenspace $\ker(A-2I)$.

## Solution

**Characteristic polynomial.**
$$\det(A-\lambda I) = (2-\lambda)^2.$$
So the only eigenvalue is $\lambda=2$, with algebraic multiplicity $2$.

$$A-2I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Statement

Find the eigenvalues of
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Hint

Observe that the vector $(1,1,1)$ is an eigenvector, then use the trace and the determinant.

## Solution

**First eigenvalue.** $A(1,1,1)^T=(4,4,4)^T$, so $\lambda_1=4$ with eigenvector $(1,1,1)$.

## Statement

Compute the characteristic polynomial of
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$$
and verify the trace and determinant formulas.

## Hint

For a triangular matrix, $\chi_A(\lambda)=(\lambda_1-\lambda)(\lambda_2-\lambda)(\lambda_3-\lambda)$.

## Solution

**Characteristic polynomial.** $A$ is upper triangular with diagonal entries $1,3,4$, so
$$\chi_A(\lambda) = (1-\lambda)(3-\lambda)(4-\lambda).$$
Expanding:
$$(1-\lambda)(3-\lambda)=3-4\lambda+\lambda^2,$$
$$(3-4\lambda+\lambda^2)(4-\lambda)=12-3\lambda-16\lambda+4\lambda^2+4\lambda^2-\lambda^3=12-19\lambda+8\lambda^2-\lambda^3.$$
So $\chi_A(\lambda)=-\lambda^3+8\lambda^2-19\lambda+12$.

**Check.** Trace: coefficient of $\lambda^2$ is $8=\textrm{tr}(A)$. Constant term: $-(-1)^3\cdot 12 = 12=\det A$. Correct.

**Conclusion.** $\chi_A(\lambda)=-(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)(\lambda-4)$.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix. Show that $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if:
1. the characteristic polynomial splits into linear factors over $\mathbb{R}$;
2. for each eigenvalue $\lambda$, its geometric multiplicity $\dim E_\lambda$ equals its algebraic multiplicity (the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $\chi_A$).

## Hint

If $A$ is diagonalizable, it is similar to a diagonal matrix $D$, and the eigenspaces of $A$ correspond to those of $D$ via the change of basis.

## Solution

**Forward direction.** If $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal, the diagonal entries of $D$ are the eigenvalues of $A$, and each eigenvalue $\lambda$ appears on the diagonal exactly as many times as its algebraic multiplicity. The corresponding eigenspace has the same dimension as the eigenspace of $D$ for $\lambda$, which is the number of times $\lambda$ appears on the diagonal. So geometric multiplicity equals algebraic multiplicity.

**Converse.** Suppose the characteristic polynomial splits and each geometric multiplicity equals algebraic multiplicity. Then the sum of the geometric multiplicities equals the sum of the algebraic multiplicities, which equals $n$. Since the eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are in direct sum, the union of bases of the eigenspaces is a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so $A$ is diagonalizable.

**Conclusion.** The two conditions are necessary and sufficient.

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Statement

Let $p:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ be a projection, i.e. $p^2=p$. Show that $p$ is diagonalizable and determine its eigenvalues.

## Hint

If $p^2=p$, then $p(p-\mathrm{id})=0$, so the minimal polynomial of $p$ divides $X(X-1)$.

## Solution

**Minimal polynomial.** From $p^2=p$: $p^2-p=0$, so $p(p-\mathrm{id})=0$. The minimal polynomial $\mu_p$ divides $X(X-1)$.

**Eigenvalues.** The possible eigenvalues are roots of $\mu_p$: $0$ and $1$.

**Diagonalizability.** The minimal polynomial has simple roots (no repeated factor), so $p$ is diagonalizable. Its eigenvalues are $0$ and $1$.

**Eigenspaces.** $E_1=\textrm{Im} p$ (fixed points), $E_0=\ker p$. Moreover $\mathbb{R}^n=E_0\oplus E_1$.

**Conclusion.** Any projection is diagonalizable with eigenvalues $0$ and $1$.

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ be a linear map with $f^2=\mathrm{id}$. Show that $f$ is diagonalizable and determine its eigenvalues.

$f^2-\mathrm{id}=0$, so $(f-\mathrm{id})(f+\mathrm{id})=0$. The minimal polynomial divides $X^2-1=(X-1)(X+1)$.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix. Show that for every eigenvalue $\lambda$,
$$1\le\dim E_\lambda\le m_\lambda,$$
where $m_\lambda$ is the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ (as root of $\chi_A$).

## Hint

The first inequality is because an eigenvalue has a nonzero eigenvector. For the second, use the rank-nullity theorem and properties of the characteristic polynomial.

## Solution

**Lower bound.** By definition, an eigenvalue has at least one nonzero eigenvector, so $\dim E_\lambda\ge1$.

**Upper bound.** Let $k=\dim E_\lambda$ and choose a basis $(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$ of $E_\lambda$. Extend this basis to a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. In this basis, the matrix of $A$ is block upper triangular:
$$\begin{pmatrix} \lambda I_k & * \\ 0 & * \end{pmatrix}.$$
Therefore $\chi_A$ has the factor $(\lambda-X)^k$, i.e. the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ is at least $k$. Hence $k\le m_\lambda$.

**Conclusion.** $1\le\dim E_\lambda\le m_\lambda$. Equality for all $\lambda$ iff $A$ is diagonalizable.

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Hint

The theorem states that $\chi_A(A)=0$. Compute $\chi_A$, then evaluate at $A$.

## Solution

**Theorem.** Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial: $\chi_A(A)=0$.

$$\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-4\lambda+3.$$

$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ real matrix. Show that $A$ is trigonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$ if and only if its characteristic polynomial has all roots in $\mathbb{R}$.

## Hint

Use induction on $n$: find a real eigenvalue, which gives a stable line, then apply the induction hypothesis to the quotient.

## Solution

**Forward direction.** If $A$ is similar to an upper triangular matrix $T$, then $\chi_A=\chi_T$, which is a product of linear factors, so all roots are real.

**Converse.** Suppose $\chi_A$ has all roots in $\mathbb{R}$. Then $A$ has at least one real eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, hence a stable line $L_1=\textrm{span}(v_1)$ with $Av_1=\lambda_1 v_1$. Complete $(v_1)$ to a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. In this basis, the matrix of $A$ is block upper triangular:
$$M = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & * \\ 0 & B \end{pmatrix}.$$
The characteristic polynomial of $B$ divides $\chi_A$, so it also has all roots in $\mathbb{R}$. By induction, $B$ is trigonalizable, and consequently so is $M$.

**Conclusion.** Trigonalizability over $\mathbb{R}$ is equivalent to all eigenvalues being real.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ (counted with multiplicity). Show that
$$\sum_i\lambda_i = \textrm{tr}(A), \qquad \prod_i\lambda_i = \det(A).$$

## Hint

Expand $\chi_A(\lambda)=\prod(\lambda-\lambda_i)$ and compare coefficients with $\chi_A(\lambda)=\lambda^n-\textrm{tr}(A)\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^n\det A$.

## Solution

**Expansion of $\prod(\lambda-\lambda_i)$.** The coefficient of $\lambda^{n-1}$ is $-\sum_i\lambda_i$. The constant term is $(-1)^n\prod_i\lambda_i$.

**Identification.** Comparing with
$$\chi_A(\lambda)=\lambda^n-\textrm{tr}(A)\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^n\det A,$$
we get
$$\sum_i\lambda_i=\textrm{tr}(A), \qquad \prod_i\lambda_i=\det A.$$

**Conclusion.** Trace and determinant are the elementary symmetric functions of degree $1$ and $n$ of the eigenvalues.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix. Show that $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if its minimal polynomial has only simple roots.

## Hint

If $A$ is diagonalizable, the minimal polynomial is $\prod(X-\lambda_i)$ over distinct eigenvalues. Conversely, if the minimal polynomial has simple roots, use the kernel decomposition.

## Solution

**Forward direction.** If $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $D=\textrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$, then $A$ is killed by $\prod_i(X-\lambda_i)$ where the product is over distinct eigenvalues. The minimal polynomial is the smallest such polynomial, so it is a product of distinct linear factors.

**Converse.** Let $\mu_A(X)=\prod_{i=1}^r(X-\lambda_i)$ with distinct $\lambda_i$. For each $i$, let $E_i=\ker(A-\lambda_i I)$. We claim that the sum $E_1+\cdots+E_r$ is $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Indeed, the polynomials $P_i(X)=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{X-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}$ form a Lagrange interpolation basis: $\sum_i P_i=1$ and $P_i$ is zero at all $\lambda_j$ for $j\neq i$, and $P_i(\lambda_i)=1$.

For any $v\in\mathbb{R}^n$: $v=\sum_i P_i(A)v$ (since $\sum_i P_i=1$). But $(A-\lambda_i I)P_i(A)v = \mu_A(A)\cdot(\text{something})=0$, so $P_i(A)v\in E_i$. Hence $v$ decomposes as a sum of eigenvectors, and $\mathbb{R}^n$ is spanned by the eigenspaces. Thus $A$ is diagonalizable.

**Conclusion.** $A$ is diagonalizable iff its minimal polynomial has only simple roots.

Its discriminant is $4\cos^2\theta-4=-4\sin^2\theta$.

**Case $\sin\theta=0$, i.e. $\theta\in\pi\mathbb{Z}$.** If $\theta\in2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, then $R_\theta=I$ and $\lambda=1$ is a double eigenvalue. If $\theta\in\pi+2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, then $R_\theta=-I$ and $\lambda=-1$ is a double eigenvalue. In both cases every nonzero vector is an eigenvector.

**Case $\sin\theta\neq0$.** The roots are
$$\lambda_\pm=\frac{2\cos\theta\pm2i|\sin\theta|}{2}=\cos\theta\pm i\sin\theta=e^{\pm i\theta},$$
which are not real. An eigenvector for $e^{i\theta}$ is $(1,-i)^T$ (indeed $R_\theta(1,-i)^T=(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta,\ \sin\theta-i\cos\theta)^T=e^{i\theta}(1,-i)^T$), and $(1,i)^T$ works for $e^{-i\theta}$.

**Conclusion.** The eigenvalues are $e^{\pm i\theta}$. For $\theta\notin\pi\mathbb{Z}$ there is no real eigenvalue, hence no real eigenvector, i.e. $R_\theta$ leaves no line of $\mathbb{R}^2$ invariant. For $\theta\in\pi\mathbb{Z}$ the rotation is $\pm I$ and every line is invariant.

## Statement

$$\det(XI-B)=\det\big(P^{-1}(XI-A)P\big)=\det(P^{-1})\det(XI-A)\det(P)=\det(XI-A),$$

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ real matrix and $\lambda$ a real eigenvalue. Show that
$$1\le\dim E_\lambda\le m_\lambda,$$
where $m_\lambda$ is the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $\chi_A(X)=\det(XI-A)$.

The first inequality is because an eigenvalue has a nonzero eigenvector. For the second, complete a basis of $E_\lambda$ to a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and look at the matrix of $A$ in this basis.

for some blocks $C$ and $B$. By similarity invariance of $\chi$ and the determinant formula for block triangular matrices,
$$\chi_A(X)=\chi_M(X)=\det\begin{pmatrix}(X-\lambda)I_k&-C\\0&XI-B\end{pmatrix}=(X-\lambda)^k\chi_B(X).$$
So $(X-\lambda)^k$ divides $\chi_A$, hence $m_\lambda\ge k$.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ real matrix. Show that $A$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$ if and only if:
1. the characteristic polynomial $\chi_A$ splits into linear factors over $\mathbb{R}$;
2. for each real eigenvalue $\lambda$, $\dim E_\lambda=m_\lambda$ (geometric multiplicity equals algebraic multiplicity).

## Hint

First prove that eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are in direct sum. For the forward direction, compute everything on the diagonal matrix $D$ and transport it by $P$.

## Solution

**Lemma.** If $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r$ are distinct and $w_i\in E_{\lambda_i}$ satisfy $w_1+\cdots+w_r=0$, then all $w_i=0$.

*Proof, by induction on $r$.* The case $r=1$ is trivial. For $r\ge2$, apply $A-\lambda_rI$: it sends $w_i$ to $(\lambda_i-\lambda_r)w_i\in E_{\lambda_i}$ and kills $w_r$. So $\sum_{i<r}(\lambda_i-\lambda_r)w_i=0$, and by induction $(\lambda_i-\lambda_r)w_i=0$, hence $w_i=0$ for $i<r$. Then $w_r=0$. $\square$

**($\Rightarrow$)** Let $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $D=\textrm{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)$. Since similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial, $\chi_A=\chi_D=\prod_j(X-d_j)$, which splits: this is (1). For a real eigenvalue $\lambda$, its algebraic multiplicity is $m_\lambda=\#\{j:d_j=\lambda\}$. Moreover $E_\lambda(D)=\textrm{span}\{e_j:d_j=\lambda\}$ has dimension $\#\{j:d_j=\lambda\}$, and $E_\lambda(A)=P\,E_\lambda(D)$ has the same dimension. So $\dim E_\lambda=m_\lambda$: this is (2).

**($\Leftarrow$)** Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r$ be the distinct eigenvalues. By (1), $\sum_im_{\lambda_i}=\deg\chi_A=n$, and by (2) $\sum_i\dim E_{\lambda_i}=n$. By the Lemma the sum of the eigenspaces is direct, so it has dimension $n$ and equals $\mathbb{R}^n$. The union of bases of the $E_{\lambda_i}$ is therefore a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ made of eigenvectors, and $A$ is diagonalizable.

**Conclusion.** Conditions (1) and (2) are necessary and sufficient.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ real matrix and $\mu_A$ its minimal polynomial (the monic polynomial of least degree annihilating $A$). Show that $A$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$ if and only if $\mu_A$ is split over $\mathbb{R}$ with simple roots, i.e. $\mu_A=\prod_{i=1}^r(X-\lambda_i)$ with distinct real $\lambda_i$.

Forward: $\mu_A$ divides any annihilating polynomial. Converse: use Lagrange interpolation polynomials $P_i$ to decompose any vector into eigenvectors.

Since $\mu_A$ divides every annihilating polynomial, $\mu_A$ divides $q$, hence $\mu_A$ is a product of distinct real linear factors.

**Converse.** Let $\mu_A=\prod_{i=1}^r(X-\lambda_i)$ with distinct real $\lambda_i$, and put
$$P_i(X)=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{X-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}.$$
Then $P_i(\lambda_j)=\delta_{ij}$. The polynomial $\sum_iP_i-1$ has degree at most $r-1$ and vanishes at the $r$ distinct points $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r$, so $\sum_iP_i=1$.

For $v\in\mathbb{R}^n$: $v=\sum_iP_i(A)v$. Moreover $(X-\lambda_i)P_i(X)=\mu_A(X)/c_i$ with the nonzero constant $c_i=\prod_{j\neq i}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)$, so $(A-\lambda_iI)P_i(A)=\mu_A(A)/c_i=0$. Hence $P_i(A)v\in E_{\lambda_i}$.

Thus every $v$ is a sum of vectors of the eigenspaces, i.e. $\mathbb{R}^n=\sum_iE_{\lambda_i}$. Selecting a basis from the union of bases of the $E_{\lambda_i}$ gives a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ made of eigenvectors: $A$ is diagonalizable.

**Conclusion.** $A$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$ iff $\mu_A$ is a product of distinct real linear factors.

**Eigenvalues.** If $p(v)=\lambda v$ with $v\neq0$, then $p^2(v)=\lambda^2v=\lambda v$, so $\lambda\in\{0,1\}$. Precisely: $1$ is an eigenvalue iff $\textrm{Im}p\neq\{0\}$, i.e. $p\neq0$; and $0$ is an eigenvalue iff $\ker p\neq\{0\}$, i.e. $p\neq\mathrm{id}$.

**Diagonalizability.** The union of a basis of $\ker p$ and a basis of $\textrm{Im}p$ is a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ made of eigenvectors. In this basis the matrix of $p$ is $\textrm{diag}(1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0)$ with $\textrm{rank}p$ ones.

**Conclusion.** Every projection is diagonalizable, with eigenvalues in $\{0,1\}$ and $\mathbb{R}^n=E_0\oplus E_1$. (Alternatively: the minimal polynomial divides $X(X-1)$, which is split with simple roots.)

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ be a linear map with $f^2=\mathrm{id}$. Show that $\mathbb{R}^n=E_1\oplus E_{-1}$, that $f$ is diagonalizable, and determine its possible eigenvalues.

## Hint

Write $x=\frac{x+f(x)}2+\frac{x-f(x)}2$. The polynomial $X^2-1=(X-1)(X+1)$ annihilates $f$.

## Solution

**Decomposition.** For $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, put $x_+=\frac{x+f(x)}2$ and $x_-=\frac{x-f(x)}2$, so $x=x_++x_-$. Then $f(x_+)=\frac{f(x)+f^2(x)}2=\frac{f(x)+x}2=x_+$ and $f(x_-)=\frac{f(x)-x}2=-x_-$. So $x_+\in E_1$ and $x_-\in E_{-1}$. If $x\in E_1\cap E_{-1}$, then $x=f(x)=-x$, so $x=0$. Hence $\mathbb{R}^n=E_1\oplus E_{-1}$ (this uses that $2$ is invertible in $\mathbb{R}$).

**Eigenvalues.** If $f(v)=\lambda v$ with $v\neq0$, then $v=f^2(v)=\lambda^2v$, so $\lambda^2=1$. Precisely: $1$ is an eigenvalue iff $f\neq-\mathrm{id}$, and $-1$ is an eigenvalue iff $f\neq\mathrm{id}$. (Indeed $E_1=\{0\}$ forces $x_+=0$ for all $x$, i.e. $f=-\mathrm{id}$.)

**Diagonalizability.** The union of a basis of $E_1$ and a basis of $E_{-1}$ is a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ made of eigenvectors.

**Conclusion.** Every involution is diagonalizable, with eigenvalues in $\{1,-1\}$. (Alternatively: the minimal polynomial divides $X^2-1$, which is split with simple roots.)

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ real matrix. Show that $A$ is trigonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$ (i.e. $P^{-1}AP$ is upper triangular for some $P\in GL_n(\mathbb{R})$) if and only if its characteristic polynomial splits into linear factors over $\mathbb{R}$, i.e. all its roots are real.

## Hint

Induction on $n$: a real eigenvalue gives an invariant line, which gives a block form $\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&*\\0&B\end{pmatrix}$ with $B$ of size $n-1$.

## Solution

**Forward direction.** If $T=P^{-1}AP$ is upper triangular, then $\chi_A=\chi_T=\prod_i(X-t_{ii})$, which splits over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Converse, by induction on $n$.** For $n=1$ every matrix is triangular.

Let $n\ge2$ and assume $\chi_A$ splits over $\mathbb{R}$. Then $\chi_A$ has a real root $\lambda_1$, hence an eigenvector $v_1\neq0$ with $Av_1=\lambda_1v_1$. Complete $(v_1)$ to a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and let $P_1$ be its matrix. Then
$$M=P_1^{-1}AP_1=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&c\\0&B\end{pmatrix}$$
with $B$ an $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ matrix and $c$ a row vector. We have $\chi_A=\chi_M=(X-\lambda_1)\chi_B$, so $\chi_B$ divides a polynomial that splits over $\mathbb{R}$, hence $\chi_B$ splits over $\mathbb{R}$ (unique factorization).

By the induction hypothesis there is $Q\in GL_{n-1}(\mathbb{R})$ with $T=Q^{-1}BQ$ upper triangular. Let $R=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&Q\end{pmatrix}$. Then
$$R^{-1}MR=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&cQ\\0&T\end{pmatrix},$$
which is upper triangular. So $A$ is trigonalizable, with $P=P_1R$.

**Conclusion.** $A$ is trigonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$ if and only if all roots of $\chi_A$ are real. In particular every complex matrix is trigonalizable over $\mathbb{C}$.

$$C = \begin{pmatrix} a & b & c \\ c & a & b \\ b & c & a \end{pmatrix}.$$

$$\lambda_k = a + b\omega^k + c\omega^{2k}, \qquad k=0,1,2,$$

Compute $Cv_k$ using $\omega^3=1$. Then show that $v_0,v_1,v_2$ are independent.

$$(Cv_k)_1=a+b\omega^k+c\omega^{2k}=\lambda_k,$$

## Statement

Orthogonally diagonalize
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix},$$
i.e. find an orthogonal matrix $Q$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ with $A=QDQ^T$.

## Hint

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors. For a symmetric matrix, eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal, so normalizing them is enough here (Gram–Schmidt is needed only inside an eigenspace of dimension at least $2$).

## Solution

**Eigenvalues.** $\det(A-\lambda I)=(3-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-6\lambda+8=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-4)$. So $\lambda_1=2$ and $\lambda_2=4$.

**Eigenvectors.**
- $\lambda=2$: $A-2I=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $v_1=(1,-1)^T$.
- $\lambda=4$: $A-4I=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $v_2=(1,1)^T$.

They are orthogonal: $\langle v_1,v_2\rangle=1-1=0$.

**Normalization.** $\|v_1\|=\|v_2\|=\sqrt2$, so $u_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^T$ and $u_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)^T$.

**Orthogonal matrix $Q$.**
$$Q=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q^TQ=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}=I.$$
Then $AQ=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\-2&4\end{pmatrix}$ and
$$Q^TAQ=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\-2&4\end{pmatrix}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&8\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&4\end{pmatrix}.$$

**Conclusion.** $A=Q\textrm{diag}(2,4)Q^T$ with $Q$ orthogonal.

## Statement

Let $A$ be a real symmetric $n\times n$ matrix with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r$. Let $P_i$ be the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace $E_{\lambda_i}$. Show that
$$A=\sum_{i=1}^r\lambda_iP_i, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^rP_i=I.$$
Illustrate on $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$.

## Hint

Use the spectral theorem: there is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Show first that eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

## Solution

**Orthogonality of eigenspaces.** Let $v\in E_{\lambda_i}$ and $w\in E_{\lambda_k}$ with $i\neq k$. Since $A$ is symmetric, $\lambda_i\langle v,w\rangle=\langle Av,w\rangle=\langle v,Aw\rangle=\lambda_k\langle v,w\rangle$, so $\langle v,w\rangle=0$.

**Orthonormal eigenbasis.** By the spectral theorem, there is an orthonormal basis $(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $Au_j=\mu_ju_j$, each $\mu_j$ being one of the $\lambda_i$. Let $J_i=\{j:\mu_j=\lambda_i\}$.

**The $u_j$, $j\in J_i$, form an orthonormal basis of $E_{\lambda_i}$.** They lie in $E_{\lambda_i}$ and are orthonormal. Conversely, let $v\in E_{\lambda_i}$; then $v=\sum_j\langle v,u_j\rangle u_j$, and for $j\notin J_i$, $u_j$ lies in some $E_{\lambda_k}$ with $k\neq i$, so $\langle v,u_j\rangle=0$ by orthogonality of eigenspaces. Hence $v\in\textrm{span}(u_j:j\in J_i)$.

**Projections.** Therefore $P_iv=\sum_{j\in J_i}\langle v,u_j\rangle u_j$ for all $v$.

**Decomposition.** For any $v$,
$$v=\sum_j\langle v,u_j\rangle u_j=\sum_iP_iv, \qquad Av=\sum_j\mu_j\langle v,u_j\rangle u_j=\sum_i\lambda_i\sum_{j\in J_i}\langle v,u_j\rangle u_j=\sum_i\lambda_iP_iv.$$

**Conclusion.** $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ and $\sum_iP_i=I$ (moreover $P_iP_k=0$ for $i\neq k$).

**Example.** For $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda_1=2$ with $u_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^T$ and $\lambda_2=4$ with $u_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)^T$, so
$$P_1=u_1u_1^T=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}, \quad P_2=u_2u_2^T=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},$$
and $2P_1+4P_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}=A$, $P_1+P_2=I$.

## Statement

Let $A$ be a diagonalizable matrix with distinct eigenvalues $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_r$, and let $f$ be a function defined at the $\mu_i$. For a diagonalization $A=PDP^{-1}$, $D=\textrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$, define $f(A)=P\,f(D)\,P^{-1}$ where $f(D)=\textrm{diag}(f(\lambda_1),\ldots,f(\lambda_n))$.

1. Show that if $f$ is a polynomial, this agrees with the usual $f(A)=\sum_ka_kA^k$.
2. Show that the definition does not depend on the choice of $P$ and $D$ (it is well defined).
3. Compute $\sqrt A$ for $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$.

## Hint

For (2), choose a polynomial $q$ with $q(\mu_i)=f(\mu_i)$ for all $i$ (Lagrange interpolation) and show $P\,f(D)P^{-1}=q(A)$.

## Solution

**(1) Polynomial case.** For $f(X)=\sum_ka_kX^k$, using $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$:
$$f(A)=\sum_ka_kPD^kP^{-1}=P\Big(\sum_ka_kD^k\Big)P^{-1}=P\,f(D)\,P^{-1},$$
since $\sum_ka_kD^k=\textrm{diag}\big(f(\lambda_j)\big)$.

**(2) Well-definedness.** Every diagonal entry $\lambda_j$ of $D$ is one of the $\mu_i$. Let $q$ be a polynomial with $q(\mu_i)=f(\mu_i)$ for $i=1,\ldots,r$ (e.g. $q=\sum_if(\mu_i)\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{X-\mu_j}{\mu_i-\mu_j}$). Then $f(D)=q(D)$, and by (1)
$$P\,f(D)\,P^{-1}=P\,q(D)\,P^{-1}=q(PDP^{-1})=q(A).$$
The right-hand side depends only on $A$ (and $f$ on the spectrum), not on the chosen $P$ and $D$.

**(3) Example.** $A(1,-1)^T=(1,-1)^T$ and $A(1,1)^T=3(1,1)^T$, so $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $D=\textrm{diag}(1,3)$, $P^{-1}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Then
$$\sqrt A=P\textrm{diag}(1,\sqrt3)P^{-1}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+\sqrt3&\sqrt3-1\\\sqrt3-1&1+\sqrt3\end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: the $(1,1)$ entry of $(\sqrt A)^2$ is $\frac14\big((1+\sqrt3)^2+(\sqrt3-1)^2\big)=\frac{8}{4}=2$ and the $(1,2)$ entry is $\frac14\cdot2(1+\sqrt3)(\sqrt3-1)=1$, as required.

**Conclusion.** Functions of a diagonalizable matrix are well defined through its eigen-decomposition and coincide with polynomial functions of $A$ on the spectrum. For a power series such as $e^{tA}=\sum_kt^kA^k/k!$ one gets $e^{tA}=P\textrm{diag}(e^{t\lambda_j})P^{-1}$. For non-diagonalizable matrices one uses the Jordan form instead.
