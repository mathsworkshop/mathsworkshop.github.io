## Statement

Compute the determinant of each matrix and decide whether it is invertible:

(a) $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$;
(b) $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$;
(c) $C = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$.

Compute the determinant of

Use Sarrus' rule: add the three products along downward diagonals and subtract the three products along upward diagonals.

## Solution

**Sarrus' rule.**
$$\det A = 1\cdot5\cdot10 + 2\cdot6\cdot7 + 3\cdot4\cdot8 - 3\cdot5\cdot7 - 1\cdot6\cdot8 - 2\cdot4\cdot10.$$

## Statement

Compute the determinant of
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 5 & 0 & 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$$
by expanding along the most convenient row or column.

## Hint

Expand along the second row, which has only one nonzero entry.

## Solution

**Expansion along the second row.** The only nonzero entry is $a_{22}=3$. Its cofactor is $(-1)^{2+2}=+1$ times the determinant of the $3\times3$ minor obtained by deleting row $2$ and column $2$:
$$M_{22} = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 5 & 0 & 6 \end{vmatrix}.$$

**Expand $M_{22}$ along its second row.** The only nonzero entry is $4$ at position $(2,2)$, cofactor $(+1)\begin{vmatrix}2&1\\5&6\end{vmatrix} = 12-5=7$.

So $M_{22} = 4\cdot7 = 28$.

**Conclusion.** $\det A = 3\cdot28 = 84$.

**Check by permutation formula.** The matrix is a block "diagonal with a corner" structure; the result $2\cdot3\cdot4\cdot6 + (\text{sign})\cdot1\cdot3\cdot4\cdot5 = 144 - 60 = 84$ confirms it.

## Statement

Describe the effect of each elementary row operation on the determinant:

1. $L_i \leftrightarrow L_j$ (swap two rows);
2. $L_i \leftarrow \lambda L_i$ with $\lambda\neq 0$ (multiply a row by $\lambda$);
3. $L_i \leftarrow L_i + \lambda L_j$ with $j\neq i$ (add a multiple of another row).

## Hint

Use multilinearity and the alternating property of the determinant.

## Solution

**1. Swapping.** The determinant changes sign: $\det = -\det_{\text{original}}$. This follows from the alternating property (the determinant is zero when two rows are equal, and swapping is an odd permutation).

**2. Scaling.** Multiplying a row by $\lambda$ multiplies the determinant by $\lambda$: $\det = \lambda\cdot\det_{\text{original}}$. This follows from linearity in each row.

**3. Adding a multiple.** Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged. Indeed, writing the new row as $L_i+\lambda L_j$, linearity gives $\det(L_i+\lambda L_j,\ldots)=\det(L_i,\ldots)+\lambda\det(L_j,\ldots)$, and the second determinant has two equal rows, so it is zero.

**Conclusion.** Swapping changes sign; scaling by $\lambda$ scales by $\lambda$; adding a multiple leaves the determinant unchanged. These rules are used to simplify determinant computations.

## Statement

Show that the determinant of an upper triangular (or lower triangular) matrix is the product of its diagonal entries.

## Hint

Expand recursively along the first column (or last row).

## Solution

**Expansion along the first column.** Let $T$ be upper triangular with diagonal entries $t_{11},\ldots,t_{nn}$. In the first column, only $t_{11}$ is nonzero, so
$$\det T = t_{11}\det T_{11},$$
where $T_{11}$ is the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ upper triangular submatrix obtained by removing the first row and first column, with diagonal entries $t_{22},\ldots,t_{nn}$.

**Induction.** Repeating the expansion, we get $\det T = t_{11}t_{22}\cdots t_{nn}$.

**Lower triangular case.** The same argument works by expanding along the first row (or by transposing: $\det T = \det T^T$, and $T^T$ is upper triangular).

**Conclusion.** For any triangular matrix, the determinant equals the product of the diagonal entries.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix. Show that the determinant is linear in each column: if $C_j = \lambda C_j' + \mu C_j''$, then
$$\det(A) = \lambda\det(A')+\mu\det(A''),$$
where $A'$ and $A''$ are obtained from $A$ by replacing column $j$ with $C_j'$ and $C_j''$ respectively, the other columns being unchanged.

## Hint

Use the Leibniz formula: $\det A = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^n a_{\sigma(i),i}$, which is linear in each column's entries.

## Solution

**Leibniz formula.** For an $n\times n$ matrix with columns $C_1,\ldots,C_n$, each entry $a_{ij}$ is the $i$-th entry of $C_j$. The Leibniz formula is
$$\det A = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^n a_{\sigma(i),i}.$$

**Linearity in column $j$.** In this formula, column $j$ appears only through the factor $a_{\sigma(j),j}$ (the entry in row $\sigma(j)$ and column $j$). Since $a_{\sigma(j),j}=\lambda a'_{\sigma(j),j}+\mu a''_{\sigma(j),j}$, the product is linear in that entry, hence the entire sum is linear in column $j$.

**Conclusion.** The determinant is linear in each column separately (multilinear). Combined with the alternating property (zero when two columns are equal, sign change under transposition), this characterises the determinant.

## Statement

Show that:
1. If two columns of $A$ are equal, then $\det A = 0$;
2. Swapping two columns changes the sign of the determinant.

## Hint

For (1), use the Leibniz formula and pair permutations with their transpositions. For (2), use multilinearity and (1).

## Solution

**1. Equal columns.** Suppose columns $j$ and $k$ (with $j\neq k$) are equal. Consider the transposition $\tau=(j\,k)$ in $S_n$. For each permutation $\sigma$, $\sigma\circ\tau$ has the same sign as $\sigma$ (since $\varepsilon(\tau)=-1$ and $\varepsilon(\sigma\circ\tau)=-\varepsilon(\sigma)$). Using the Leibniz formula, pair up the terms:
$$\det A = \sum_\sigma \varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_i a_{\sigma(i),i} = \sum_\sigma \varepsilon(\sigma\circ\tau)\prod_i a_{\sigma\circ\tau(i),i}.$$
But because columns $j$ and $k$ are equal, $\prod_i a_{\sigma\circ\tau(i),i} = \prod_i a_{\sigma(i),i}$. So the two sums cancel, giving $\det A = -\det A$, hence $\det A=0$.

**2. Swapping columns.** Let $B$ be obtained from $A$ by swapping columns $j$ and $k$. Consider the determinant of $A+B$: column $j$ of $A+B$ is $C_j + C_k$, and column $k$ is $C_k+C_j$, so these columns are equal. By (1), $\det(A+B)=0$. By multilinearity:
$$0 = \det(A+B) = \det A + \det A^{j\leftarrow k} + \det A^{k\leftarrow j} + \det B,$$
where $A^{j\leftarrow k}$ has column $j$ replaced by $C_k$ (and column $k$ unchanged) â€” this matrix has two equal columns $C_k$, so its determinant is zero. Similarly $\det A^{k\leftarrow j}=0$. Hence $\det B = -\det A$.

**Conclusion.** The determinant is alternating: zero on equal columns, and swapping two columns flips the sign.

## Statement

Show that $\det(A^T)=\det(A)$ for every square matrix $A$.

## Hint

Use the Leibniz formula on both sides and reindex the permutations.

## Solution

**Leibniz formula.** 
$$\det A = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^n a_{\sigma(i),i}.$$

**Transpose.** The entry $(i,j)$ of $A^T$ is $a_{ji}$. So
$$\det(A^T) = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)}.$$
Since $\prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)}=\prod_{j=1}^n a_{\sigma^{-1}(j),j}$ (reindexing $j=\sigma(i)$), and $\varepsilon(\sigma^{-1})=\varepsilon(\sigma)$ (since $\varepsilon$ is a group homomorphism and $\sigma\sigma^{-1}=e$), we can rewrite:
$$\det(A^T) = \sum_{\sigma}\varepsilon(\sigma^{-1})\prod_j a_{\sigma^{-1}(j),j} = \sum_{\tau}\varepsilon(\tau)\prod_j a_{\tau(j),j} = \det A,$$
where the last change of variables is $\tau=\sigma^{-1}$.

**Conclusion.** $\det(A^T)=\det A$. In particular, properties of columns hold for rows too.

## Statement

Show that $\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)$ for $A,B\in\mathcal{M}_n(\mathbb{R})$.

## Hint

Consider the map $B\mapsto\det(AB)$ as a function of the columns of $B$, and show it is alternating multilinear.

## Solution

**Key idea.** The map $\varphi:B\mapsto\det(AB)$ is multilinear and alternating in the columns of $B$, because $AB$ is linear in the columns of $B$, and the determinant is multilinear and alternating. Moreover, $\varphi(I)=\det(A)$.

**Uniqueness.** Any alternating multilinear form on $\mathcal{M}_n(\mathbb{R})$ is a scalar multiple of the determinant. So there exists a constant $c$ with $\varphi(B)=c\det(B)$ for all $B$. Setting $B=I$: $\varphi(I)=c\det I=c$, so $c=\det A$.

**Conclusion.** $\det(AB)=\det A\cdot\det B$ for all $A,B$.

**Corollary.** If $A$ is invertible, $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det A$. If $A$ is invertible and $B=P^{-1}AP$, then $\det B=\det A$.

## Statement

Let $A$ be invertible. Define the cofactor $C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\det M_{ij}$, where $M_{ij}$ is the minor obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$. Define the comatrix $\operatorname{com}(A)=(C_{ij})^T$ (transpose of the cofactor matrix). Show that
$$A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}\operatorname{com}(A).$$

## Hint

Show that $A\cdot\operatorname{com}(A)=\det(A)\cdot I$, using the expansion of the determinant along a row.

## Solution

**Entry $(i,j)$ of $A\cdot\operatorname{com}(A)$.** 
$$(A\cdot\operatorname{com}(A))_{ij}=\sum_k a_{ik}C_{jk}.$$

**Case $i=j$.** $\sum_k a_{ik}C_{ik}=\det A$ by expansion along row $i$ (each term is a cofactor times its entry).

**Case $i\neq j$.** $\sum_k a_{ik}C_{jk}$ is the determinant of the matrix obtained from $A$ by replacing row $j$ with row $i$. This matrix has two equal rows, so its determinant is zero.

**Conclusion.** $A\cdot\operatorname{com}(A)=\det(A)\cdot I$, so $A^{-1}=\operatorname{com}(A)/\det A$.

## Statement

Compute the Vandermonde determinant
$$V(a_1,a_2,a_3) = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ a_1^2 & a_2^2 & a_3^2 \end{vmatrix}.$$

Subtract the first column from the others, factor $a_2-a_1$ and $a_3-a_1$, then expand.

**Conclusion.** $V(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(a_2-a_1)(a_3-a_1)(a_3-a_2)$. In particular, $V\neq0$ iff the $a_i$ are pairwise distinct.

## Statement

Generalise to $n$ variables:
$$V(a_1,\ldots,a_n) = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ a_1 & a_2 & \cdots & a_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ a_1^{n-1} & a_2^{n-1} & \cdots & a_n^{n-1} \end{vmatrix}.$$
Show that
$$V(a_1,\ldots,a_n) = \prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(a_j-a_i).$$

## Hint

Proceed by induction, subtracting the first column from the others, factoring $a_j-a_1$, and identifying a smaller Vandermonde determinant.

## Solution

**Base case $n=2$.** $V(a_1,a_2)=a_2-a_1$.

**Inductive step.** Subtract column 1 from columns $2,\ldots,n$:
$$V = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ a_1 & a_2-a_1 & \cdots & a_n-a_1 \\ a_1^2 & a_2^2-a_1^2 & \cdots & a_n^2-a_1^2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ a_1^{n-1} & a_2^{n-1}-a_1^{n-1} & \cdots & a_n^{n-1}-a_1^{n-1} \end{vmatrix}.$$
Expand along the first row. For each column $j\ge2$, factor $a_j-a_1$:
$$V = \left(\prod_{j=2}^n(a_j-a_1)\right)\cdot\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ a_1+a_2 & a_1+a_3 & \cdots & a_1+a_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ \sum_{k=0}^{n-2}a_1^{n-2-k}a_j^k & \cdots & & \end{vmatrix}.$$
By column operations (subtracting $a_1$ times the previous column), the remaining determinant reduces to $V(a_2,\ldots,a_n)$.

**Induction.** By the induction hypothesis, $V(a_2,\ldots,a_n)=\prod_{2\le i<j\le n}(a_j-a_i)$.

**Conclusion.**
$$V(a_1,\ldots,a_n) = \prod_{j=2}^n(a_j-a_1)\prod_{2\le i<j\le n}(a_j-a_i) = \prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(a_j-a_i).$$

Show that for a block triangular matrix

## Statement

Let $Q$ be an orthogonal matrix, i.e. $Q^T Q=I$. Show that $\det Q=\pm1$.

## Hint

Take determinants of $Q^T Q=I$.

## Solution

**Computation.** From $Q^T Q=I$:
$$\det(Q^T Q) = \det I = 1.$$
By multiplicativity of the determinant and $\det(Q^T)=\det Q$:
$$\det(Q)\det(Q) = 1.$$
So $(\det Q)^2=1$, hence $\det Q = \pm1$.

## Statement

## Hint

Use the characterisation of invertibility via the determinant, and the characterisation of full rank.

## Solution

## Statement

Let $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Show that the Gram determinant
$$G(u,v) = \begin{vmatrix} \langle u,u\rangle & \langle u,v\rangle \\ \langle v,u\rangle & \langle v,v\rangle \end{vmatrix}$$
satisfies $G(u,v)\ge0$, with equality iff $u,v$ are linearly dependent. Deduce the Cauchyâ€“Schwarz inequality.

## Hint

$G(u,v)=\|u\|^2\|v\|^2-\langle u,v\rangle^2$. Show it is nonnegative by a direct computation with $\|u-tv\|^2\ge0$ for all $t$.

## Solution

**Explicit formula.** $\langle u,u\rangle=\|u\|^2$, $\langle v,v\rangle=\|v\|^2$, $\langle u,v\rangle=\langle v,u\rangle$, so
$$G(u,v) = \|u\|^2\|v\|^2 - \langle u,v\rangle^2.$$

**Non-negativity.** For all $t\in\mathbb{R}$, $\|u-tv\|^2\ge0$, so
$$\|u\|^2 - 2t\langle u,v\rangle + t^2\|v\|^2\ge0.$$

$$\|u\|^2 - \frac{\langle u,v\rangle^2}{\|v\|^2}\ge0,$$

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ real matrix with columns $C_1,\ldots,C_n$. Show that
$$|\det A|\le\prod_{i=1}^n\|C_i\|.$$

## Hint

Use Gram-Schmidt or the geometric interpretation of the determinant as a volume.

## Solution

**Geometric interpretation.** $|\det A|$ is the volume of the parallelotope spanned by $C_1,\ldots,C_n$. This volume equals the product of the lengths of the columns *only* when the columns are orthogonal. In general, the volume is at most the product of lengths (the "base times height" formula, with the height bounded by the corresponding side).

**Formal proof by Gram-Schmidt.** Apply Gram-Schmidt to $C_1,\ldots,C_n$: let $q_1,\ldots,q_n$ be the orthonormal basis, and $r_{ij}$ the coefficients. Then $A=QR$ with $R$ upper triangular, and $|\det A|=|\det Q||\det R|=\prod_i|r_{ii}|$. But $|r_{ii}|\le\|C_i\|$ (each $r_{ii}$ is the length of the component of $C_i$ orthogonal to the previous columns, hence bounded by $\|C_i\|$).

**Conclusion.** $|\det A|\le\prod\|C_i\|$, with equality iff the columns are orthogonal.

$$F'(x)=-e^{-x}+(x+1)e^{-x}=xe^{-x}.$$

For $A>0$,
$$\int_0^Axe^{-x}\,dx=F(A)-F(0)=1-(A+1)e^{-A}.$$

As $A\to+\infty$, $(A+1)e^{-A}\to0$ (exponential beats polynomial). So the integral converges and equals $1$.

## Statement

Prove that the series $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^2}$ converges.

## Hint

For $n\ge2$, compare $\frac1{n^2}$ with $\frac1{n(n-1)}=\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n$.

## Solution

For $n\ge2$ we have $n^2\ge n(n-1)$, so
$$\frac1{n^2}\le\frac1{n(n-1)}=\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n.$$

Let $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1{n^2}$. For $N\ge2$, the sum telescopes:
$$S_N\le1+\sum_{n=2}^N\left(\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n\right)=1+1-\frac1N<2.$$

The sequence $(S_N)$ is increasing (the terms are positive) and bounded above by $2$, so it converges. Hence the series converges.

## Statement

Let $H_n=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1k$. Show that $H_n\to+\infty$.

## Hint

Compare $H_{2^{k+1}}$ and $H_{2^k}$: the terms between them are all at least $\frac1{2^{k+1}}$.

## Solution

For $k\ge0$,
$$H_{2^{k+1}}-H_{2^k}=\sum_{j=2^k+1}^{2^{k+1}}\frac1j\ge2^k\cdot\frac1{2^{k+1}}=\frac12,$$
because the sum has $2^k$ terms, each at least $\frac1{2^{k+1}}$.

Adding these inequalities for $0,1,\dots,k-1$ and using $H_1=1$ gives
$$H_{2^k}\ge1+\frac k2.$$

The sequence $(H_n)$ is increasing and has a subsequence going to $+\infty$, so it is unbounded and $H_n\to+\infty$.

## Statement

A right triangle has legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$. Show that the radius of its inscribed circle is $r=\dfrac{a+b-c}{2}$. What is $r$ for the $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle?

## Hint

Use the fact that the two tangent segments from a vertex to a circle have equal length.

## Solution

Let the right angle be at $C$, with $CB=a$ and $CA=b$. The incircle touches the legs at two points. Together with $C$ and the centre $I$, these points form a square of side $r$ (all four angles are right angles and two adjacent sides equal $r$). So the tangent length from $C$ is $r$.

Hence the tangent length from $B$ is $a-r$ and from $A$ is $b-r$. The hypotenuse $AB$ is split by its point of tangency into these two lengths, so
$$(a-r)+(b-r)=c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r=\frac{a+b-c}{2}.$$

For the $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle, $r=\dfrac{3+4-5}{2}=1$.

## Statement

Prove that for all real numbers $a$ and $b$,
$$|\sin b-\sin a|\le|b-a|.$$

## Hint

Apply the Mean Value Theorem to $\sin$ on the interval between $a$ and $b$.

## Solution

If $a=b$ both sides are $0$. Otherwise assume $a<b$ (the other case is symmetric). The function $\sin$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, with derivative $\cos$.

By the Mean Value Theorem there is $c\in(a,b)$ such that

## Statement

Let $[AB]$ be a diameter of a circle with centre $O$, and let $M$ be a point of the circle different from $A$ and $B$. Prove that the angle $\widehat{AMB}$ is a right angle.

## Hint

Triangles $OAM$ and $OBM$ are isosceles.

## Solution

Since $OA=OM=OB$, the triangles $OAM$ and $OBM$ are isosceles at $O$. Let $\alpha=\widehat{OAM}=\widehat{OMA}$ and $\beta=\widehat{OBM}=\widehat{OMB}$.

The angle $\widehat{AMB}$ equals $\widehat{OMA}+\widehat{OMB}=\alpha+\beta$.

The angles of triangle $ABM$ are $\alpha$ (at $A$), $\beta$ (at $B$) and $\alpha+\beta$ (at $M$). Their sum is $180^\circ$, so $2(\alpha+\beta)=180^\circ$, hence $\widehat{AMB}=\alpha+\beta=90^\circ$.

## Statement

In a triangle $ABC$, show that the three medians meet at one point $G$, and that $G$ lies at two thirds of each median from its vertex.

## Hint

Work with position vectors and look at the point $G=\frac13(A+B+C)$.

## Solution

Identify points with their position vectors. Let $M=\frac12(B+C)$ be the midpoint of $BC$, so $AM$ is the median from $A$.

Define $G=\dfrac{A+B+C}3$. Then
$$A+\frac23(M-A)=\frac A3+\frac{2M}3=\frac A3+\frac{B+C}3=G.$$
So $G$ lies on the median $AM$, at two thirds of the way from $A$ to $M$.

## Statement

Prove that $\ln(1+x)\le x$ for every $x>-1$.

## Hint

Study $f(x)=x-\ln(1+x)$ on $(-1,+\infty)$.

## Solution

Let $f(x)=x-\ln(1+x)$ for $x>-1$. Then
$$f'(x)=1-\frac1{1+x}=\frac{x}{1+x}.$$
