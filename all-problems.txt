## Statement

Compute the determinant of each matrix and decide whether it is invertible:

(a) $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$;
(b) $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$;
(c) $C = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$.

## Hint

For a $2\times2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, the determinant is $ad-bc$. The matrix is invertible iff the determinant is nonzero.

## Solution

**(a)** $\det A = 3\cdot2 - 1\cdot5 = 6-5=1$. Invertible.

**(b)** $\det B = 1\cdot4 - 2\cdot2 = 0$. Not invertible.

**(c)** $\det C = \cos\theta\cdot\cos\theta - (-\sin\theta)(\sin\theta) = \cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta = 1$. Invertible for all $\theta$ (it is a rotation matrix).

## Statement

Compute the determinant of
$$M=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\0&4&5\\1&0&6\end{pmatrix}.$$

## Hint

Expand along the first row.

## Solution

Expanding along the first row,
$$\det M=1\begin{vmatrix}4&5\\0&6\end{vmatrix}-2\begin{vmatrix}0&5\\1&6\end{vmatrix}+3\begin{vmatrix}0&4\\1&0\end{vmatrix}.$$

## Statement

For $a,b>0$, prove that
$$(a+b)\left(\frac1a+\frac1b\right)\ge 4,$$
with equality if and only if $a=b$.

## Hint

Expand the product, then set $t=a/b$.

## Solution

Expanding gives $(a+b)\left(\frac1a+\frac1b\right)=2+\dfrac ab+\dfrac ba$.

Let $t=a/b>0$. Then $t+\dfrac1t-2=\dfrac{(t-1)^2}{t}\ge0$, so the product is at least $4$, with equality iff $t=1$, that is $a=b$.

## Statement

Show that the equation $x^3+x-1=0$ has exactly one real solution, and that it lies in $(0,1)$.

## Hint

Use the intermediate value theorem for existence and the derivative for uniqueness.

## Solution

Let $f(x)=x^3+x-1$. It is continuous, with $f(0)=-1<0$ and $f(1)=1>0$. By the intermediate value theorem, $f$ vanishes at some point of $(0,1)$.

Its derivative $f'(x)=3x^2+1$ is positive everywhere, so $f$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$. A strictly increasing function takes each value at most once, so the root is unique.

## Statement

Show that $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac2n\right)^n=e^2$.

## Hint

Write the expression as $\exp\!\big(n\ln(1+\tfrac2n)\big)$ and use $\ln(1+u)\sim u$ as $u\to0$.

## Solution

For $n\ge1$,
$$\left(1+\frac2n\right)^n=\exp\!\left(n\ln\!\left(1+\frac2n\right)\right).$$

Since $\exp$ is continuous, the original expression tends to $e^2$.

## Statement

A disease affects $1\%$ of a population. A test is positive for $99\%$ of the sick people and also for $5\%$ of the healthy people. A person tests positive. What is the probability that this person is sick?

## Hint

Use Bayes' formula with the events "sick" and "positive".

## Solution

Let $S$ be "sick" and $+$ be "positive". We know $P(S)=0.01$, $P(+\mid S)=0.99$ and $P(+\mid\bar S)=0.05$.

By the law of total probability,
$$P(+)=0.99\cdot0.01+0.05\cdot0.99=0.0099+0.0495=0.0594.$$

By Bayes' formula,
$$P(S\mid+)=\frac{P(+\mid S)P(S)}{P(+)}=\frac{0.0099}{0.0594}=\frac16\approx16.7\%.$$

Even with a good test, a positive result for a rare disease is far from certain, because healthy people are so much more numerous.

## Statement

Let $F=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3:\ x+y+z=0\}$. Show that $F$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$, and find a basis and the dimension of $F$.

## Hint

Check that $F$ contains $0$ and is stable under sums and scalar multiples. Then solve for $z$.

## Solution

**Subspace.** $(0,0,0)\in F$. If $(x,y,z),(x',y',z')\in F$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$, then
$$(x+\lambda x')+(y+\lambda y')+(z+\lambda z')=(x+y+z)+\lambda(x'+y'+z')=0,$$
so $(x,y,z)+\lambda(x',y',z')\in F$.

**Basis.** A vector of $F$ has $z=-x-y$, so
$$(x,y,z)=x\,(1,0,-1)+y\,(0,1,-1).$$
The vectors $(1,0,-1)$ and $(0,1,-1)$ span $F$ and are linearly independent (neither is a multiple of the other). They form a basis, and $\dim F=2$.

## Statement

Let $u_0=0$ and $u_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+u_n}$. Show that $(u_n)$ converges and find its limit.

## Hint

Show by induction that $0\le u_n<2$, then that $(u_n)$ is increasing.

## Solution

**Bounded.** We show $0\le u_n<2$ by induction. It holds for $n=0$. If $0\le u_n<2$, then $2\le2+u_n<4$, so $\sqrt2\le u_{n+1}<2$.

**Increasing.** Since all terms are non-negative,
$$u_{n+1}^2-u_n^2=2+u_n-u_n^2=(2-u_n)(1+u_n)>0,$$
so $u_{n+1}>u_n$.

**Limit.** An increasing sequence bounded above converges, say to $L$ with $0\le L\le2$. Passing to the limit in $u_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+u_n}$ gives $L=\sqrt{2+L}$, so $L^2-L-2=0$, that is $(L-2)(L+1)=0$. Since $L\ge0$, $L=2$.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$ with $abc=1$. Prove that $a+b+c\ge3$, and find when equality holds.

## Hint

First prove that $x^3+y^3+z^3\ge3xyz$ for $x,y,z\ge0$, using a factorisation. Then take cube roots of $a,b,c$.

## Solution

For $x,y,z\ge0$ we have the identity
$$x^3+y^3+z^3-3xyz=\tfrac12(x+y+z)\left[(x-y)^2+(y-z)^2+(z-x)^2\right]\ge0.$$

Apply it to $x=a^{1/3}$, $y=b^{1/3}$, $z=c^{1/3}$. Then $xyz=(abc)^{1/3}=1$ and $x^3+y^3+z^3=a+b+c$, so $a+b+c\ge3$.

Equality holds exactly when $x=y=z$, that is $a=b=c$, and with $abc=1$ this gives $a=b=c=1$.

## Statement

Solve in $\mathbb{R}$ the equation $|x-1|+|x+2|=5$.

## Hint

Split $\mathbb{R}$ into three intervals according to the signs of $x-1$ and $x+2$.

## Solution

**Case $x\ge1$.** The equation becomes $(x-1)+(x+2)=5$, that is $2x+1=5$, so $x=2$, which is in the interval.

**Case $-2\le x<1$.** The equation becomes $(1-x)+(x+2)=3=5$, which is impossible.

**Case $x<-2$.** The equation becomes $(1-x)+(-x-2)=5$, that is $-2x-1=5$, so $x=-3$, which is in the interval.

The solution set is $\{-3,\,2\}$.

## Statement

Show that $\displaystyle\int_0^{+\infty}x\,e^{-x}\,dx$ converges and compute its value.

## Hint

Find an antiderivative by parts, then let the upper bound go to infinity.

## Solution

An antiderivative of $xe^{-x}$ is $F(x)=-(x+1)e^{-x}$, because
$$F'(x)=-e^{-x}+(x+1)e^{-x}=xe^{-x}.$$

For $A>0$,
$$\int_0^Axe^{-x}\,dx=F(A)-F(0)=1-(A+1)e^{-A}.$$

As $A\to+\infty$, $(A+1)e^{-A}\to0$ (exponential beats polynomial). So the integral converges and equals $1$.

## Statement

Prove that for every real $x\ge-1$ and every integer $n\ge0$,
$$(1+x)^n\ge1+nx.$$

## Hint

Use induction on $n$, and remember that $1+x\ge0$.

## Solution

**Base case.** For $n=0$ both sides equal $1$.

**Induction step.** Assume $(1+x)^n\ge1+nx$. Since $1+x\ge0$, multiplying by $1+x$ keeps the inequality:
$$(1+x)^{n+1}\ge(1+nx)(1+x)=1+(n+1)x+nx^2\ge1+(n+1)x.$$

So the statement holds for $n+1$, and by induction for all $n\ge0$.

@@@ cantors-theorem.md
---
title: Cantor's theorem
category: logic/cardinality
level: l3
difficulty: 4
tags: diagonal argument, sets
date: 2026-10-31
---

## Statement

Let $E$ be a set and $\mathcal{P}(E)$ the set of its subsets. Prove that there is no surjective map from $E$ onto $\mathcal{P}(E)$.

## Hint

Given $f:E\to\mathcal{P}(E)$, consider $A=\{x\in E : x\notin f(x)\}$.

## Solution

Let $f:E\to\mathcal{P}(E)$ be any map and define
$$A=\{x\in E:\ x\notin f(x)\}\in\mathcal{P}(E).$$

Suppose $f$ is surjective. Then $A=f(a)$ for some $a\in E$.

- If $a\in A$, then by the definition of $A$ we have $a\notin f(a)=A$, a contradiction.
- If $a\notin A$, then $a\notin f(a)$ fails, so $a\in f(a)=A$, a contradiction.

Both cases are impossible, so $f$ is not surjective. Since $f$ was arbitrary, no surjection $E\to\mathcal{P}(E)$ exists.

## Statement

Let $n\ge2$ be an integer. For $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$, write $a\equiv b\pmod n$ when $n$ divides $a-b$. Prove that $\equiv$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$, and say how many equivalence classes it has.

## Hint

Check reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity, then use division with remainder.

## Solution

**Reflexive.** $a-a=0$ is divisible by $n$.

**Symmetric.** If $n\mid a-b$ then $n\mid b-a=-(a-b)$.

**Transitive.** If $n\mid a-b$ and $n\mid b-c$, then $n$ divides the sum $(a-b)+(b-c)=a-c$.

**Classes.** Every integer $a$ can be written $a=qn+r$ with $0\le r<n$, so $a\equiv r$. Two different remainders $r\ne r'$ in $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ differ by less than $n$ and are not $0$, so they are not congruent. There are exactly $n$ classes.

## Statement

Prove that the series $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^2}$ converges.

## Hint

For $n\ge2$, compare $\frac1{n^2}$ with $\frac1{n(n-1)}=\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n$.

## Solution

For $n\ge2$ we have $n^2\ge n(n-1)$, so
$$\frac1{n^2}\le\frac1{n(n-1)}=\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n.$$

Let $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1{n^2}$. For $N\ge2$, the sum telescopes:
$$S_N\le1+\sum_{n=2}^N\left(\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n\right)=1+1-\frac1N<2.$$

The sequence $(S_N)$ is increasing (the terms are positive) and bounded above by $2$, so it converges. Hence the series converges.

## Statement

Let $A$ and $B$ be subsets of a set $E$. Prove that
$$E\setminus(A\cup B)=(E\setminus A)\cap(E\setminus B).$$

## Hint

## Solution

Let $x\in E$. Then
$$x\in E\setminus(A\cup B)\iff x\notin A\cup B\iff \text{not}\,(x\in A\text{ or }x\in B)\iff x\notin A\text{ and }x\notin B.$$

## Statement

Let $H_n=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1k$. Show that $H_n\to+\infty$.

## Hint

Compare $H_{2^{k+1}}$ and $H_{2^k}$: the terms between them are all at least $\frac1{2^{k+1}}$.

## Solution

For $k\ge0$,
$$H_{2^{k+1}}-H_{2^k}=\sum_{j=2^k+1}^{2^{k+1}}\frac1j\ge2^k\cdot\frac1{2^{k+1}}=\frac12,$$

## Statement

Compute $\gcd(252,198)$ with the Euclidean algorithm, then find integers $u,v$ with $252u+198v=\gcd(252,198)$.

## Hint

Run the divisions, then substitute backwards from the last non-zero remainder.

## Solution

## Statement

Prove that $e^x\ge1+x$ for every real $x$, with equality only for $x=0$.

## Hint

Study the function $f(x)=e^x-1-x$.

## Solution

Let $f(x)=e^x-1-x$. Then $f'(x)=e^x-1$.

- For $x<0$ we have $e^x<1$, so $f'(x)<0$ and $f$ is strictly decreasing on $(-\infty,0]$.

Count the total number of hands shaken in two ways.

## Solution

Let $d_i$ be the number of hands shaken by person $i$. Each handshake involves two people, so
$$\sum_id_i=2h,$$
where $h$ is the total number of handshakes. This sum is even.

Modulo $2$, each $d_i$ is $0$ or $1$, so the sum is congruent to the number of people with $d_i$ odd. Since the sum is even, that number is even.

## Statement

$n$ people leave their hats at a cloakroom. The hats are returned in a uniformly random order. Show that the expected number of people who get their own hat is $1$, whatever $n$ is.

## Hint

Write the number of matches as a sum of indicator variables and use linearity of expectation.

## Solution

Let $X_i=1$ if person $i$ gets their own hat and $X_i=0$ otherwise. The number of matches is $X=X_1+\cdots+X_n$.

By symmetry, person $i$ is equally likely to receive any of the $n$ hats, so $E[X_i]=P(X_i=1)=\dfrac1n$.

Linearity of expectation (which does not need the $X_i$ to be independent) gives
$$E[X]=\sum_{i=1}^nE[X_i]=n\cdot\frac1n=1.$$

## Statement

Show that there is a bijection from $\mathbb{N}$ to $\mathbb{Z}$.

## Hint

Send even numbers to non-negative integers and odd numbers to negative integers.

## Solution

Define $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z}$ by
$$f(n)=\begin{cases}\dfrac n2&\text{if }n\text{ is even},\\[2mm]-\dfrac{n+1}{2}&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}$$
The values start $0,-1,1,-2,2,-3,\dots$

On even numbers $n=2k$ we have $f(n)=k$, which takes every non-negative integer exactly once. On odd numbers $n=2k+1$ we have $f(n)=-(k+1)$, which takes every negative integer exactly once.

The two sets of values are disjoint and together cover $\mathbb{Z}$, so $f$ is a bijection.

## Statement

What is the last digit of $7^{2026}$?

## Hint

Write out the last digits of the first few powers of $7$ and look for a cycle.

## Solution

The last digits of $7^1,7^2,7^3,7^4$ are $7,9,3,1$, and $7^4\equiv1\pmod{10}$, so the pattern repeats with period $4$.

Since $2026=4\cdot506+2$,
$$7^{2026}=\left(7^4\right)^{506}\cdot7^2\equiv49\equiv9\pmod{10}.$$

The last digit is $9$.

## Statement

Prove that $\ln(1+x)\le x$ for every $x>-1$.

## Hint

Study $f(x)=x-\ln(1+x)$ on $(-1,+\infty)$.

## Solution

Let $f(x)=x-\ln(1+x)$ for $x>-1$. Then
$$f'(x)=1-\frac1{1+x}=\frac{x}{1+x}.$$

Since $1+x>0$, $f'(x)<0$ on $(-1,0)$ and $f'(x)>0$ on $(0,+\infty)$. So $f$ has its minimum at $0$, where $f(0)=0$. Hence $f(x)\ge0$, that is $\ln(1+x)\le x$.

## Statement

Prove that $n^5-n$ is divisible by $30$ for every integer $n$.

## Hint

Factor $n^5-n=n(n-1)(n+1)(n^2+1)$ and check divisibility by $2$, $3$ and $5$ separately.

## Solution

We have $n^5-n=(n-1)\,n\,(n+1)\,(n^2+1)$.

- **By 2 and 3.** The product $(n-1)n(n+1)$ of three consecutive integers is divisible by $2$ and by $3$.
- **By 5.** Look at $n$ modulo $5$. If $n\equiv0,1$ or $4$, one of $n$, $n-1$, $n+1$ is divisible by $5$. If $n\equiv2$ or $3$, then $n^2\equiv4$, so $n^2+1\equiv0\pmod5$.

The numbers $2,3,5$ are pairwise coprime, so $n^5-n$ is divisible by $2\cdot3\cdot5=30$.

## Statement

A bag contains $3$ red balls and $2$ blue balls. Two balls are drawn at random without replacement. What is the probability that they have the same colour?

## Hint

Count the pairs of balls: all pairs, then the pairs of the same colour.

## Solution

There are $\binom52=10$ equally likely pairs of balls.

Pairs of the same colour: $\binom32=3$ red pairs and $\binom22=1$ blue pair, so $4$ in total.

The probability is $\dfrac4{10}=\dfrac25$.

## Statement

Two fair six-sided dice are rolled. Given that at least one die shows a $6$, what is the probability that the sum is $8$?

## Hint

Count the outcomes in the conditioned sample space first.

## Solution

There are $36$ equally likely outcomes. Those with at least one $6$ number $36-25=11$.

Among them, the sum is $8$ only for $(6,2)$ and $(2,6)$. The probability is
$$P(S=8\mid \text{at least one }6)=\frac{2}{11}.$$

## Statement

A fair die is rolled repeatedly until a six appears. What is the expected number of rolls?

## Hint

Condition on the first roll: either you are done, or you start again.

## Solution

Let $T$ be the number of rolls and $E=E[T]$, which is finite because $T$ has a geometric distribution. With probability $\frac16$ the first roll is a six and we stop after $1$ roll. Otherwise, with probability $\frac56$, we have used one roll and are back to the start. So
$$E=1+\frac56E\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac16E=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E=6.$$

**Check with the distribution.** $P(T=k)=\left(\frac56\right)^{k-1}\frac16$, and with $q=\frac56$,
$$E[T]=\frac16\sum_{k\ge1}kq^{k-1}=\frac16\cdot\frac1{(1-q)^2}=\frac16\cdot36=6.$$
