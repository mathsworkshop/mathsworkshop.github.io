## Statement

Compute
$$\lim_{x\to0^+}\int_x^{3x}\frac{1}{te^t}\,dt.$$

## Hint

Apply the mean value theorem for integrals: for continuous $f$ and $g$ with $g\ge0$, there exists $c$ between the bounds with $\int_a^b f g=f(c)\int_a^b g$.

## Solution

**Mean value theorem.** Fix $x>0$. On $[x,3x]$, take $f(t)=\dfrac{1}{e^t}$ (continuous) and $g(t)=\dfrac{1}{t}$ (continuous, positive on $[x,3x]$). There exists $c_x\in[x,3x]$ such that
$$\int_x^{3x}\frac{1}{te^t}\,dt=\frac{1}{e^{c_x}}\int_x^{3x}\frac{1}{t}\,dt=\frac{\ln 3}{e^{c_x}}.$$

**Limit.** Since $c_x\in[x,3x]$ and $x\to0^+$, we have $c_x\to0^+$, hence $e^{c_x}\to1$. Therefore
$$\lim_{x\to0^+}\int_x^{3x}\frac{1}{te^t}\,dt=\frac{\ln 3}{1}=\ln 3.$$

## Statement

Compute
$$\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{n^2+1}^{n^2+n+1}\frac{\arctan(t^2)}{\sqrt t}\,dt.$$

## Hint

Apply the mean value theorem for integrals with $f(t)=\arctan(t^2)$ and $g(t)=1/\sqrt t$; estimate $c_n\in[n^2+1,n^2+n+1]$ and use $\arctan(c_n^2)\to\pi/2$.

## Solution

**Mean value theorem.** On $[n^2+1,n^2+n+1]$, $f(t)=\arctan(t^2)$ is continuous and $g(t)=1/\sqrt t$ is continuous and positive. There exists $c_n\in[n^2+1,n^2+n+1]$ with
$$\int_{n^2+1}^{n^2+n+1}\frac{\arctan(t^2)}{\sqrt t}\,dt=\arctan(c_n^2)\int_{n^2+1}^{n^2+n+1}\frac{dt}{\sqrt t}.$$

**Compute the remaining integral.**
$$\int_{n^2+1}^{n^2+n+1}\frac{dt}{\sqrt t}=\bigl[2\sqrt t\bigr]_{n^2+1}^{n^2+n+1}=2\sqrt{n^2+n+1}-2\sqrt{n^2+1}.$$
Rationalize:
$$2\sqrt{n^2+n+1}-2\sqrt{n^2+1}=\frac{2n}{\sqrt{n^2+n+1}+\sqrt{n^2+1}}.$$
As $n\to+\infty$, the denominator is $\sim 2n$, so the quotient tends to $1$. More precisely, it equals
$$\frac{2n}{n\sqrt{1+\frac1n+\frac1{n^2}}+n\sqrt{1+\frac1{n^2}}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+\frac1n+\frac1{n^2}}+\sqrt{1+\frac1{n^2}}}\to\frac{2}{1+1}=1.$$

**Limit of $\arctan(c_n^2)$.** Since $c_n\ge n^2+1\to+\infty$, we have $c_n^2\to+\infty$, so $\arctan(c_n^2)\to\pi/2$.

**Conclusion.** The product of these two limits gives
$$\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{n^2+1}^{n^2+n+1}\frac{\arctan(t^2)}{\sqrt t}\,dt=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot1=\frac{\pi}{2}.$$

## Statement

Compute
$$\lim_{x\to1^+}\int_x^{x^2}\frac{\cos(\pi t)}{\ln t}\,dt.$$

## Hint

The integrand is singular at $t=1$, so rewrite the integral using the mean value theorem with $f(t)=t\cos(\pi t)$ and $g(t)=\frac{1}{t\ln t}$. Then estimate $c_x\in[x,x^2]$ and let $x\to1^+$.

## Solution

**Rewriting the integrand.** Note that for $t>1$,
$$\frac{\cos(\pi t)}{\ln t}=t\cos(\pi t)\cdot\frac{1}{t\ln t}.$$

**Mean value theorem for integrals.** On $[x,x^2]$ (with $1<x$), the function $t\mapsto t\cos(\pi t)$ is continuous and $t\mapsto\frac{1}{t\ln t}$ is continuous and positive. There exists $c_x\in[x,x^2]$ with
$$\int_x^{x^2}\frac{\cos(\pi t)}{\ln t}\,dt=c_x\cos(\pi c_x)\int_x^{x^2}\frac{dt}{t\ln t}.$$

**Compute the remaining integral.**
$$\int_x^{x^2}\frac{dt}{t\ln t}=\bigl[\ln(\ln t)\bigr]_x^{x^2}=\ln(\ln(x^2))-\ln(\ln x)=\ln 2.$$
So
$$\int_x^{x^2}\frac{\cos(\pi t)}{\ln t}\,dt=c_x\cos(\pi c_x)\ln 2.$$

**Limit as $x\to1^+$.** Since $c_x\in[x,x^2]$ and $x\to1^+$, we have $c_x\to1$. Thus $c_x\cos(\pi c_x)\to1\cdot\cos(\pi)=-1$. Therefore
$$\lim_{x\to1^+}\int_x^{x^2}\frac{\cos(\pi t)}{\ln t}\,dt=(-1)\cdot\ln 2=-\ln 2.$$

## Statement

Show that
$$\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sqrt[n]{(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(2n)}=\frac{4}{e}.$$

## Hint

Take the logarithm of the product, factor out $n$ in each factor, and recognize a Riemann sum for $\int_1^2\ln t\,dt$.

## Solution

**Step 1: take logarithms.** Let
$$u_n=\frac{1}{n}\sqrt[n]{(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(2n)}>0.$$
Then
$$\ln(u_n)=-\ln n+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ln(n+k).$$
Factor $n$ in each factor inside the sum:
$$\ln(n+k)=\ln n+\ln\!\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right).$$
Therefore
$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ln(n+k)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\ln n+\ln\!\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right)\right)=\ln n+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right),$$
since the constant $\ln n$ is summed $n$ times and then divided by $n$. Hence
$$\ln(u_n)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right).$$

**Step 2: Riemann sum.** The expression
$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right)$$
is a left Riemann sum for the continuous function $t\mapsto\ln(1+t)$ on $[0,1]$: the sample points are $t_k=k/n$ with step $1/n$, for $k=1,\ldots,n$, i.e. on the partition $[0,1/n],[1/n,2/n],\ldots,[(n-1)/n,1]$. As $n\to+\infty$,
$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right)\longrightarrow\int_0^1\ln(1+t)\,dt.$$

**Step 3: compute the integral.** Substituting $s=1+t$, $ds=dt$, with $s$ from $1$ to $2$:
$$\int_0^1\ln(1+t)\,dt=\int_1^2\ln s\,ds.$$
An antiderivative of $\ln s$ is $s\ln s-s$; hence
$$\int_1^2\ln s\,ds=\bigl[s\ln s-s\bigr]_1^2=(2\ln2-2)-(0-1)=2\ln2-1.$$
So
$$\ln(u_n)\longrightarrow 2\ln 2-1=\ln 4-\ln e=\ln\!\left(\frac{4}{e}\right).$$

**Step 4: exponentiate.** By continuity of $\exp$,
$$u_n=\exp(\ln u_n)\longrightarrow\exp\!\left(\ln\frac{4}{e}\right)=\frac{4}{e}.$$

## Statement

For $x>0$, define
$$F(x)=\int_{1/x}^{x}\frac{t}{1+t+t^2+t^3}\,dt.$$

(a) Show that $F$ is of class $C^1$ on $]0,+\infty[$ and that
$$F'(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^2},\quad \forall x>0.$$

(b) Deduce that
$$\int_{1/x}^{x}\frac{t}{1+t+t^2+t^3}\,dt=\arctan(x)-\frac{\pi}{4},\quad \forall x>0.$$
In particular, for $x=\sqrt3$,
$$\int_{1/\sqrt3}^{\sqrt3}\frac{t}{1+t+t^2+t^3}\,dt=\frac{\pi}{12}.$$

## Hint

For (a), write $F(x)=G(x)-G(1/x)$ where $G$ is an antiderivative of $t\mapsto\frac{t}{1+t+t^2+t^3}$, then differentiate using the chain rule. Simplify the resulting expression using the factorization $1+t+t^2+t^3=(1+t)(1+t^2)$. For (b), determine $F(1)$ and use the fact that two functions with the same derivative differ by a constant.

## Solution

**(a) Differentiability.** Let $g(t)=\dfrac{t}{1+t+t^2+t^3}$, which is continuous on $]0,+\infty[$ (the denominator is positive for $t>0$). By the fundamental theorem of calculus, $G(x)=\int_1^x g(t)\,dt$ is $C^1$ on $]0,+\infty[$ with $G'(x)=g(x)$. Now
$$F(x)=G(x)-G(1/x).$$
The map $x\mapsto 1/x$ is $C^1$ on $]0,+\infty[$, so by composition $G(1/x)$ is $C^1$ and
$$\frac{d}{dx}G(1/x)=g(1/x)\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{x^2}\right).$$
Thus
$$F'(x)=g(x)+\frac{1}{x^2}\,g(1/x).$$

**Computation.** We have
$$g(x)=\frac{x}{1+x+x^2+x^3},\qquad \frac{1}{x^2}\,g(1/x)=\frac{1}{x^2}\cdot\frac{1/x}{1+1/x+1/x^2+1/x^3}.$$
Multiply numerator and denominator of $g(1/x)$ by $x^3$:
$$g(1/x)=\frac{x^2}{x^3+x^2+x+1}.$$
Hence
$$\frac{1}{x^2}\,g(1/x)=\frac{1}{x^3+x^2+x+1}.$$
Adding:
$$F'(x)=\frac{x}{1+x+x^2+x^3}+\frac{1}{1+x+x^2+x^3}=\frac{x+1}{1+x+x^2+x^3}.$$
Now factor the denominator:
$$1+x+x^2+x^3=(1+x)(1+x^2).$$
Indeed $(1+x)(1+x^2)=1+x+x^2+x^3$. Therefore
$$F'(x)=\frac{x+1}{(1+x)(1+x^2)}=\frac{1}{1+x^2}.$$

**Conclusion (a).** $F$ is $C^1$ on $]0,+\infty[$ and $F'(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$.

**(b) Identification.** The function $x\mapsto\arctan(x)$ has derivative $\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$ on $]0,+\infty[$. Therefore $F-\arctan$ is constant on $]0,+\infty[$:
$$F(x)-\arctan(x)=C.$$
Evaluate at $x=1$: the lower and upper bounds coincide, so $F(1)=0$. Also $\arctan(1)=\pi/4$. Hence
$$C=0-\frac{\pi}{4}=-\frac{\pi}{4},$$
i.e.
$$F(x)=\arctan(x)-\frac{\pi}{4}.$$

**Special value.** For $x=\sqrt3$, $\arctan(\sqrt3)=\pi/3$, so
$$F(\sqrt3)=\frac{\pi}{3}-\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\pi}{12}.$$

**Conclusion (b).** For every $x>0$,
$$\int_{1/x}^{x}\frac{t}{1+t+t^2+t^3}\,dt=\arctan(x)-\frac{\pi}{4},$$
and in particular
$$\int_{1/\sqrt3}^{\sqrt3}\frac{t}{1+t+t^2+t^3}\,dt=\frac{\pi}{12}.$$

## Statement

Let $\lfloor x\rfloor$ denote the integer part (floor) of $x$, and define $f:[0,4]\to\mathbb{R}$ by
$$f(x)=(-1)^{\lfloor x\rfloor}\,\lfloor x\rfloor.$$
Show that $f$ is a step function on $[0,4]$ and compute
$$\int_0^4 f(x)\,dx.$$

## Hint

Identify the subdivision points $0,1,2,3,4$ and the constant value of $f$ on each open interval $]k,k+1[$; then sum $c_k\cdot 1$.

## Solution

**Step function.** On each open interval $]k,k+1[$ with $k\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, the integer part equals $k$, so $f$ is constant there with value
$$c_k=(-1)^k\,k.$$
Hence $f$ is a step function associated with the subdivision $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$. The values at the subdivision points do not affect the integral.

**Values on each subinterval.**
- $]0,1[$: $c_0=(-1)^0\cdot 0=0$,
- $]1,2[$: $c_1=(-1)^1\cdot 1=-1$,
- $]2,3[$: $c_2=(-1)^2\cdot 2=2$,
- $]3,4[$: $c_3=(-1)^3\cdot 3=-3$.

**Integral.**
$$\int_0^4 f=\sum_{k=0}^{3}c_k\cdot 1=0+(-1)+2+(-3)=-2.$$

**Conclusion.** $f$ is a step function on $[0,4]$ and
$$\int_0^4(-1)^{\lfloor x\rfloor}\,\lfloor x\rfloor\,dx=-2.$$

## Statement

Let $f:[0,3]\to\mathbb{R}$ be defined by
$$f(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }0\le x<1,\\ 3&\text{if }1\le x<2,\\ -1&\text{if }2\le x\le3.\end{cases}$$
Show that $f$ is a step function and compute $\int_0^3 f(x)\,dx$.

## Hint

A step function is constant on the intervals of a partition. Identify the subdivision and sum $\sum_k c_k(x_k-x_{k-1})$.

## Solution

**Step function.** Take the subdivision $\sigma=\{0,1,2,3\}$. On each open interval $]0,1[$, $]1,2[$, $]2,3[$, $f$ is constant ($1,3,-1$ respectively). The values at the subdivision points do not matter for the integral, so $f$ is a step function.

**Integral.**
$$\int_0^3 f=\sum_{k=1}^3 c_k(x_k-x_{k-1})=1\cdot1+3\cdot1+(-1)\cdot1=3.$$

**Conclusion.** $f$ is a step function and $\int_0^3 f=3$.

## Statement

Let $f:[0,2]\to\mathbb{R}$ be the step function associated with the subdivision $\sigma=\{0,1,2\}$ with values $c_1=2$, $c_2=5$ on the two subintervals. Show that the "integral" $\sum_k c_k(x_k-x_{k-1})$ does not depend on the subdivision.

## Hint

Refine the subdivision: replace $[0,1]$ by $[0,1/2]\cup[1/2,1]$, both with value $2$. Compare.

## Solution

**Original subdivision.** $\int f=2\cdot1+5\cdot1=7$.

**Refined subdivision.** $\sigma'=\{0,1/2,1,2\}$ with values $(2,2,5)$:
$$2\cdot\tfrac12+2\cdot\tfrac12+5\cdot1=1+1+5=7.$$

**General principle.** If $\sigma'\supseteq\sigma$ refines $\sigma$, the subinterval $[x_{k-1},x_k]$ is split into $[x_{k-1},y]\cup[y,x_k]$ with the same value $c_k$, contributing $c_k(y-x_{k-1})+c_k(x_k-y)=c_k(x_k-x_{k-1})$: the sum is unchanged.

**Conclusion.** The integral of a step function is well-defined.

## Statement

Let $f,g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ be step functions and $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$. Show that $f+\lambda g$ is a step function and that
$$\int_a^b(f+\lambda g)=\int_a^bf+\lambda\int_a^bg.$$

## Hint

Take the common refinement of the two subdivisions, then use linearity of finite sums.

## Solution

**Common refinement.** Let $\sigma_f$ and $\sigma_g$ be subdivisions adapted to $f$ and $g$. Take $\sigma=\sigma_f\cup\sigma_g$ (the union of all subdivision points). On each interval of $\sigma$, both $f$ and $g$ are constant, say $f=u_k$, $g=v_k$.

**Compute.**
$$\int_a^b(f+\lambda g)=\sum_k(u_k+\lambda v_k)(x_k-x_{k-1})=\sum_ku_k\Delta_k+\lambda\sum_kv_k\Delta_k.$$
This is $\int f+\lambda\int g$.

**Conclusion.** The integral is linear on the space of step functions.

## Statement

Let $f:[a,c]\to\mathbb{R}$ be a step function, and $b\in]a,c[$. Show that
$$\int_a^cf=\int_a^bf+\int_b^cf.$$

## Hint

Add $b$ to the subdivision of $f$ and compare sums.

## Solution

**Refinement.** Let $\sigma$ be a subdivision of $[a,c]$ adapted to $f$. Set $\sigma'=\sigma\cup\{b\}$: still adapted to $f$. The sum over $\sigma'$ splits into the sum over the parts of $[a,b]$ and over the parts of $[b,c]$.

**Sum.** $\sum_{k}\Delta_kc_k=\sum_{\Delta_k\subseteq[a,b]}c_k\Delta_k+\sum_{\Delta_k\subseteq[b,c]}c_k\Delta_k=\int_a^bf+\int_b^cf$.

**Conclusion.** Chasles' relation holds for step functions.

Let $f,g$ be step functions on $[a,b]$ with $f\le g$. Show that $\int_a^bf\le\int_a^bg$.

## Hint

Compare the two sums on a common refinement.

## Solution

**Common refinement.** Choose a common subdivision $\sigma$ of $[a,b]$. On each $]x_{k-1},x_k[$, $f\le g$ implies $c_k\le d_k$.

## Statement

Let $f\ge0$ be a step function on $[a,b]$ with $\int_a^bf=0$. Show that $f$ vanishes at every point of continuity of $f$.

## Hint

If $f$ were positive on some open interval, the integral would be positive.

## Solution

**Setup.** Suppose $f(x_0)>0$ at a continuity point $x_0$. By continuity, there is a neighbourhood $]x_0-\varepsilon,x_0+\varepsilon[$ on which $f\ge f(x_0)/2>0$.

**Positive integral.** The integral of $f$ over $[a,b]$ is at least $(f(x_0)/2)\cdot2\varepsilon$ if the whole neighbourhood lies in $[a,b]$, contradicting $\int f=0$. (If $x_0$ is an endpoint, a similar half-neighbourhood argument applies.)

**Conclusion.** $f$ vanishes at each continuity point.

## Statement

Let $f$ be a step function on $[a,b]$. Show that its lower and upper Darboux sums (over the subdivision adapted to $f$) coincide, and deduce that $f$ is Riemann integrable with $\int_a^bf$ defined as usual.

## Hint

On the intervals of the adapted subdivision, $\inf f=\sup f=c_k$.

## Solution

**Darboux sums.** Take a subdivision $\sigma$ adapted to $f$. On each $]x_{k-1},x_k[$, $f=c_k$, so $\inf=c_k=\sup$. Hence
$$L(f,\sigma)=\sum_kc_k\Delta_k=U(f,\sigma).$$

**Riemann integrability.** Since $\inf_\sigma L(f,\sigma)=\sup_\sigma U(f,\sigma)$, $f$ is Riemann integrable with value $\int_a^bf$.

**Conclusion.** Step functions are Riemann integrable with the expected integral.

## Statement

Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ be bounded. Suppose that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exist step functions $\varphi\le f\le\psi$ with $\int_a^b(\psi-\varphi)<\varepsilon$. Show that $f$ is Riemann integrable.

## Hint

The lower and upper Darboux integrals of $f$ differ by at most $\int(\psi-\varphi)$.

## Solution

**Darboux integrals.** For every $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\varphi\le f\le\psi$ with $\int(\psi-\varphi)<\varepsilon$. Monotonicity gives
$$\int_a^b\varphi\le\int_a^{\underline b}f\le\int_a^{\overline b}f\le\int_a^b\psi,$$
so
$$0\le\int_a^{\overline b}f-\int_a^{\underline b}f<\varepsilon.$$

**Conclusion.** Since this holds for every $\varepsilon>0$, the lower and upper Darboux integrals coincide: $f$ is Riemann integrable.

## Statement

Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ be monotone (say nondecreasing). Show that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exist step functions $\varphi\le f\le\psi$ with $\int(\psi-\varphi)<\varepsilon$.

## Hint

Take a subdivision with small mesh $\eta$; define $\varphi$ and $\psi$ constant on each interval with values $f(x_{k-1})$ and $f(x_k)$.

## Solution

**Subdivision.** Since $f$ is bounded (being monotone on a compact interval), let $M=f(b)-f(a)$. Choose a subdivision $\sigma=\{a=x_0<\cdots<x_n=b\}$ with $\max_k\Delta_k<\varepsilon/M$.

**Step functions.** Define $\varphi(x)=f(x_{k-1})$ and $\psi(x)=f(x_k)$ on $]x_{k-1},x_k[$, with arbitrary values at the points $x_k$. Monotonicity ensures $\varphi\le f\le\psi$.

**Integral difference.**
$$\int_a^b(\psi-\varphi)=\sum_k(f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1}))\Delta_k<\eta\sum_k(f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1}))=\eta\cdot M<\varepsilon.$$

**Conclusion.** $f$ is Riemann integrable (by the squeeze criterion).

## Statement

Summarize the main facts about step functions and their role in Riemann integration.

## Hint

Review the definitions and key properties.

## Solution

**Step function.** Constant on the intervals of a subdivision of $[a,b]$.

**Integral.** $\int_a^bf=\sum_kc_k\Delta_k$ (independent of the subdivision).

**Properties.**
- Linearity: $\int(f+\lambda g)=\int f+\lambda\int g$.
- Additivity (Chasles): $\int_a^cf=\int_a^bf+\int_b^cf$.
- Monotonicity: $f\le g\Rightarrow\int f\le\int g$.
- Triangle inequality: $|\int f|\le\int|f|$.
- Zero integral: $f\ge0$ and $\int f=0\Rightarrow f$ vanishes at continuity points.

**Darboux sums.** For step functions, lower and upper sums coincide; step functions are Riemann integrable.

**Squeeze criterion.** $f$ is Riemann integrable iff for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exist step functions $\varphi\le f\le\psi$ with $\int(\psi-\varphi)<\varepsilon$.

**Applications.** Monotone functions and continuous functions are Riemann integrable (approximated by step functions).

**Composition.** $\Phi\circ f$ is Riemann integrable when $f$ is and $\Phi$ is continuous.

**Conclusion.** Step functions are the elementary building blocks of the Riemann integral; integrability is captured by their approximation.

$$\int_\varepsilon^1\frac{dx}{x^p}=\frac{1-\varepsilon^{1-p}}{1-p}.$$

- If $p=1$: $\int_\varepsilon^1\frac{dx}x=-\ln\varepsilon\to+\infty$: divergence.

So $\int_0^1x^{-p}dx$ converges **iff $p<1$**.

## Solution

**1.** The integrand is continuous on $[0,1]$, so only $[1,+\infty)$ matters. For $x\ge1$, $x^2\ge x$ so $e^{-x^2}\le e^{-x}$, and $\int_1^\infty e^{-x}dx=e^{-1}<\infty$. The integral **converges**.

**2.** For $x\ge1$, $x^3+x\ge x^3$, hence $\frac1{\sqrt{x^3+x}}\le\frac1{x^{3/2}}$. Since $\frac32>1$, $\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}dx$ converges. The integral **converges**.

**3.** For $x\ge2$, $\ln x<x$, so $\frac1{\ln x}>\frac1x$. Since $\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}x=+\infty$, the integral **diverges**.

**Remark.** In (1) the exact value is $\frac{\sqrt\pi}2$, but it cannot be found with an elementary antiderivative; the comparison test proves convergence without knowing the value.

## Statement

Show that $\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}$ converges and compute it.

## Hint

The integrand blows up at $0$ and at $1$. Substitute $x=\sin^2\theta$.

## Solution

**Convergence.** Near $0$, $\frac1{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}\sim\frac1{\sqrt x}$ and $\int_0\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}$ converges ($p=\frac12<1$). Near $1$, the integrand behaves like $\frac1{\sqrt{1-x}}$, which also gives a convergent integral. So the integral converges.

**Value.** Let $x=\sin^2\theta$ with $\theta\in\left(0,\frac\pi2\right)$. Then $dx=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{x(1-x)}=\sin\theta\cos\theta$, so
$$\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}=\int_0^{\pi/2}2\,d\theta=\pi.$$

**Remark.** Since the substitution is a bijection between the open intervals and the integrand becomes constant, the singularities disappear completely. This integral is the Beta function value $B\left(\frac12,\frac12\right)=\pi$.

## Statement

1. Show that $\displaystyle\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{\sin x}x\,dx$ converges.
2. Show that it is **not** absolutely convergent: $\displaystyle\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{|\sin x|}x\,dx=+\infty$.

## Hint

At $0$ the integrand extends continuously. At infinity, integrate by parts to gain a factor $\frac1x$. For part 2, bound $\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi}\frac{|\sin x|}x\,dx$ from below.

## Solution

**Near $0$.** Since $\frac{\sin x}x\to1$, the integrand extends to a continuous function on $[0,1]$: no problem there.

**1. At infinity.** For $R>1$, integrate by parts with $u=\frac1x$, $v'=\sin x$:
$$\int_1^R\frac{\sin x}x\,dx=\left[-\frac{\cos x}x\right]_1^R-\int_1^R\frac{\cos x}{x^2}\,dx=\cos1-\frac{\cos R}R-\int_1^R\frac{\cos x}{x^2}\,dx.$$
As $R\to+\infty$, $\frac{\cos R}R\to0$, and $\int_1^\infty\frac{|\cos x|}{x^2}dx\le\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2}<\infty$, so $\int_1^R\frac{\cos x}{x^2}dx$ converges. Hence $\int_1^R\frac{\sin x}xdx$ has a limit: the integral converges.

**2. Not absolutely.** On $[k\pi,(k+1)\pi]$, $x\le(k+1)\pi$ and $\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi}|\sin x|\,dx=2$, so
$$\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi}\frac{|\sin x|}x\,dx\ge\frac2{(k+1)\pi}.$$
Summing over $k=0,\dots,N-1$ gives at least $\frac2\pi\sum_{k=1}^N\frac1k=\frac2\pi H_N\to+\infty$ (harmonic series). So the absolute integral diverges.

**Remark.** The integral converges only thanks to cancellations between positive and negative lobes. Its exact value is $\frac\pi2$ (the Dirichlet integral), which can be computed with differentiation under the integral sign or complex analysis.
