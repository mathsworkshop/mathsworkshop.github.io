## Statement

Let $P$ be a real polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ all of whose $n$ complex roots (counted with multiplicity) are real. Prove that $P'$ also has all its $n-1$ roots real.

## Hint

Let $r_1<\dots<r_k$ be the distinct roots of $P$, with multiplicities $m_1,\dots,m_k$. First find how many roots of $P'$ sit at the points $r_i$ themselves, then look for roots between consecutive $r_i$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Count the roots of $P'$ that we can *see* and show that there are already $n-1=\deg P'$ of them.

**Roots at the $r_i$.** Write $P(x)=(x-r_i)^{m_i}Q(x)$ with $Q(r_i)\ne0$. Then $P'(x)=(x-r_i)^{m_i-1}\bigl(m_iQ(x)+(x-r_i)Q'(x)\bigr)$ and the second factor equals $m_iQ(r_i)\ne0$ at $r_i$. So $r_i$ is a root of $P'$ of multiplicity exactly $m_i-1$ (no root if $m_i=1$). Together these give $\sum_i(m_i-1)=n-k$ roots of $P'$.

**Roots between the $r_i$.** For $1\le i\le k-1$, $P(r_i)=P(r_{i+1})=0$, so by Rolle's theorem $P'$ vanishes at some point of the open interval $(r_i,r_{i+1})$. These $k-1$ points are distinct from each other and from all the $r_j$.

**Conclusion.** $P'$ has at least $(n-k)+(k-1)=n-1$ real roots counted with multiplicity. Since $\deg P'=n-1$, these are all its roots, and they are all real. $\blacksquare$

**Example.** $P(x)=x^3-3x+2=(x-1)^2(x+2)$: $P'(x)=3(x-1)(x+1)$, with the roots $1$ (from the double root) and $-1\in(-2,1)$ (from Rolle).

**Lesson.** Rolle's theorem counts roots: between two roots of $P$ there is a root of $P'$, and a multiple root of $P$ stays a root of $P'$. Count both and compare with the degree.

## Statement

For a real number $a$, determine the number of distinct real roots of $P_a(x)=x^5-5x+a$.

## Hint

$P_a'(x)=5(x^4-1)$. A polynomial is monotone between consecutive critical points, so it has at most one root in each such interval.

## Solution

**Critical points.** $P_a'(x)=5(x^2-1)(x^2+1)$ vanishes only at $x=\pm1$. Hence $P_a$ is increasing on $(-\infty,-1]$, decreasing on $[-1,1]$ and increasing on $[1,+\infty)$.

**Values.** $P_a(-1)=4+a$ (local maximum) and $P_a(1)=a-4$ (local minimum). Moreover $P_a(x)\to-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$ and $P_a(x)\to+\infty$ as $x\to+\infty$.

**Counting.** On each of the three monotone pieces there is at most one root, and by the intermediate value theorem there is one exactly when the endpoint values (or limits) have opposite signs or one is zero.
- $|a|<4$: $P_a(-1)>0>P_a(1)$. One root in each piece: **three** roots.
- $a=4$: $P_a(-1)=8>0$ and $P_a(1)=0$. The root $x=1$ is a double root and there is one more root on $(-\infty,-1)$: **two** distinct roots.
- $a=-4$: symmetric: $P_a(-1)=0$ is a double root and there is one root on $(1,\infty)$: **two** distinct roots.
- $a>4$: $P_a(1)>0$, so $P_a>0$ on $[-1,\infty)$. One root, on $(-\infty,-1)$: **one** root.
- $a<-4$: symmetric, **one** root on $(1,\infty)$.

**Lesson.** To count the real roots of a polynomial depending on a parameter, study the critical values: roots appear or disappear exactly when a critical value crosses $0$.

## Statement

Find all polynomials $P$ with real coefficients such that $P(x)\,P(x-1)=P(x^2)$ for all real $x$.

## Hint

Check that $x^2+x+1$ works, using $(x^2+x+1)(x^2-x+1)=x^4+x^2+1$. For the converse, show that if $a$ is a root then $a^2$ and $(a+1)^2$ are roots too, and use that the set of roots is finite.

## Solution

**Answer.** $P=0$ and $P(x)=(x^2+x+1)^n$ for $n\ge0$.

**These work.** $(x^2+x+1)^n\bigl((x-1)^2+(x-1)+1\bigr)^n=(x^2+x+1)^n(x^2-x+1)^n=(x^4+x^2+1)^n=P(x^2)$.

**Converse.** Let $P\ne0$ be a solution and let $R$ be its (finite) set of complex roots. If $R=\varnothing$, $P$ is a constant $c$ with $c^2=c$, so $c=1$. Assume $R\ne\varnothing$ and let $a\in R$.

*Two closure properties.* Putting $x=a$ gives $P(a^2)=P(a)P(a-1)=0$, so $a^2\in R$. Putting $x=a+1$ gives $P((a+1)^2)=P(a+1)P(a)=0$, so $(a+1)^2\in R$.

*Roots have modulus $0$ or $1$.* By the first property, $a,a^2,a^4,\dots$ all lie in the finite set $R$, so $a^{2^j}=a^{2^k}$ for some $j<k$. Taking moduli, $|a|^{2^j}=|a|^{2^k}$, so $|a|\in\{0,1\}$. The same applies to the root $(a+1)^2$: either $(a+1)^2=0$ or $|a+1|=1$.

*$0$ and $-1$ are not roots.* If $0\in R$, then $(0+1)^2=1\in R$ and then $(1+1)^2=4\in R$, but $|4|\notin\{0,1\}$: contradiction. If $-1\in R$, then $(-1+1)^2=0\in R$, which we just excluded.

*All roots are $\omega,\bar\omega$.* So every root has $|a|=1$ and $|a+1|=1$. From $|a+1|^2=|a|^2+2\operatorname{Re}a+1=2+2\operatorname{Re}a=1$ we get $\operatorname{Re}a=-\tfrac12$, so $a=-\tfrac12\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}2i$, the roots of $x^2+x+1$.

*Conclusion.* $P(x)=c\,(x-\omega)^p(x-\bar\omega)^q$. Real coefficients force $p=q=n$, so $P=c(x^2+x+1)^n$. Comparing leading coefficients gives $c^2=c$, so $c=1$. $\blacksquare$

**Lesson.** For $P(f(x))$-type equations, study how the equation moves roots around: it produces infinite orbits of roots, which a polynomial cannot have unless the orbit is finite.

## Statement

Let $P$ be a polynomial of degree $d$ with leading coefficient $a$ and let $n\ge0$ be an integer. Prove:
$$\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nkP(k)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }d<n,\\(-1)^n\,n!\,a&\text{if }d=n.\end{cases}$$
Deduce that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^4(-1)^k\binom4kk^m=0$ for $m=0,1,2,3$ and $=24$ for $m=4$.

## Hint

Define the difference operator $(\Delta P)(x)=P(x+1)-P(x)$. What happens to the degree of $P$ when we apply $\Delta$?

## Solution

**Step 1: $\Delta$ lowers the degree by $1$.** If $P(x)=ax^d+\cdots$ with $d\ge1$, then $(x+1)^d-x^d=dx^{d-1}+\cdots$, so $\Delta P$ has degree $d-1$ and leading coefficient $da$. If $P$ is constant, $\Delta P=0$. By induction, if $\deg P=d$ then $\Delta^dP=a\,d!$ (a constant) and $\Delta^{m}P=0$ for $m>d$.

**Step 2: a formula for $\Delta^n$.** Let $E$ be the shift $(EP)(x)=P(x+1)$ and $I$ the identity. Then $\Delta=E-I$, and $E$, $I$ commute, so the binomial theorem applies:
$$\Delta^n=(E-I)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^{n-k}\binom nkE^k,\qquad\text{so}\qquad(\Delta^nP)(0)=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^{n-k}\binom nkP(k).$$

**Step 3.** Multiplying by $(-1)^n$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nkP(k)=(-1)^n(\Delta^nP)(0).$$
By Step 1 this is $0$ if $d<n$ and $(-1)^n\,n!\,a$ if $d=n$. $\blacksquare$

**Check.** For $P(x)=x^m$ with $n=4$: the sums for $m=0,\dots,4$ are $0,0,0,0,24$.

**Lesson.** $\Delta$ is "differentiation for sequences": it lowers the degree by one, and $n$ applications expand into an alternating binomial sum.

## Statement

Let $P$ be a polynomial of degree at most $n$ such that $P(k)=\dfrac1{\binom{n+1}k}$ for $k=0,1,\dots,n$. Find $P(n+1)$.

## Hint

Use the vanishing alternating sum for polynomials of degree less than $N$: with $N=n+1$, $\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}(-1)^k\binom{n+1}kP(k)=0$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The weights $\binom{n+1}k$ in the alternating sum exactly cancel the given values.

**The fact used.** For a polynomial $Q$ of degree $<N$, $\sum_{k=0}^N(-1)^k\binom NkQ(k)=(-1)^N(\Delta^NQ)(0)=0$, because $\Delta$ lowers the degree by one at each application and $\deg P\le n<n+1=N$.

**Apply it with $N=n+1$.**
$$0=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k\binom{n+1}k\cdot\frac1{\binom{n+1}k}+(-1)^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{n+1}P(n+1)=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k+(-1)^{n+1}P(n+1).$$

**Evaluate.** $\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k$ equals $1$ if $n$ is even and $0$ if $n$ is odd. Hence
$$P(n+1)=(-1)^n\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k=\begin{cases}1&n\text{ even},\\0&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}$$

**Check.** $n=1$: $P(0)=1$, $P(1)=\frac12$ gives $P(x)=1-\frac x2$ and $P(2)=0$ ✓. $n=2$: $P(0)=1$, $P(1)=\frac13$, $P(2)=\frac13$ gives $P(3)=1$ ✓.

**Lesson.** When a polynomial of degree $\le n$ is known at $n+1$ points, finite differences give its value at the next point with no interpolation formula.

## Statement

Let $P$ be a non-constant polynomial with integer coefficients. Prove that there are infinitely many integers $n$ for which $|P(n)|$ is not a prime number.

## Hint

Take $n_0$ with $m=P(n_0)$ and $|m|\ge2$. For which $k$ is $P(n_0+km)$ divisible by $m$? Why can $P(n_0+km)$ not equal $\pm m$ for most $k$?

## Solution

**A value of absolute value at least $2$.** A non-constant polynomial is unbounded, so there is an integer $n_0$ with $|P(n_0)|\ge2$. Put $m=P(n_0)$.

**Divisibility.** For integer polynomials, $a-b\mid P(a)-P(b)$. With $a=n_0+km$, $b=n_0$ we get $m\mid P(n_0+km)-m$, so $m\mid P(n_0+km)$ for every integer $k$.

**Large values.** A non-constant polynomial takes each value at most $\deg P$ times, so for all but finitely many $k$, $P(n_0+km)\notin\{m,-m\}$. Taking also $|n_0+km|$ large enough that $|P(n_0+km)|>|m|$, we get a multiple of $m$ whose absolute value is strictly larger than $|m|\ge2$.

**Conclusion.** Such a number $|P(n_0+km)|$ has the divisor $|m|$ with $1<|m|<|P(n_0+km)|$, so it is composite. This happens for infinitely many $k$, giving infinitely many integers $n=n_0+km$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** Famous examples such as $n^2+n+41$ are prime for many consecutive $n$ (here $n=0,\dots,39$), but must fail eventually (at $n=41$, $P(41)=41\cdot43$).

**Lesson.** The congruence $a-b\mid P(a)-P(b)$ lets you make a polynomial value divisible by a number you control.

## Statement

Find all polynomials $P$ with real coefficients such that $P(0)=0$ and $P(x^2+1)=P(x)^2+1$ for all real $x$.

## Hint

Define $a_0=0$ and $a_{k+1}=a_k^2+1$. What can you say about $P(a_k)$?

## Solution

**Answer.** $P(x)=x$.

**It works.** $P(x)=x$ gives $x^2+1=x^2+1$ and $P(0)=0$.

**Uniqueness.** Let $a_0=0$, $a_1=1$, $a_2=2$, $a_3=5,\dots$ with $a_{k+1}=a_k^2+1$. We prove by induction that $P(a_k)=a_k$. For $k=0$ this is $P(0)=0$. If $P(a_k)=a_k$, then the equation with $x=a_k$ gives
$$P(a_{k+1})=P(a_k^2+1)=P(a_k)^2+1=a_k^2+1=a_{k+1}.$$

**Infinitely many roots.** The sequence $(a_k)$ is strictly increasing ($a_{k+1}-a_k=a_k^2-a_k+1>0$), so the $a_k$ are pairwise distinct. The polynomial $P(x)-x$ has infinitely many roots, so it is the zero polynomial: $P(x)=x$. $\blacksquare$

**Lesson.** A functional equation for polynomials plus one starting value often generates infinitely many points where the polynomial agrees with a known one.

## Statement

Find all real polynomials $P$ such that $P(x^2)=P(x)^2$ for all real $x$.

## Hint

Compare the two highest-degree terms of $P$. Where does the second-highest term of $P(x)^2$ land, and can $P(x^2)$ have a term there?

## Solution

**Answer.** $P=0$ and $P(x)=x^n$ for $n\ge0$ (including $P=1$).

**These work.** $P(x^2)=x^{2n}=(x^n)^2$.

**Converse.** Let $P\ne0$ have degree $n$ and leading coefficient $a$. Comparing the coefficients of $x^{2n}$: $a=a^2$, so $a=1$.

Suppose $P$ has another nonzero term, and let $bx^j$ ($b\ne0$, $j<n$) be the highest of the remaining terms. Look at the coefficient of $x^{n+j}$.

*In $P(x)^2$:* it is a sum of $c_ic_{i'}$ over pairs of exponents $(i,i')$ with $i+i'=n+j$ and both coefficients nonzero. If neither exponent is $n$, both are $\le j$, and $i+i'\le2j<n+j$: impossible. So one exponent is $n$ and the other is $j$, and the coefficient is $2\cdot1\cdot b=2b\ne0$.

*In $P(x^2)$:* only even exponents $2e$ appear, where $e$ is an exponent of $P$. For $x^{n+j}$ we would need $e=\frac{n+j}2$, which lies strictly between $j$ and $n$, and $P$ has no nonzero term there. So the coefficient is $0$.

The two sides differ: contradiction. Hence $P$ has a single nonzero term, which is $x^n$. $\blacksquare$

**Lesson.** When comparing coefficients in a polynomial identity, look at the leading term and the *next* term: one of the two sides often cannot produce the exponent the other one does.

## Statement

Let $n\ge2$ and $\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}$.

1. Prove that for every complex $x$ that is not an $n$-th root of unity,
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac1{x-\omega^k}=\frac{nx^{n-1}}{x^n-1}.$$
2. Compute $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac1{2-\omega^k}$.
3. Prove that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac1{1-\omega^k}=\frac{n-1}2$.

## Hint

For part 1, $x^n-1=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(x-\omega^k)$. Differentiate the product and divide by the product. For part 3, pair $k$ with $n-k$.

## Solution

**1.** Let $f(x)=x^n-1=\prod_k(x-\omega^k)$. By the product rule,
$$\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac1{x-\omega^k}.$$
Since $f'(x)=nx^{n-1}$, the identity follows.

**2.** Take $x=2$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac1{2-\omega^k}=\frac{n\,2^{n-1}}{2^n-1}.$$
For $n=3$ this is $\frac{12}7\approx1.714$.

**3.** The terms $k$ and $n-k$ have $\omega^{n-k}=\omega^{-k}=\overline{\omega^k}$. For $z=\omega^k\ne1$ with $|z|=1$,
$$\frac1{1-z}+\frac1{1-\frac1z}=\frac1{1-z}+\frac z{z-1}=\frac{1-z}{1-z}=1.$$
So the terms $k=1,\dots,n-1$ can be grouped into pairs $\{k,n-k\}$ each summing to $1$. If $n$ is even, the term $k=\frac n2$ has $\omega^k=-1$ and equals $\frac12$, which is half of a "pair with itself". In all cases the total is $\frac{n-1}2$. $\blacksquare$

**Lesson.** A logarithmic derivative turns a product over roots into a sum over roots; pairing $z$ with $\bar z=1/z$ evaluates sums over the unit circle.

## Statement

Let $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$, $F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}$. Prove that for every integer $m\ge2$ there is a positive integer $p$ such that $m\mid F_p$.

## Hint

Look at the pairs $(F_n,F_{n+1})\bmod m$. There are only $m^2$ possible pairs. Show that the map producing the next pair can be undone.

## Solution

**Idea.** The sequence of pairs is eventually periodic, and because the rule is reversible it is *purely* periodic: it returns to its starting pair $(0,1)$.

**The pair map.** Let $T(a,b)=(b,a+b)$ on $(\mathbb{Z}/m)^2$. Then $(F_n,F_{n+1})\bmod m=T^n(0,1)$. The map $T$ is injective: if $T(a,b)=T(a',b')$ then $b=b'$ and $a+b=a'+b'$, so $a=a'$.

**Pigeonhole.** The $m^2+1$ pairs $T^0(0,1),\dots,T^{m^2}(0,1)$ cannot all be different, so $T^i(0,1)=T^j(0,1)$ for some $0\le i<j$. Since $T$ is injective, we can cancel one $T$ at a time: $(0,1)=T^{j-i}(0,1)$.

**Conclusion.** With $p=j-i>0$ we have $(F_p,F_{p+1})\equiv(0,1)\pmod m$. In particular $m\mid F_p$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The least such $p$ is the "rank of apparition" of $m$: e.g. $5\mid F_5=5$ and $9\mid F_{12}=144$.

**Lesson.** A recurrence determined by a bijective step, taken modulo $m$, is purely periodic.

## Statement

With $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$, $F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}$, prove that for all positive integers $m,n$,
$$\gcd(F_m,F_n)=F_{\gcd(m,n)}.$$
Deduce that if $F_n$ is prime and $n>4$, then $n$ is prime.

## Hint

First prove $F_{a+b}=F_{a+1}F_b+F_aF_{b-1}$ and $\gcd(F_n,F_{n+1})=1$. Then show $\gcd(F_{a+b},F_b)=\gcd(F_a,F_b)$ and imitate the Euclidean algorithm on the indices.

## Solution

**Step 1: addition formula.** For $b\ge1$: $F_{a+b}=F_{a+1}F_b+F_aF_{b-1}$. Proven by induction on $b$ (both sides satisfy the Fibonacci recurrence in $b$).

**Step 2: consecutive terms are coprime.** $\gcd(F_{n+1},F_n)=\gcd(F_n+F_{n-1},F_n)=\gcd(F_{n-1},F_n)=\dots=\gcd(F_1,F_0)=1$.

**Step 3: the key reduction.** By Step 1, $F_{a+b}=F_{a+1}F_b+F_aF_{b-1}$, so
$$\gcd(F_{a+b},F_b)=\gcd(F_aF_{b-1},F_b)=\gcd(F_a,F_b),$$
where the last equality holds because $F_{b-1}$ and $F_b$ are coprime (Step 2).

**Step 4: Euclid on the indices.** By Step 3, $\gcd(F_m,F_n)=\gcd(F_{m-n},F_n)$ for $m>n$. This is the subtraction form of the Euclidean algorithm, applied to the indices, which ends at $\gcd(F_g,F_0)=F_g$ with $g=\gcd(m,n)$. Hence $\gcd(F_m,F_n)=F_{\gcd(m,n)}$. $\blacksquare$

**Consequence.** Let $n>4$ be composite, $n=ab$ with $2\le a\le b$. Since $n\ne4$, $b\ge3$, and $b<n$. By the formula, $\gcd(F_b,F_n)=F_{\gcd(b,n)}=F_b$, i.e. $F_b\mid F_n$. Here $F_b\ge F_3=2$ and $F_b<F_n$, so $F_n$ has a proper divisor $F_b>1$ and is composite. Hence if $F_n$ is prime and $n>4$, then $n$ is prime.

**Remark.** The converse fails: $F_{19}=4181=37\cdot113$.

**Lesson.** A divisibility property that is "Euclid-like" (gcd of values equals value at the gcd) is proved by showing $\gcd(f(a+b),f(b))=\gcd(f(a),f(b))$.

## Statement

Let $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$, $F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}$. Prove that for $n\ge1$:
1. $F_{n+1}F_{n-1}-F_n^2=(-1)^n$ (Cassini),
2. $F_1^2+F_2^2+\cdots+F_n^2=F_nF_{n+1}$,
3. $F_{n}^2+F_{n+1}^2=F_{2n+1}$.

Compute $F_1^2+\dots+F_{10}^2$.

## Hint

For part 1, consider the matrix $M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and its powers. For part 2, use induction.

## Solution

**1.** By induction, $M^n=\begin{pmatrix}F_{n+1}&F_n\\F_n&F_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}$. Taking determinants, $\det M^n=(\det M)^n=(-1)^n$, i.e. $F_{n+1}F_{n-1}-F_n^2=(-1)^n$.

**2.** For $n=1$: $F_1^2=1=F_1F_2$. If the formula holds for $n$, then
$$F_nF_{n+1}+F_{n+1}^2=F_{n+1}(F_n+F_{n+1})=F_{n+1}F_{n+2}.$$

**3.** $M^{2n+1}=M^nM^{n+1}$, and the $(1,2)$ entry of $M^k$ is $F_k$. The $(1,2)$ entry of the product is $F_{n+1}\cdot F_{n+1}+F_n\cdot F_n$. Hence $F_{2n+1}=F_n^2+F_{n+1}^2$.

$$\frac{P''(x)P(x)-P'(x)^2}{P(x)^2}=-\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{(x-r_i)^2}<0.$$
