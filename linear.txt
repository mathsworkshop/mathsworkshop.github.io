## Statement

Let $F=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3:\ x+y+z=0\}$. Show that $F$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$, and find a basis and the dimension of $F$.

## Hint

Check that $F$ contains $0$ and is stable under sums and scalar multiples. Then solve for $z$.

## Solution

**Subspace.** $(0,0,0)\in F$. If $(x,y,z),(x',y',z')\in F$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$, then
$$(x+\lambda x')+(y+\lambda y')+(z+\lambda z')=(x+y+z)+\lambda(x'+y'+z')=0,$$
so $(x,y,z)+\lambda(x',y',z')\in F$.

**Basis.** A vector of $F$ has $z=-x-y$, so
$$(x,y,z)=x\,(1,0,-1)+y\,(0,1,-1).$$
The vectors $(1,0,-1)$ and $(0,1,-1)$ span $F$ and are linearly independent (neither is a multiple of the other). They form a basis, and $\dim F=2$.

## Statement

Describe the effect of each elementary row operation on the determinant:

1. $L_i \leftrightarrow L_j$ (swap two rows);
2. $L_i \leftarrow \lambda L_i$ with $\lambda\neq 0$ (multiply a row by $\lambda$);
3. $L_i \leftarrow L_i + \lambda L_j$ with $j\neq i$ (add a multiple of another row).

## Hint

Use multilinearity and the alternating property of the determinant.

## Solution

**1. Swapping.** The determinant changes sign: $\det = -\det_{\text{original}}$. This follows from the alternating property (the determinant is zero when two rows are equal, and swapping is an odd permutation).

**2. Scaling.** Multiplying a row by $\lambda$ multiplies the determinant by $\lambda$: $\det = \lambda\cdot\det_{\text{original}}$. This follows from linearity in each row.

**3. Adding a multiple.** Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged. Indeed, writing the new row as $L_i+\lambda L_j$, linearity gives $\det(L_i+\lambda L_j,\ldots)=\det(L_i,\ldots)+\lambda\det(L_j,\ldots)$, and the second determinant has two equal rows, so it is zero.

**Conclusion.** Swapping changes sign; scaling by $\lambda$ scales by $\lambda$; adding a multiple leaves the determinant unchanged. These rules are used to simplify determinant computations.

## Statement

Show that the determinant of an upper triangular (or lower triangular) matrix is the product of its diagonal entries.

## Hint

Expand recursively along the first column (or last row).

## Solution

**Expansion along the first column.** Let $T$ be upper triangular with diagonal entries $t_{11},\ldots,t_{nn}$. In the first column, only $t_{11}$ is nonzero, so
$$\det T = t_{11}\det T_{11},$$
where $T_{11}$ is the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ upper triangular submatrix obtained by removing the first row and first column, with diagonal entries $t_{22},\ldots,t_{nn}$.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix. Show that the determinant is linear in each column: if $C_j = \lambda C_j' + \mu C_j''$, then
$$\det(A) = \lambda\det(A')+\mu\det(A''),$$
where $A'$ and $A''$ are obtained from $A$ by replacing column $j$ with $C_j'$ and $C_j''$ respectively, the other columns being unchanged.

## Hint

Use the Leibniz formula: $\det A = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^n a_{\sigma(i),i}$, which is linear in each column's entries.

## Solution

**Leibniz formula.** For an $n\times n$ matrix with columns $C_1,\ldots,C_n$, each entry $a_{ij}$ is the $i$-th entry of $C_j$. The Leibniz formula is
$$\det A = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^n a_{\sigma(i),i}.$$

**Linearity in column $j$.** In this formula, column $j$ appears only through the factor $a_{\sigma(j),j}$ (the entry in row $\sigma(j)$ and column $j$). Since $a_{\sigma(j),j}=\lambda a'_{\sigma(j),j}+\mu a''_{\sigma(j),j}$, the product is linear in that entry, hence the entire sum is linear in column $j$.

**Conclusion.** The determinant is linear in each column separately (multilinear). Combined with the alternating property (zero when two columns are equal, sign change under transposition), this characterises the determinant.

category: linear/determinants

## Statement

Show that:
1. If two columns of $A$ are equal, then $\det A = 0$;
2. Swapping two columns changes the sign of the determinant.

## Hint

For (1), use the Leibniz formula and pair permutations with their transpositions. For (2), use multilinearity and (1).

## Solution

**1. Equal columns.** Suppose columns $j$ and $k$ (with $j\neq k$) are equal. Consider the transposition $\tau=(j\,k)$ in $S_n$. For each permutation $\sigma$, $\sigma\circ\tau$ has the same sign as $\sigma$ (since $\varepsilon(\tau)=-1$ and $\varepsilon(\sigma\circ\tau)=-\varepsilon(\sigma)$). Using the Leibniz formula, pair up the terms:
$$\det A = \sum_\sigma \varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_i a_{\sigma(i),i} = \sum_\sigma \varepsilon(\sigma\circ\tau)\prod_i a_{\sigma\circ\tau(i),i}.$$
But because columns $j$ and $k$ are equal, $\prod_i a_{\sigma\circ\tau(i),i} = \prod_i a_{\sigma(i),i}$. So the two sums cancel, giving $\det A = -\det A$, hence $\det A=0$.

**2. Swapping columns.** Let $B$ be obtained from $A$ by swapping columns $j$ and $k$. Consider the determinant of $A+B$: column $j$ of $A+B$ is $C_j + C_k$, and column $k$ is $C_k+C_j$, so these columns are equal. By (1), $\det(A+B)=0$. By multilinearity:
$$0 = \det(A+B) = \det A + \det A^{j\leftarrow k} + \det A^{k\leftarrow j} + \det B,$$
where $A^{j\leftarrow k}$ has column $j$ replaced by $C_k$ (and column $k$ unchanged). This matrix has two equal columns $C_k$, so its determinant is zero. Similarly $\det A^{k\leftarrow j}=0$. Hence $\det B = -\det A$.

**Conclusion.** The determinant is alternating: zero on equal columns, and swapping two columns flips the sign.

## Statement

Show that $\det(A^T)=\det(A)$ for every square matrix $A$.

## Hint

Use the Leibniz formula on both sides and reindex the permutations.

## Solution

**Leibniz formula.** 
$$\det A = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^n a_{\sigma(i),i}.$$

**Transpose.** The entry $(i,j)$ of $A^T$ is $a_{ji}$. So
$$\det(A^T) = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)}.$$
Since $\prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)}=\prod_{j=1}^n a_{\sigma^{-1}(j),j}$ (reindexing $j=\sigma(i)$), and $\varepsilon(\sigma^{-1})=\varepsilon(\sigma)$ (since $\varepsilon$ is a group homomorphism and $\sigma\sigma^{-1}=e$), we can rewrite:
$$\det(A^T) = \sum_{\sigma}\varepsilon(\sigma^{-1})\prod_j a_{\sigma^{-1}(j),j} = \sum_{\tau}\varepsilon(\tau)\prod_j a_{\tau(j),j} = \det A,$$
where the last change of variables is $\tau=\sigma^{-1}$.

**Conclusion.** $\det(A^T)=\det A$. In particular, properties of columns hold for rows too.

Similarly reduce $C$ to upper triangular form. So we may assume $A$ and $C$ are upper triangular.

**Triangular case.** In this case $M$ is upper triangular with diagonal entries those of $A$ and $C$, so
$$\det M = \prod_i a_{ii}\cdot\prod_j c_{jj} = \det A\cdot\det C.$$

**Conclusion.** The formula holds in general.

## Statement

Let $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Show that the Gram determinant
$$G(u,v) = \begin{vmatrix} \langle u,u\rangle & \langle u,v\rangle \\ \langle v,u\rangle & \langle v,v\rangle \end{vmatrix}$$
satisfies $G(u,v)\ge0$, with equality iff $u,v$ are linearly dependent. Deduce the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

## Hint

$G(u,v)=\|u\|^2\|v\|^2-\langle u,v\rangle^2$. Show it is nonnegative by a direct computation with $\|u-tv\|^2\ge0$ for all $t$.

## Solution

**Explicit formula.** $\langle u,u\rangle=\|u\|^2$, $\langle v,v\rangle=\|v\|^2$, $\langle u,v\rangle=\langle v,u\rangle$, so
$$G(u,v) = \|u\|^2\|v\|^2 - \langle u,v\rangle^2.$$

**Non-negativity.** For all $t\in\mathbb{R}$, $\|u-tv\|^2\ge0$, so
$$\|u\|^2 - 2t\langle u,v\rangle + t^2\|v\|^2\ge0.$$
If $v=0$, then $G(u,v)=0$ and $u,v$ are dependent. Otherwise, choosing $t=\langle u,v\rangle/\|v\|^2$:
$$\|u\|^2 - \frac{\langle u,v\rangle^2}{\|v\|^2}\ge0,$$
which rearranges to $G(u,v)\ge0$. Equality holds iff $u=tv$ for some $t$, i.e. iff $u,v$ are dependent.

**Cauchy-Schwarz.** From $G(u,v)\ge0$: $\langle u,v\rangle^2\le\|u\|^2\|v\|^2$, so $|\langle u,v\rangle|\le\|u\|\|v\|$.

**Conclusion.** The Gram determinant is nonnegative, vanishes iff the vectors are dependent, and yields the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

## Statement

## Statement

Show that a linear map $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ is self-adjoint (i.e. $f=f^*$) iff its matrix in an orthonormal basis is symmetric.

## Hint

Use $f^*$ has matrix $M^T$.

## Solution

**If $M$ is symmetric.** Then the matrix of $f^*$ is $M^T=M$, so $f^*=f$.

**If $f$ is self-adjoint.** Then the matrix of $f^*$ equals the matrix of $f$. But the matrix of $f^*$ is $M^T$, so $M=M^T$.

**Conclusion.** In an orthonormal basis, $f$ is self-adjoint iff $M$ is symmetric.

**Consequence.** Self-adjoint operators have real eigenvalues, and eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal (spectral theorem for symmetric matrices).

## Statement

Let $Q$ be a real $n\times n$ matrix. Show that $Q$ is orthogonal ($Q^TQ=I$) iff $\langle Qu,Qv\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle$ for all $u,v$.

## Hint

Compute $\langle Qu,Qv\rangle=u^T Q^T Qv$.

## Solution

**If $Q^TQ=I$.** Then $\langle Qu,Qv\rangle=(Qu)^T(Qv)=u^TQ^TQv=u^Tv=\langle u,v\rangle$.

**Conversely.** If $\langle Qu,Qv\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle$ for all $u,v$, then $u^T(Q^TQ-I)v=0$ for all $u,v$. Taking $u=e_i$, $v=e_j$, we get $(Q^TQ)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$, so $Q^TQ=I$.

**Conclusion.** Orthogonal matrices are exactly the linear isometries of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

**Consequences.** $\det Q=\pm1$, and the columns of $Q$ form an orthonormal basis.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $m\times n$ matrix with full column rank ($\operatorname{rank}A=n$). Show that the matrix of orthogonal projection onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is
$$P=A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T.$$

## Hint

Show that $P$ is symmetric, idempotent, and fixes $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, and that its kernel is $\operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp$.

## Solution

**Symmetry.** $P^T=(A^TA)^{-1}$-part: $P^T=A((A^TA)^{-1})^T A^T=A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T=P$ since $A^TA$ is symmetric.

**Idempotence.**
$$P^2=A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T=A(A^TA)^{-1}(A^TA)(A^TA)^{-1}A^T=P.$$

**Fixes $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.** For $x=Ay$: $Px=A(A^TA)^{-1}A^TAy=Ay=x$.

**Kernel.** If $Px=0$, then $A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T x=0$. Multiply by $(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$? Actually, $\langle Px,x\rangle=0\iff\|A^T x\|^2_{?}=0$. Note $x^T P x=x^TA(A^TA)^{-1}A^Tx=\|(A^TA)^{-1/2}A^Tx\|^2\ge0$. If $Px=0$, then $A^Tx=0$, so $x\perp\operatorname{Col}(A)$.

**Conclusion.** $P$ is the orthogonal projection onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.

## Statement

Let $A$ be an $m\times n$ matrix with full column rank, $b\in\mathbb{R}^m$. Show that the vector $x^*$ minimizing $\|Ax-b\|$ satisfies the normal equations
$$A^TAx^*=A^Tb.$$

## Hint

Interpret $Ax$ as a point in $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, and $x^*$ as the orthogonal projection of $b$.

## Solution

**Geometric interpretation.** Minimizing $\|Ax-b\|$ over $x$ is equivalent to finding the point of $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ closest to $b$. This is the orthogonal projection of $b$ onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.

**Orthogonality.** For $x^*$ to be the projection, $b-Ax^*$ must be orthogonal to $\operatorname{Col}(A)$, i.e. to every column of $A$:
$$A^T(b-Ax^*)=0.$$

**Normal equations.** Rearranging:
$$A^TAx^*=A^Tb.$$
Since $A$ has full column rank, $A^TA$ is invertible, so $x^*=(A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb$.

**Conclusion.** The least-squares solution satisfies the normal equations.

## Statement

In $\mathbb{R}_n[X]$ with inner product $\langle P,Q\rangle=\int_{-1}^1 P(t)Q(t)\,dt$, construct the first three Legendre polynomials by orthonormalizing $(1,X,X^2)$.

## Hint

Apply Gram–Schmidt.

## Solution

**Step 1: normalize $1$.** $\langle 1,1\rangle=\int_{-1}^1 dt=2$, so
$$u_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}.$$

**Step 2: orthogonalize $X$ against $u_1$.** $\langle X,1\rangle=\int_{-1}^1 t\,dt=0$, so $X$ is already orthogonal to $1$. Norm:
$$\langle X,X\rangle=\int_{-1}^1 t^2\,dt=\frac23,\qquad u_2=\frac{X}{\sqrt{2/3}}=\sqrt{\frac32}\,X.$$

**Step 3: orthogonalize $X^2$ against $u_1,u_2$.**
$$\langle X^2,1\rangle=\int_{-1}^1 t^2\,dt=\frac23,\qquad \langle X^2,X\rangle=\int_{-1}^1 t^3\,dt=0.$$
So the orthogonal part is
$$X^2-\frac{\langle X^2,1\rangle}{\langle 1,1\rangle}\cdot 1=X^2-\frac{2/3}{2}=X^2-\frac13.$$
Its squared norm:
$$\int_{-1}^1\left(t^2-\frac13\right)^2 dt=\int_{-1}^1 t^4\,dt-\frac23\int_{-1}^1 t^2\,dt+\frac19\int_{-1}^1 dt=\frac25-\frac49+\frac29=\frac{18-20+10}{45}=\frac{8}{45}.$$
Hence
$$u_3=\frac{X^2-1/3}{\sqrt{8/45}}=\sqrt{\frac{45}{8}}\left(X^2-\frac13\right)=\frac{3\sqrt5}{2\sqrt2}\left(X^2-\frac13\right).$$

**The orthonormal triple.**
$$u_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\qquad u_2=\sqrt{\frac32}\,X,\qquad u_3=\frac{3\sqrt5}{2\sqrt2}\left(X^2-\frac13\right).$$
They satisfy $\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$.

**Standard Legendre polynomials.** Rescaling to monic (or to the classical normalization), one gets
$$P_0=1,\qquad P_1=X,\qquad P_2=\frac{3X^2-1}{2}.$$
These are proportional to $u_1,u_2,u_3$ and orthogonal for the integral inner product on $[-1,1]$.


**Conclusion.** The first three Legendre polynomials (unnormalized) are $P_0=1$, $P_1=X$, $P_2=\dfrac{3X^2-1}{2}$; the corresponding orthonormal vectors are $u_1,u_2,u_3$ above.

## Statement

On the space of continuous $2\pi$-periodic functions with inner product $\langle f,g\rangle=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)g(t)\,dt$, show that the functions $1,\cos t,\sin t,\cos 2t,\sin 2t,\ldots$ are orthogonal.

## Hint

Compute $\langle \cos mt,\cos nt\rangle$, $\langle\sin mt,\sin nt\rangle$, $\langle\cos mt,\sin nt\rangle$ using product-to-sum formulas.

## Solution

**Orthogonality of cosines.** For $m\neq n$:
$$\langle\cos mt,\cos nt\rangle=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi\cos mt\cos nt\,dt=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi[\cos(m-n)t+\cos(m+n)t]\,dt=0.$$

**Orthogonality between cosine and sine.** $\langle\cos mt,\sin nt\rangle=0$ because the integrand is odd.

**Orthogonality with constant.** $\langle 1,\cos mt\rangle=0$ and $\langle 1,\sin nt\rangle=0$ (integral of cosine/sine over a period).

**Conclusion.** These functions form an orthogonal family. The Fourier coefficients $a_n=\langle f,\cos nt\rangle$, $b_n=\langle f,\sin nt\rangle$ are the projections of $f$ onto these orthogonal functions.

## Statement

Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space, and $\varphi:E\to\mathbb{R}$ a linear form. Show that there exists a unique $u\in E$ such that
$$\varphi(v)=\langle v,u\rangle$$
for all $v\in E$.

## Hint

Choose an orthonormal basis $(e_i)$ and set $u=\sum_i\varphi(e_i)e_i$.

## Solution

**Existence.** Let $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ be an orthonormal basis of $E$. Define $u=\sum_i\varphi(e_i)e_i$. For any $v=\sum_j v_j e_j$:
$$\langle v,u\rangle=\sum_j v_j\langle e_j,u\rangle=\sum_j v_j\varphi(e_j)=\varphi\left(\sum_j v_j e_j\right)=\varphi(v).$$

**Uniqueness.** If $\langle v,u\rangle=\langle v,u'\rangle$ for all $v$, then $\langle v,u-u'\rangle=0$ for all $v$. Taking $v=u-u'$: $\|u-u'\|^2=0$, so $u=u'$.

**Conclusion.** Every linear form on a finite-dimensional inner product space is represented by a unique vector.

**Matrix form.** In matrix terms, a linear form $\varphi(v)=a^Tv$ is represented by the vector $u=a$ (in an orthonormal basis).

## Statement

Show that a nonempty subset $F$ of a vector space $E$ is a subspace if and only if it is closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

## Hint

The forward direction is immediate from the axioms. For the converse, verify that the axioms of a vector space hold in $F$.

## Solution

**Forward direction.** If $F$ is a subspace, it is a vector space under the induced operations, so it is closed under addition and scalar multiplication by definition.

**Converse.** Suppose $F$ is nonempty and closed under addition and scalar multiplication. We check the axioms:
- *Zero vector.* Since $F$ is nonempty, take $v\in F$. Then $0\cdot v\in F$ by scalar closure, and $0\cdot v=0_E$. So $0_E\in F$.
- *Opposite.* For $v\in F$, $(-1)\cdot v\in F$ by scalar closure, and $(-1)\cdot v=-v$. So $F$ contains opposites.
- *Addition, associativity, commutativity, distributivity, scalar compatibility*: these hold in $F$ because they hold in $E$ and $F$ is closed under the relevant operations.

**Conclusion.** $F$ is a subspace.

## Statement

**Conclusion.** $f|_F$ is a well-defined linear endomorphism of $F$.

**Example.** If $f$ has an eigenvector $v$, the line $\operatorname{span}(v)$ is $f$-invariant. More generally, any eigenspace is $f$-invariant.

## Statement

Let $f:E\to E$ be a linear endomorphism and $F$ an $f$-invariant subspace. Show that $f$ induces a well-defined linear map $\bar f:E/F\to E/F$ by $\bar f([x])=[f(x)]$.

## Hint

Show that if $[x]=[y]$, then $[f(x)]=[f(y)]$.

## Solution

**Well-defined.** Suppose $[x]=[y]$. Then $x-y\in F$. Since $F$ is $f$-invariant, $f(x-y)\in F$, so $f(x)-f(y)\in F$, i.e. $[f(x)]=[f(y)]$. So $\bar f$ is well-defined.

**Linearity.** $\bar f([x]+\lambda[y])=\bar f([x+\lambda y])=[f(x+\lambda y)]=[f(x)+\lambda f(y)]=[f(x)]+\lambda[f(y)]=\bar f([x])+\lambda\bar f([y])$.

**Conclusion.** $\bar f$ is a well-defined linear endomorphism of $E/F$, called the **quotient map** induced by $f$.

**Remark.** This construction is the basis for studying the structure of endomorphisms via invariant subspaces.
