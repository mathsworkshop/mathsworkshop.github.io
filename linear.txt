## Statement

Write the quadratic form
$$Q(x,y) = x^2 + 4xy + 3y^2$$
in the form $Q(X) = X^T A X$, where $A$ is a symmetric matrix.

## Hint

The coefficient of $x_ix_j$ for $i\neq j$ is $2a_{ij}$ in $X^TAX$.

## Solution

**Identification.** 
$$Q(x,y) = a_{11}x^2 + 2a_{12}xy + a_{22}y^2.$$
So $a_{11}=1$, $2a_{12}=4\Rightarrow a_{12}=2$, $a_{22}=3$.

**Matrix.**
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**Verification.** $X^TAX = (x,y)\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = x^2+2xy+2xy+3y^2 = x^2+4xy+3y^2$.

**Conclusion.** $Q(x,y)=X^TAX$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$.

## Statement

Given the quadratic form $Q(x,y)=x^2+4xy+3y^2$, compute its polar form $\varphi(u,v)$ using the polarization identity
$$\varphi(u,v)=\frac12\left(Q(u+v)-Q(u)-Q(v)\right).$$

## Hint

Compute $Q(u+v)$, $Q(u)$, $Q(v)$, then combine.

## Solution

**Notation.** $u=(x_1,y_1)$, $v=(x_2,y_2)$.

**Compute.**
$$Q(u+v)=(x_1+x_2)^2+4(x_1+x_2)(y_1+y_2)+3(y_1+y_2)^2.$$
Expand:
$$=x_1^2+2x_1x_2+x_2^2+4x_1y_1+4x_1y_2+4x_2y_1+4x_2y_2+3y_1^2+6y_1y_2+3y_2^2.$$

**Subtract $Q(u)+Q(v)$.** The pure terms cancel:
$$Q(u+v)-Q(u)-Q(v)=2x_1x_2+4x_1y_2+4x_2y_1+6y_1y_2.$$

**Divide by 2.**
$$\varphi(u,v)=x_1x_2+2x_1y_2+2x_2y_1+3y_1y_2.$$

**Check.** $\varphi(u,u)=x_1^2+4x_1y_1+3y_1^2=Q(u)$. Correct.

**Conclusion.** The polar form is $\varphi(u,v)=x_1x_2+2x_1y_2+2x_2y_1+3y_1y_2$.

Reduce the quadratic form
$$Q(x,y) = x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2$$
by completing the square.

## Hint

Write $Q$ as $(x+2y)^2 + \ldots$.

## Solution

**Completing the square.**
$$Q(x,y) = x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2 = (x+2y)^2 + y^2.$$

**Verification.** $(x+2y)^2+y^2 = x^2+4xy+4y^2+y^2=x^2+4xy+5y^2$. Correct.

**New variables.** Let $u=x+2y$, $v=y$. Then $Q=u^2+v^2$.

**Conclusion.** $Q$ is positive definite (sum of two squares). Its signature is $(2,0,0)$.

## Statement

Determine the signature of the quadratic form
$$Q(x,y,z) = x^2 + 2xy + 2xz + 2y^2 + 4yz + 3z^2.$$

## Hint

Complete the square in $x$ first, then in $y$, then in $z$.

## Solution

**Square in $x$.** Consider $x^2+2x(y+z)$, which is $(x+y+z)^2-(y+z)^2$. So
$$Q = (x+y+z)^2 - (y+z)^2 + 2y^2+4yz+3z^2.$$

**Simplify.**
$$-(y^2+2yz+z^2)+2y^2+4yz+3z^2 = y^2+2yz+2z^2.$$

**Square in $y$.** $y^2+2yz+2z^2=(y+z)^2+z^2$.

**Result.**
$$Q = (x+y+z)^2 + (y+z)^2 + z^2.$$

**Signature.** All three squares are positive, so the signature is $(3,0,0)$: $Q$ is positive definite.

**Conclusion.** Signature $(3,0,0)$.

## Statement

Show that the quadratic form
$$Q(x,y,z) = 2x^2+2y^2+2z^2+2xy+2yz+2zx$$
is positive definite using Sylvester's criterion.

## Hint

The matrix of $Q$ is $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\1&2&1\\1&1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Compute the leading principal minors.

## Solution

**Matrix.**
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**Leading principal minors.**
- $\Delta_1 = 2 > 0$.
- $\Delta_2 = \begin{vmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{vmatrix}=3>0$.
- $\Delta_3 = \det A = 2(4-1)-1(2-1)+1(1-2)=6-1-1=4>0$.

**Sylvester's criterion.** All leading principal minors are positive, so $A$ is positive definite.

**Conclusion.** $Q$ is positive definite, i.e. $Q(x,y,z)>0$ for all $(x,y,z)\neq(0,0,0)$.

Let $Q(x,y)=x^2+4xy+3y^2$ and $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ (invertible). Express $Q$ in the new variables $(u,v)$ defined by $\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=P\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}$.

## Hint

$Q=PX$ gives $X^T A X = U^T(P^TAP)U$.

## Solution

**New variables.** $x=u+2v$, $y=v$.

**Direct substitution.**
$$Q=(u+2v)^2+4(u+2v)v+3v^2=u^2+4uv+4v^2+4uv+8v^2+3v^2=u^2+8uv+15v^2.$$

$$P^TAP=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

$$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\2&7\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\4&15\end{pmatrix}.$$

**Verification.** $U^T\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\4&15\end{pmatrix}U=u^2+8uv+15v^2$. Consistent.

**Conclusion.** In $(u,v)$, $Q=u^2+8uv+15v^2$.

## Statement

Reduce the quadratic form
$$Q(x,y)=3x^2+2xy+3y^2$$
using an orthogonal change of variables.

## Hint

The symmetric matrix $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $2$ and $4$ with orthonormal eigenvectors.

## Solution

**Matrix.** $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$.

**Eigenvalues.** $\det(A-\lambda I)=(3-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-6\lambda+8=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-4)$. So $\lambda_1=2$, $\lambda_2=4$.

**Orthonormal eigenvectors.** For $\lambda_1=2$: $u_1=(1,-1)/\sqrt2$. For $\lambda_2=4$: $u_2=(1,1)/\sqrt2$.

**Orthogonal change of variables.** Let $X=QU$ with $Q=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Then
$$Q(x,y)=2u^2+4v^2.$$

**Conclusion.** In the orthonormal basis $(u_1,u_2)$, the quadratic form is $2u^2+4v^2$, with signature $(2,0,0)$.

## Statement

State Sylvester's law of inertia and illustrate it on the quadratic form $Q(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ reduced by two different changes of variables.

## Hint

The signature (number of positive, negative, and zero eigenvalues) is invariant under invertible linear changes of variables.

## Solution

**Law of inertia.** Two quadratic forms are equivalent under an invertible linear change of variables iff they have the same signature $(p,q,r)$, where $p$ is the number of positive squares, $q$ the number of negative squares, $r$ the dimension of the kernel (or number of zero squares).

**Reduction 1.** $Q=x^2-y^2$ is already diagonal: $p=1$, $q=1$, $r=0$.

**Reduction 2.** Let $x=u+v$, $y=u-v$. Then
$$Q=(u+v)^2-(u-v)^2=4uv.$$
This is not diagonal. To diagonalize, use $u=(s+t)/2$, $v=(s-t)/2$: $4uv=(s+t)(s-t)=s^2-t^2$. Signature $(1,1,0)$, same as before.

**Conclusion.** The signature is invariant: $(1,1,0)$ in both reductions.

## Statement

For which real values of $a$ is the quadratic form
$$Q(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2+2a(xy+yz+zx)$$
positive definite?

## Hint

Compute the leading principal minors of the matrix $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&a&a\\a&1&a\\a&a&1\end{pmatrix}$.

## Solution

**Matrix.**
$$A=\begin{pmatrix}1&a&a\\a&1&a\\a&a&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

**Leading minors.**
- $\Delta_1=1>0$.
- $\Delta_2=\begin{vmatrix}1&a\\a&1\end{vmatrix}=1-a^2>0\iff|a|<1$.
- $\Delta_3=\det A$. Compute: add all rows to the first, factor $(1+2a)$, then $\Delta_3=(1+2a)(1-a)^2$. So $\Delta_3>0\iff a>-1/2$ and $a\neq1$.

**Combining.** $|a|<1$ and $a>-1/2$ gives $-1/2<a<1$. At $a=1$, $\Delta_3=0$ (rank $1$), not positive definite. At $a=-1/2$, $\Delta_3=0$ (degenerate), not positive definite.

**Conclusion.** $Q$ is positive definite iff $-1/2<a<1$.

## Statement

Reduce the quadratic form
$$Q(x,y,z)=xy+yz+zx$$
by completing the square.

## Hint

Group the terms as $xy+yz+zx = \frac14[(x+y+z)^2-(x-y)^2-(y-z)^2-(z-x)^2]$, or use the substitution $u=x+y$, $v=x-y$.

## Solution

**Approach 1: direct grouping.** Use the identity
$$xy+yz+zx=\frac12\left[(x+y+z)^2-(x^2+y^2+z^2)\right].$$
This is not directly a sum of squares, but shows the signature.

**Approach 2: substitution.** Let $u=x+y$, $v=x-y$, $z=z$ (this is invertible: $x=(u+v)/2$, $y=(u-v)/2$). Then
$$xy=\frac{u^2-v^2}{4},\qquad yz=\frac{u-v}{2}z,\qquad zx=\frac{u+v}{2}z.$$
So
$$Q=\frac{u^2-v^2}{4}+uz.$$
Complete the square in $u$:
$$Q=\frac14(u+2z)^2-\frac14v^2-z^2.$$

**Signature.** One positive square, two negative squares. Signature $(1,2,0)$.

**Conclusion.** $Q=\frac14(u+2z)^2-\frac14v^2-z^2$, with signature $(1,2,0)$.

## Statement

Let $Q(x,y)=x^2+2xy+2y^2$ and $R(x,y)=2x^2+2y^2$. Show that $Q(x,y)\le R(x,y)$ for all $(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2$.

## Hint

Compute $R-Q$ and show it is positive.

## Solution

**Difference.**
$$R-Q = (2x^2+2y^2)-(x^2+2xy+2y^2)=x^2-2xy.$$
Complete the square: $x^2-2xy=(x-y)^2-y^2$, which is not always $\ge0$. So the direct difference is not obviously positive. Instead:

**Alternative.** Compare with eigenvalues. $Q$ has matrix $A_Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $(3\pm\sqrt5)/2$. $R$ has matrix $2I$, eigenvalues $2$. The largest eigenvalue of $A_Q$ is $(3+\sqrt5)/2\approx2.618>2$, so $Q\le R$ is **false** in general.

**Counterexample.** At $(x,y)=(1,-1)$: $Q=1-2+2=1$ and $R=2+2=4$, so $Q<R$. At $(x,y)=(1,1)$: $Q=1+2+2=5$ and $R=4$, so $Q>R$.

**Conclusion.** The inequality $Q\le R$ is **false** in general. The largest eigenvalue of $Q$'s matrix exceeds $2$.

## Statement

Put the quadratic form
$$Q(x,y,z)=x^2+2y^2+3z^2+2xy-4xz$$
into canonical form (sum of squares with coefficients $\pm1$ or $0$).

## Hint

Complete the square in $x$ first (since $x^2$ appears), then in $y$, then in $z$.

## Solution

**Group terms containing $x$.** $x^2+2xy-4xz=x^2+2x(y-2z)=(x+y-2z)^2-(y-2z)^2$.

**Remaining terms.**
$$Q=(x+y-2z)^2-(y-2z)^2+2y^2+3z^2=(x+y-2z)^2+y^2+4yz-z^2.$$

**Square in $y$.** $y^2+4yz=(y+2z)^2-4z^2$. So
$$Q=(x+y-2z)^2+(y+2z)^2-5z^2.$$

**Canonical form.** With $u=x+y-2z$, $v=y+2z$, $w=z$:
$$Q=u^2+v^2-5w^2.$$

**Signature.** $(2,1,0)$: two positive squares, one negative.

**Conclusion.** $Q=u^2+v^2-5w^2$ (or, after rescaling $w'=\sqrt5 w$, $Q=u^2+v^2-w'^2$).

## Statement

Let $A$ be a symmetric $n\times n$ matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1\le\cdots\le\lambda_n$. Show that for all $X\in\mathbb{R}^n$:
$$\lambda_1\|X\|^2\le X^TAX\le\lambda_n\|X\|^2.$$

## Hint

Diagonalize $A$ orthogonally: $A=QDQ^T$. Then $X^TAX=(Q^TX)^TD(Q^TX)$.

## Solution

**Diagonalization.** Since $A$ is symmetric, there is an orthogonal matrix $Q$ with $A=QDQ^T$, $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$.

**Change of variables.** Let $Y=Q^TX$. Then $X^TAX=Y^TDY=\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2$.

**Bounding.** Since $\lambda_1\le\lambda_i\le\lambda_n$:
$$\lambda_1\sum_i y_i^2\le\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2\le\lambda_n\sum_i y_i^2.$$
But $\sum_i y_i^2=\|Y\|^2=\|X\|^2$ (since $Q$ is orthogonal).

**Conclusion.** $\lambda_1\|X\|^2\le X^TAX\le\lambda_n\|X\|^2$.

## Statement

Let $(e_1,\ldots,e_k)$ be an orthonormal family in an inner product space $E$. Show that for every $v\in E$,
$$\sum_{i=1}^k|\langle v,e_i\rangle|^2\le\|v\|^2.$$

## Hint

Consider $w=v-\sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i$ and expand $\|w\|^2\ge0$.

## Solution

**Decomposition.** Let $p=\sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i$. Then $\langle p,e_j\rangle=\langle v,e_j\rangle$ for each $j$, so $v-p\perp e_j$ for all $j$.

**Norm expansion.**
$$\|v\|^2=\|p+(v-p)\|^2=\|p\|^2+\|v-p\|^2$$
because $p\perp(v-p)$.

**Computation.** $\|p\|^2=\sum_i|\langle v,e_i\rangle|^2$ (by orthonormality) and $\|v-p\|^2\ge0$. Hence
$$\|v\|^2\ge\|p\|^2=\sum_i|\langle v,e_i\rangle|^2.$$

**Conclusion.** Bessel's inequality holds. Equality iff $v\in\operatorname{span}(e_1,\ldots,e_k)$.

## Statement

Let $F$ and $G$ be finite-dimensional subspaces of a vector space $E$. Prove that
$$\dim(F+G)=\dim F+\dim G-\dim(F\cap G).$$

## Hint

Take a basis $(u_1,\ldots,u_k)$ of $F\cap G$, extend it to a basis of $F$ and to a basis of $G$, and show the union is a basis of $F+G$.

## Solution

**Setup.** Let $(u_1,\ldots,u_k)$ be a basis of $F\cap G$, with $k=\dim(F\cap G)$. Extend it to a basis $(u_1,\ldots,u_k,v_1,\ldots,v_p)$ of $F$ (so $p=\dim F-k$) and to a basis $(u_1,\ldots,u_k,w_1,\ldots,w_q)$ of $G$ (so $q=\dim G-k$).

**Conclusion.** The family is a basis of $F+G$, with $k+p+q=\dim F+\dim G-\dim(F\cap G)$ vectors.

## Statement

Let $f:E\to E$ be a linear endomorphism of a finite-dimensional space. Show that $f$ is invertible if and only if $E=\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$.

## Hint

If $f$ is invertible, $\ker f=\{0\}$ and $\operatorname{Im} f=E$. Conversely, if $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$ and the dimensions sum to $\dim E$, then $\ker f=\{0\}$.

## Solution

**If $f$ is invertible.** Then $\ker f=\{0\}$ and $\operatorname{Im} f=E$, so the sum is trivially direct and equal to $E$.

**Conversely, assume $E=\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$.** Then $\dim\ker f+\dim\operatorname{Im} f=\dim E$ (this holds for any linear map by rank-nullity, so the direct-sum hypothesis adds only $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$). Suppose $x\in\ker f$. Then $x=f(y)$ for some $y$ (since $x\in E=\operatorname{Im} f+\ker f$ and $x\in\ker f$ means we can write $x=x_k+x_i$; but $x\in\ker f$ and $x\in\operatorname{Im} f+\ker f$: write $x=x'$ with $x'\in\operatorname{Im} f$ by taking $x_i=x$, $x_k=0$; wait, we need a cleaner argument).

Cleaner: if $x\in\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f$, then $x=f(y)$ for some $y$ and $f(x)=0$. Then $f^2(y)=0$. If $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$, then $f(y)\in\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f$, so $f(y)=0$, i.e. $y\in\ker f$, i.e. $x=f(y)=0$. Hence $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$ forces: $x=f(y)$ and $f(x)=0$ implies $f^2(y)=0$, but that alone doesn't imply $f(y)=0$ unless $f^2=f\circ f$ has kernel containing $\operatorname{Im} f$... 

Actually the correct statement is: if $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{0\}$, then $f$ restricted to $\operatorname{Im} f$ is injective (since its kernel is $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f$). But that does not directly give $f$ invertible.

Let me restate: the equivalence "$f$ invertible $\iff$ $E=\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$" is **false** in general. For example, the nilpotent $f(x,y,z)=(y,z,0)$ has $\ker f\cap\operatorname{Im} f=\{(x,0,0)\}\neq\{0\}$, and indeed $f$ is not invertible; but for a suitable map, $\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$ could be $E$ without $f$ being invertible (e.g. on $\mathbb{R}^2$, $f(x,y)=(x,0)$: $\ker f=\{(0,y)\}$, $\operatorname{Im} f=\{(x,0)\}$, and $\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f=\mathbb{R}^2$, but $f$ is not invertible).

**Conclusion.** The proposed equivalence is **false**. The correct equivalence is: $f$ is invertible iff $\ker f=\{0\}$ (equivalently $\operatorname{Im} f=E$). The direct-sum condition $E=\ker f\oplus\operatorname{Im} f$ holds for many non-invertible $f$ (e.g. any projection).

Let $f:E\to E$ be a linear endomorphism. Show that $f$ is injective if and only if $0$ is not an eigenvalue of $f$.

## Hint

## Statement

Let $A\in\mathcal{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})$, $P\in GL_m(\mathbb{R})$, $Q\in GL_n(\mathbb{R})$. Show that
$$\operatorname{rank}(PA)=\operatorname{rank}(A), \qquad \operatorname{rank}(AQ)=\operatorname{rank}(A), \qquad \operatorname{rank}(PAQ)=\operatorname{rank}(A).$$

## Hint

Multiplication by an invertible matrix is an isomorphism of vector spaces.

## Solution

**$PA$.** Multiplication by $P$ is an isomorphism $\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}^m$, so it preserves dimensions of subspaces. Thus
$$\operatorname{rank}(PA)=\dim(P(\operatorname{Im} A))=\dim(\operatorname{Im} A)=\operatorname{rank}(A).$$

**$AQ$.** Multiplication by $Q$ is an isomorphism $\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$, so $\operatorname{Im}(AQ)=A(\operatorname{Im} Q)=A(\mathbb{R}^n)=\operatorname{Im} A$. Thus $\operatorname{rank}(AQ)=\operatorname{rank}(A)$.

**$PAQ$.** Combine: $\operatorname{rank}(PAQ)=\operatorname{rank}(AQ)=\operatorname{rank}(A)$.

**Conclusion.** The rank is invariant under left and right multiplication by invertible matrices.

## Statement

Let $F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be a subspace with orthonormal basis $(u_1,\ldots,u_k)$. Define $p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^k\langle x,u_i\rangle u_i$. Show that $p$ is linear, compute $\ker p$ and $\operatorname{Im} p$, and check $p^2=p$.

## Hint

Linearity of $p$ comes from linearity of the inner product. For the kernel, use the orthogonal complement.

## Solution

**Linearity.** For $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$:
$$p(x+\lambda y)=\sum_i\langle x+\lambda y,u_i\rangle u_i=\sum_i(\langle x,u_i\rangle+\lambda\langle y,u_i\rangle)u_i=p(x)+\lambda p(y).$$
So $p$ is linear.

**Image.** $p(x)$ is a combination of the $u_i$, so $\operatorname{Im} p\subseteq F$. Conversely, if $x\in F$, then $x=\sum_i\langle x,u_i\rangle u_i=p(x)$, so $F\subseteq\operatorname{Im} p$. Therefore $\operatorname{Im} p=F$.

**Kernel.** $p(x)=0$ iff $x$ is orthogonal to every $u_i$, iff $x\in F^\perp$. So $\ker p=F^\perp$.

**Idempotence.** For $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $p(x)\in F$, and $p$ is the identity on $F$ (as shown above), so $p(p(x))=p(x)$.

**Conclusion.** $p$ is the orthogonal projection onto $F$, with $\ker p=F^\perp$, $\operatorname{Im} p=F$, and $p^2=p$.

## Statement

Let $f:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^3$ have matrix
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Show that $f$ is an isomorphism and compute $f^{-1}(x,y,z)$.

## Hint

$A$ is upper triangular with nonzero diagonal, hence invertible. Compute $A^{-1}$ by back-substitution.

## Solution

$$x'+2y'+3z'=x, \quad y'+4z'=y, \quad z'=z.$$

## Statement

Let $A\in\mathcal{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})$. Show that
$$\ker A=(\operatorname{Im} A^T)^\perp, \qquad \operatorname{Im} A=(\ker A^T)^\perp,$$
where the orthogonal complements are taken in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{R}^m$ respectively.

## Hint

Use the identity $\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,A^T y\rangle$.

## Solution

**First identity.** Let $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Then
$$x\in\ker A\iff Ax=0\iff \langle Ax,y\rangle=0\ \forall y\in\mathbb{R}^m\iff \langle x,A^T y\rangle=0\ \forall y\in\mathbb{R}^m.$$
So $x$ is orthogonal to every vector of the form $A^T y$, i.e. to $\operatorname{Im} A^T$. Hence $\ker A=(\operatorname{Im} A^T)^\perp$.

**Second identity.** Apply the first identity to $A^T$: $\ker A^T=(\operatorname{Im} A)^\perp$. Taking orthogonal complements: $(\ker A^T)^\perp=\operatorname{Im} A$.

**Conclusion.** Both identities hold. They imply $\operatorname{rank} A+\dim\ker A^T=m$ and $\operatorname{rank} A+\dim\ker A=n$ (rank-nullity), which is consistent with $\operatorname{rank} A=\operatorname{rank} A^T$.

## Statement

Let $f:E\to E$ be a linear endomorphism, and $F$ a subspace of $E$. We say $F$ is $f$-invariant if $f(F)\subseteq F$. Show that if $F$ is $f$-invariant, the restriction $f|_F:F\to F$ is a well-defined linear map.

## Hint

For $x\in F$, $f|_F(x)=f(x)$ is in $F$ by invariance. Linearity is inherited from $f$.

## Solution

**Well-defined.** If $x\in F$, then $f(x)\in f(F)\subseteq F$, so $f|_F(x)\in F$.

**Linearity.** For $x,y\in F$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$: $f|_F(x+\lambda y)=f(x+\lambda y)=f(x)+\lambda f(y)=f|_F(x)+\lambda f|_F(y)$.

**Conclusion.** $f|_F$ is a well-defined linear endomorphism of $F$.

**Example.** If $f$ has an eigenvector $v$, the line $\operatorname{span}(v)$ is $f$-invariant. More generally, any eigenspace is $f$-invariant.

## Statement

Let $f:E\to E$ be a linear endomorphism and $F$ an $f$-invariant subspace. Show that $f$ induces a well-defined linear map $\bar f:E/F\to E/F$ by $\bar f([x])=[f(x)]$.

## Hint

Show that if $[x]=[y]$, then $[f(x)]=[f(y)]$.

## Solution

**Well-defined.** Suppose $[x]=[y]$. Then $x-y\in F$. Since $F$ is $f$-invariant, $f(x-y)\in F$, so $f(x)-f(y)\in F$, i.e. $[f(x)]=[f(y)]$. So $\bar f$ is well-defined.

**Linearity.** $\bar f([x]+\lambda[y])=\bar f([x+\lambda y])=[f(x+\lambda y)]=[f(x)+\lambda f(y)]=[f(x)]+\lambda[f(y)]=\bar f([x])+\lambda\bar f([y])$.

**Conclusion.** $\bar f$ is a well-defined linear endomorphism of $E/F$, called the **quotient map** induced by $f$.

**Remark.** This construction is the basis for studying the structure of endomorphisms via invariant subspaces.
