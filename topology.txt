## Statement

A sequence $(A^{(k)})_{k\ge1}$ of matrices in $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ is said to *converge entrywise* to $A\in M_n(\mathbb{R})$ if for every $i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$, the real sequence $(a^{(k)}_{ij})_{k\ge1}$ converges to $a_{ij}$. Show that entrywise convergence is equivalent to convergence for the metric
$$d(A,B)=\max_{i,j}|a_{ij}-b_{ij}|.$$

## Hint

Compare $d(A^{(k)},A)$ with the largest entrywise difference; use the fact that a maximum of finitely many quantities vanishes iff each one vanishes.

## Solution

**$(\Leftarrow)$.** Suppose $d(A^{(k)},A)\to0$. For each $(i,j)$, $|a^{(k)}_{ij}-a_{ij}|\le d(A^{(k)},A)\to0$, so entrywise convergence holds.

**$(\Rightarrow)$.** Suppose entrywise convergence. For each $(i,j)$, $|a^{(k)}_{ij}-a_{ij}|\to0$. There are finitely many pairs $(i,j)$, so
$$d(A^{(k)},A)=\max_{i,j}|a^{(k)}_{ij}-a_{ij}|\to0.$$

**Conclusion.** Entrywise convergence and convergence for the sup metric coincide on $M_n(\mathbb{R})$.

## Statement

Consider the following three norms on $M_n(\mathbb{R})$:
$$\|A\|_\infty=\max_{i,j}|a_{ij}|,\qquad \|A\|_1=\sum_{i,j}|a_{ij}|,\qquad \|A\|_F=\left(\sum_{i,j}|a_{ij}|^2\right)^{1/2}.$$
Show that they are equivalent.

## Hint

Bound each by the others using the number of entries $n^2$.

## Solution

**Bounds.** For every $A\in M_n(\mathbb{R})$:
$$\|A\|_\infty\le\|A\|_F\le\|A\|_1\le n^2\|A\|_\infty.$$
- $\|A\|_\infty\le\|A\|_F$: each entry squared is $\le\sum|a_{ij}|^2$.
- $\|A\|_F\le\|A\|_1$: from $\|A\|_F^2=\sum|a_{ij}|^2\le(\sum|a_{ij}|)^2$.
- $\|A\|_1\le n^2\|A\|_\infty$: sum of $n^2$ terms each $\le\|A\|_\infty$.

**Conclusion.** The three norms define the same topology on $M_n(\mathbb{R})$.

Show that $\exp:M_n(\mathbb{R})\to M_n(\mathbb{R})$, $\exp(A)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{A^k}{k!}$, is continuous.

Bound $\|\exp(A)-\exp(B)\|$ using the series and the inequality $\|A^k-B^k\|\le\|A-B\|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|A\|^j\|B\|^{k-1-j}$. Or use local boundedness and uniform convergence on bounded sets.

## Statement

Define a metric space and give two examples.

## Hint

A metric $d:X\times X\to[0,\infty)$ satisfies positivity, symmetry, and the triangle inequality.

## Solution

**Definition.** A metric on $X$ is a map $d:X\times X\to[0,+\infty[$ such that for all $x,y,z\in X$:
1. $d(x,y)=0\iff x=y$;
2. $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$;
3. $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$.

**Examples.**
- Euclidean metric on $\mathbb{R}^n$: $d(x,y)=\|x-y\|_2$.
- Discrete metric: $d(x,y)=0$ if $x=y$, $d(x,y)=1$ otherwise.

**Conclusion.** A metric space is a pair $(X,d)$; the metric encodes distance and topology.

## Statement

Define a Cauchy sequence and show that a convergent sequence is Cauchy.

## Hint

**Definition.** $(x_n)$ is Cauchy if $\forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists N,\ \forall m,n\ge N,\ d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon$.

title: Solving a non-linear system via the continuity method
category: topology/topology-of-rn
level: m1
difficulty: 5
tags: continuity method, implicit function theorem, connectedness, nonlinear system
date: 2026-11-06
---

## Statement

Prove that the nonlinear system
$$\begin{cases}
x+\tfrac{1}{2}\sin(y)=3,\\[2mm]
y+\tfrac{1}{2}\cos(x)=4
\end{cases}$$
has at least one solution $(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2$.

## Hint

For $t\in[0,1]$, consider the deformed system
$$\begin{cases}
x+\tfrac{t}{2}\sin(y)=3,\\[2mm]
y+\tfrac{t}{2}\cos(x)=4.
\end{cases}$$
Let $S\subseteq[0,1]$ be the set of parameters $t$ for which the system is solvable. Show that $S$ is nonempty, closed, and open. Conclude by connectedness of $[0,1]$.

## Solution

**Setup.** Define $F:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ by
$$F(t,x,y)=\bigl(x+\tfrac{t}{2}\sin(y)-3,\ y+\tfrac{t}{2}\cos(x)-4\bigr).$$
This map is continuous ($C^\infty$ in all variables). Let
$$S=\{t\in[0,1]:\exists(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2,\ F(t,x,y)=(0,0)\}.$$

**Nonemptiness.** For $t=0$, the system reduces to $x-3=0$ and $y-4=0$, giving the unique solution $(3,4)$. Hence $0\in S$, so $S\neq\emptyset$.

**Closedness.** Let $(t_k)\subseteq S$ with $t_k\to t^*\in[0,1]$, and choose $(x_k,y_k)$ with $F(t_k,x_k,y_k)=0$. From the equations,
$$|x_k|=\Bigl|3-\tfrac{t_k}{2}\sin(y_k)\Bigr|\le3+\tfrac12=\tfrac72,\qquad |y_k|=\Bigl|4-\tfrac{t_k}{2}\cos(x_k)\Bigr|\le4+\tfrac12=\tfrac92.$$
Thus $(x_k,y_k)$ lies in the compact rectangle $[-\tfrac72,\tfrac72]\times[-\tfrac92,\tfrac92]$. By Bolzano–Weierstrass, extract a convergent subsequence $(x_{k_j},y_{k_j})\to(x^*,y^*)$. By continuity of $F$, passing to the limit gives $F(t^*,x^*,y^*)=0$, so $t^*\in S$. Thus $S$ is closed.

**Openness.** Let $t_0\in S$ with a solution $(x_0,y_0)$, so $F(t_0,x_0,y_0)=0$. The Jacobian of $F$ with respect to $(x,y)$ at $(t_0,x_0,y_0)$ is
$$J_{(x,y)}F=\begin{pmatrix}1&\tfrac{t_0}{2}\cos(y_0)\\[2mm]-\tfrac{t_0}{2}\sin(x_0)&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
Its determinant is
$$\det J_{(x,y)}F=1+\tfrac{t_0^2}{4}\sin(x_0)\cos(y_0)\ge 1-\tfrac14=\tfrac34>0.$$
Therefore $J_{(x,y)}F$ is invertible, and the implicit function theorem gives a neighbourhood $]t_0-\delta,t_0+\delta[\,\cap[0,1]$ on which $F(t,x,y)=0$ has a solution. Hence $S$ is open in $[0,1]$.

**Conclusion.** $S$ is a nonempty subset of the connected set $[0,1]$ that is both open and closed in $[0,1]$. Therefore $S=[0,1]$. In particular $1\in S$: the original system $x+\tfrac12\sin(y)=3,\ y+\tfrac12\cos(x)=4$ has a real solution.

## Statement

Recall the definition of compactness by open covers and show that $[0,1]$ has the Borel–Lebesgue property.
