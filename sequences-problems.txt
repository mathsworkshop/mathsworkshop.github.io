## Statement

1. Let $(u_n)$ be arithmetic with $u_0=2$ and common difference $4$. Compute $u_{10}$ and $S_n=\sum_{k=0}^nu_k$.
2. Let $(v_n)$ be geometric with $v_0=3$ and common ratio $\frac12$. Compute $v_5$, $T_n=\sum_{k=0}^nv_k$, and $\lim_{n\to\infty}T_n$.

## Hint

Arithmetic: $u_n=u_0+nr$ and $\sum_{k=0}^nu_k=(n+1)\frac{u_0+u_n}2$. Geometric: $v_n=v_0q^n$ and $\sum_{k=0}^nv_k=v_0\frac{1-q^{n+1}}{1-q}$ for $q\ne1$.

## Solution

**1.** $u_n=2+4n$, so $u_{10}=42$ and
$$S_n=(n+1)\frac{2+(2+4n)}2=(n+1)(2+2n)=2(n+1)^2.$$
(Check: $S_1=2+6=8=2\cdot4$.)

**2.** $v_n=3\left(\frac12\right)^n$, so $v_5=\frac3{32}$ and
$$T_n=3\cdot\frac{1-2^{-(n+1)}}{1-\frac12}=6\left(1-2^{-(n+1)}\right).$$
Since $2^{-(n+1)}\to0$, $T_n\to6$.

**Remark.** Adding infinitely many positive numbers can give a finite sum: this is the first example of a convergent series.

## Statement

Let $u_0=1$ and $u_{n+1}=u_n+n+2$.

1. Compute $u_1,u_2,u_3$. Is $(u_n)$ arithmetic?
2. Prove by induction that $u_n=\dfrac{(n+1)(n+2)}2$ for all $n\ge0$.

## Hint

For part 1, look at the differences $u_{n+1}-u_n$. For part 2, check the base case, then plug the formula into the recurrence.

## Solution

**1.** $u_1=3$, $u_2=6$, $u_3=10$. The differences are $2,3,4,\dots$, not constant, so the sequence is not arithmetic.

**2. Base case.** $u_0=1=\frac{1\cdot2}2$.

**Inductive step.** Assume $u_n=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2$. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{n+1}&=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2+(n+2) \\ &=\frac{(n+2)\bigl[(n+1)+2\bigr]}2 \\ &=\frac{(n+2)(n+3)}2
\end{aligned}
$$
which is the formula at rank $n+1$. 

**Conclusion.** We conclude $u_n=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2$.

## Statement

Let $u_0=0$ and $u_{n+1}=\frac12u_n+1$.

1. Find the fixed point $\ell$ with $\ell=\frac12\ell+1$.
2. Show that $v_n=u_n-\ell$ is geometric.
3. Deduce an explicit formula for $u_n$ and its limit.

## Hint

Subtract the fixed-point equation from the recurrence.

## Solution

**1.** $\ell=\frac12\ell+1\iff\ell=2$.

**2.** Subtracting $\ell=2$ from $u_{n+1}=\frac12u_n+1$ gives
$$v_{n+1}=u_{n+1}-2=\frac12(u_n-2)=\frac12v_n.$$
So $(v_n)$ is geometric with ratio $\frac12$.

**3.** $v_0=-2$, so $v_n=-2\cdot2^{-n}=-2^{1-n}$ and
$$u_n=2-2^{1-n}\longrightarrow2.$$

**Remark.** The trick of subtracting the fixed point works for every recurrence $u_{n+1}=au_n+b$ with $a\ne1$: it turns it into a geometric sequence of ratio $a$.

## Hint

Write out the first few terms and watch what cancels.

## Statement

Find the general term of $(u_n)$ with $u_0=3$, $u_1=5$ and $u_{n+2}=3u_{n+1}-2u_n$.

## Hint

Look for solutions $u_n=r^n$. This leads to the characteristic equation $r^2-3r+2=0$.

## Solution

**Characteristic equation.** $r^2-3r+2=(r-1)(r-2)=0$, so $r_1=1$ and $r_2=2$.

**General solution.** Both $1^n$ and $2^n$ satisfy the recurrence, and so does every combination $u_n=A+B2^n$.

**Initial conditions.** $A+B=3$ and $A+2B=5$, so $B=2$ and $A=1$.

**Answer.** $u_n=1+2^{n+1}$.

## Statement

Find the general term of each sequence.

1. $u_0=1$, $u_1=0$, $u_{n+2}=5u_{n+1}-6u_n$.
2. $u_0=1$, $u_1=0$, $u_{n+2}=4u_{n+1}-4u_n$.
3. $u_0=0$, $u_1=1$, $u_{n+2}=2u_{n+1}-2u_n$.

## Hint

Solve the characteristic equation $r^2=ar+b$. Distinct real roots give $A r_1^n+Br_2^n$; a double root $r$ gives $(A+Bn)r^n$; complex roots $\rho e^{\pm i\theta}$ give $\rho^n(A\cos n\theta+B\sin n\theta)$.

## Solution

**1. Two real roots.** $r^2-5r+6=0$ gives $r=2,3$. So $u_n=A2^n+B3^n$ with $A+B=1$ and $2A+3B=0$, hence $B=-2$, $A=3$:
$$u_n=3\cdot2^n-2\cdot3^n.$$

**2. Double root.** $r^2-4r+4=(r-2)^2$, so $u_n=(A+Bn)2^n$. $u_0=1$ gives $A=1$ and $u_1=2(1+B)=0$ gives $B=-1$:
$$u_n=(1-n)2^n.$$

**3. Complex roots.** $r^2-2r+2=0$ has roots $1\pm i=\sqrt2\,e^{\pm i\pi/4}$. So $u_n=2^{n/2}\left(A\cos\frac{n\pi}4+B\sin\frac{n\pi}4\right)$. $u_0=0$ gives $A=0$, and $u_1=\sqrt2B\sin\frac\pi4=B=1$:
$$u_n=2^{n/2}\sin\frac{n\pi}4.$$

**Checks.** (1) $u_2=-6=12-18$. (2) $u_2=-4=(1-2)\cdot4$. (3) $u_2=2=2\sin\frac\pi2$ and $u_3=4-2=2=2^{3/2}\sin\frac{3\pi}4$.

**Remark.** In case 3 the sequence rotates and grows at the same time: it oscillates with an amplitude $2^{n/2}$.

Let $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$ and $F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_n$.

1. Find a closed formula for $F_n$.
2. Show that $\dfrac{F_{n+1}}{F_n}\to\varphi=\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$.

## Hint

The characteristic equation is $r^2=r+1$. Write $\psi=\frac{1-\sqrt5}2$ for the other root and note that $|\psi|<1$.

## Solution

**1.** The roots of $r^2-r-1=0$ are $\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$ and $\psi=\frac{1-\sqrt5}2$. So $F_n=A\varphi^n+B\psi^n$. From $F_0=0$: $B=-A$. From $F_1=1$: $A(\varphi-\psi)=A\sqrt5=1$. Hence
$$F_n=\frac{\varphi^n-\psi^n}{\sqrt5}.$$

**2.** Since $|\psi|<1<\varphi$, we have $\left(\frac\psi\varphi\right)^n\to0$ and
Dividing numerator and denominator by $\varphi^n$:
$$\frac{F_{n+1}}{F_n} = \frac{\varphi^{n+1}-\psi^{n+1}}{\varphi^n-\psi^n}=\frac{\varphi-\psi\left(\frac\psi\varphi\right)^n}{1-\left(\frac\psi\varphi\right)^n}\longrightarrow\varphi.$$

## Statement

1. Prove by induction that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^nk^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6$.
2. Deduce $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1{n^3}\sum_{k=1}^nk^2$.

## Hint

In the inductive step, add $(n+1)^2$ to the formula and factor.

## Solution

**1. Base case.** $n=1$: $1=\frac{1\cdot2\cdot3}6$.

**Inductive step.** Assume the formula for $n$. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}k^2&=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6+(n+1)^2 \\ &=\frac{(n+1)\bigl[n(2n+1)+6(n+1)\bigr]}6 \\ &=\frac{(n+1)(2n^2+7n+6)}6.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $2n^2+7n+6=(n+2)(2n+3)$, this equals $\frac{(n+1)(n+2)(2n+3)}6$, the formula at rank $n+1$.

**2.**
$$\frac1{n^3}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6=\frac16\cdot\frac{n+1}n\cdot\frac{2n+1}n\longrightarrow\frac16\cdot1\cdot2=\frac13.$$

**Remark.** This is the Riemann sum for $\int_0^1x^2\,dx=\frac13$: the same result obtained from an algebraic formula.

## Statement

Let $S_n=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n\frac k{(k+1)!}$. Compute $S_n$ and its limit.

## Hint

Write $k=(k+1)-1$ and split the fraction.

## Solution

**Splitting.**
$$\frac k{(k+1)!}=\frac{(k+1)-1}{(k+1)!}=\frac1{k!}-\frac1{(k+1)!}.$$

**Telescoping.**
$$S_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\left(\frac1{k!}-\frac1{(k+1)!}\right)=1-\frac1{(n+1)!}\longrightarrow1.$$

## Statement

Let $P_n=\displaystyle\prod_{k=2}^n\left(1-\frac1{k^2}\right)$. Compute $P_n$ and its limit.

## Hint

Factor $1-\frac1{k^2}=\frac{(k-1)(k+1)}{k^2}$ and write out the product.

## Solution

**Factoring.**
$$P_n=\prod_{k=2}^n\frac{k-1}k\cdot\prod_{k=2}^n\frac{k+1}k.$$

**First product.** $\frac12\cdot\frac23\cdots\frac{n-1}n=\frac1n$.

**Second product.** $\frac32\cdot\frac43\cdots\frac{n+1}n=\frac{n+1}2$.

**Result.**
$$P_n=\frac{n+1}{2n}\longrightarrow\frac12.$$

## Statement

1. Using the definition, prove that $\dfrac{3n+1}{n+2}\to3$ and find an explicit $N$ for each $\varepsilon>0$.
2. Prove that $u_n=(-1)^n$ does not converge.

## Hint

For part 1, compute $\left|u_n-3\right|$. For part 2, assume a limit $\ell$ exists and apply the definition with $\varepsilon=1$.

## Solution

**Definition.** $u_n\to\ell$ means: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that $n\ge N\Rightarrow|u_n-\ell|<\varepsilon$.

**1.** We have $$ \left|\dfrac{3n+1}{n+2}-3\right|=\dfrac{5}{n+2}<\dfrac5n, \, \, \forall n \geq 0. $$ 
Given $\varepsilon>0$ take $N=\lceil5/\varepsilon\rceil+1$; for $n\ge N$ we get $\frac5n<\varepsilon$, hence 
$ \left|\dfrac{3n+1}{n+2}-3\right| \leq \varepsilon$. 

**2.** Suppose $(-1)^n\to\ell$. With $\varepsilon=1$ there is $N$ such that $|(-1)^n-\ell|<1$ for $n\ge N$. Then for $n\ge N$,
$$2=\left|(-1)^{n+1}-(-1)^n\right|\le\left|(-1)^{n+1}-\ell\right|+\left|\ell-(-1)^n\right|<1+1=2,$$
which is absurd. So the sequence diverges.

**Remark.** In part 2 the contradiction arises because the terms stay at distance $2$ from each other forever, whereas convergent sequences have terms that eventually cluster.

## Statement

Compute:
1. $A_n=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n\frac n{n^2+k}$,
2. $B_n=\left(1+2^n+3^n\right)^{1/n}$,
3. $C_n=\left(a_1^n+\dots+a_p^n\right)^{1/n}$ for fixed positive numbers $a_1,\dots,a_p$.

## Hint

Bound every term of a sum by the smallest and the largest one.

## Solution

**1.** Each term satisfies $\frac n{n^2+n}\le\frac n{n^2+k}\le\frac n{n^2+1}$. Summing the $n$ terms:
$$\frac{n^2}{n^2+n}\le A_n\le\frac{n^2}{n^2+1}.$$
Both bounds tend to $1$, so $A_n\to1$.

**2.** $3^n\le1+2^n+3^n\le3\cdot3^n$, so taking $n$-th roots, $3\le B_n\le3\cdot3^{1/n}$, and $3^{1/n}\to1$. So $B_n\to3$.

**3.** Let $M=\max a_i$. Then $M^n\le a_1^n+\dots+a_p^n\le p\,M^n$, so $M\le C_n\le p^{1/n}M$ and $p^{1/n}\to1$. Hence $C_n\to M$.

**Remark.** The result in 3 says that a sum of powers behaves, to the $n$-th root, like its largest term: the same idea underlies the $\ell^p$ norms tending to the maximum norm as $p\to\infty$.

## Statement

1. Prove: if $u_n>0$ and $\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}\to L<1$, then $u_n\to0$.
2. Deduce that for $k\in\mathbb{N}$ and $a>1$: $\dfrac{n^k}{a^n}\to0$, $\dfrac{a^n}{n!}\to0$ and $\dfrac{n!}{n^n}\to0$.

## Hint

For part 1, choose $q$ with $L<q<1$: eventually $u_{n+1}\le qu_n$. For part 2, compute the ratio of consecutive terms.

## Solution

**1.** Pick $q\in(L,1)$. Since $\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}\to L$, for $\varepsilon = q - L$, there is $N$ such that 
$$ \dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}\leq L + \varepsilon = q, \, \, \forall n\ge N. $$
Hence 
$$ u_{n+1} \le qu_n, \, \, \forall n\ge N. $$ 
By induction, we get  $u_n\le u_N q^{n-N}\to0$ as $n \to +\infty$.

**2.**
- For $u_n=\frac{n^k}{a^n}$: $\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\left(1+\frac1n\right)^k\frac1a\to\frac1a<1$.
- For $u_n=\frac{a^n}{n!}$: $\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\frac a{n+1}\to0<1$.
- For $u_n=\frac{n!}{n^n}$: $\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\frac{(n+1)n^n}{(n+1)^{n+1}}=\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{-n}\to\frac1e<1$.

**Conclusion.** For large $n$,
$$\ln n\ll n^k\ll a^n\ll n!\ll n^n.$$

**Warning.** If $L=1$ the test says nothing: $u_n=\frac1n$ tends to $0$, while $u_n=n$ diverges, though both have ratio $\to1$.

## Statement

For each sequence ($n\ge1$), find $\sup$, $\inf$, $\limsup$, $\liminf$, and say whether the sup and inf are attained.

1. $u_n=(-1)^n\dfrac n{n+1}$
2. $v_n=(-1)^n+\dfrac1n$

## Hint

Treat even and odd indices separately: they form two monotone subsequences.

## Solution

**1.** For even $n$: $u_n=\frac n{n+1}$ increases to $1$ without reaching it. For odd $n$: $u_n=-\frac n{n+1}$ decreases to $-1$ without reaching it. Hence
$$\sup u_n=1,\ \inf u_n=-1\ \text{(not attained)},\qquad\limsup u_n=1,\ \liminf u_n=-1.$$

**2.** For even $n$: $v_n=1+\frac1n$ decreases to $1$, with maximum $v_2=\frac32$. For odd $n$: $v_n=-1+\frac1n$ is $0$ at $n=1$ and then decreases to $-1$ without reaching it. Hence
$$\sup v_n=\frac32\ \text{(attained)},\quad\inf v_n=-1\ \text{(not attained)},\qquad\limsup v_n=1,\ \liminf v_n=-1.$$

**Remark.** The supremum describes the *whole* sequence, whereas $\limsup$ only describes its behaviour at the tail. In example 2 they differ ($\frac32$ versus $1$) because the largest value occurs early. A bounded sequence converges iff $\limsup=\liminf$.

## Statement

Prove that the sequence $(\sin n)_{n\ge1}$ has no limit.

## Hint

If $\sin n\to\ell$, use $\sin(n+1)-\sin(n-1)=2\sin1\cos n$ to find the limit of $\cos n$, then use $\sin2n=2\sin n\cos n$.

## Solution

**Suppose** $\sin n\to\ell$. Then $\sin(n+1)\to\ell$ and $\sin(n-1)\to\ell$, so
$$2\sin1\cos n=\sin(n+1)-\sin(n-1)\longrightarrow0.$$
Since $\sin1\ne0$, we get $\cos n\to0$.

**Two consequences.**
- From $\sin^2n+\cos^2n=1$ we get $\ell^2=1$.
- The sequence $\sin2n=2\sin n\cos n$ tends to $2\ell\cdot0=0$. But $(\sin2n)$ is a subsequence of $(\sin n)$, so it tends to $\ell$. Hence $\ell=0$.

**Contradiction.** $\ell^2=1$ and $\ell=0$ cannot both hold. So $(\sin n)$ does not converge.

**Remark.** In fact, the set $\{n\bmod2\pi\}$ is dense in $[0,2\pi]$ and every value in $[-1,1]$ is a limit point of $\sin n$.

## Statement

Let $S_n=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}k$. Show that $(S_{2n})$ and $(S_{2n+1})$ are adjacent sequences, and deduce that $(S_n)$ converges.

## Hint

Compute $S_{2n+2}-S_{2n}$, $S_{2n+3}-S_{2n+1}$ and $S_{2n+1}-S_{2n}$.

## Solution

**Monotonicity.**
$$S_{2n+2}-S_{2n}=\frac1{2n+1}-\frac1{2n+2}>0,\qquad S_{2n+3}-S_{2n+1}=-\frac1{2n+2}+\frac1{2n+3}<0.$$
So $(S_{2n})$ is increasing and $(S_{2n+1})$ is decreasing.

**Adjacent sequences.** They converge to the same limit $\ell$. Since both the even and the odd subsequences tend to $\ell$, the whole sequence $(S_n)$ converges to $\ell$.

**Remark.** The limit is $\ln2\approx0.6931$ ($S_{2000}\approx0.6929$). The bounds $S_{2n}\le\ln2\le S_{2n+1}$ give an error estimate of $\frac1{2n+1}$ for free.

## Statement

Let $a_n=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^n\frac1{k!}$ and $b_n=a_n+\dfrac1{n\cdot n!}$ for $n\ge1$.

1. Show that $(a_n)$ is strictly increasing and $(b_n)$ strictly decreasing.
2. Show that $b_n-a_n\to0$, and deduce that both sequences have the same limit $e$.
3. Deduce that $e$ is irrational.

## Hint

For part 3, suppose $e=\frac pq$ and multiply the inequality $a_q<e<b_q$ by $q!$.

## Solution

**1.** $a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac1{(n+1)!}>0$. For $b$:
$$b_{n+1}-b_n=\frac1{(n+1)!}+\frac1{(n+1)(n+1)!}-\frac1{n\,n!}=\frac1{(n+1)!}\left(\frac{n+2}{n+1}-\frac{n+1}n\right)=-\frac1{n(n+1)(n+1)!}<0.$$

**2.** $b_n-a_n=\frac1{n\,n!}\to0$. The sequence $(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded above by $b_1$, so it converges, say to $\ell$; $(b_n)$ is decreasing and bounded below by $a_1$, so it converges to the same $\ell$, since the difference tends to $0$. This common limit is $e=\sum\frac1{k!}$.

**3.** Strict monotonicity gives $a_q<e<b_q$ for every $q\ge1$. Suppose $e=\frac pq$ with integers $p,q\ge1$. Multiplying $a_q<\frac pq<a_q+\frac1{q\cdot q!}$ by $q!$:
$$q!\,a_q<p\,(q-1)!<q!\,a_q+\frac1q.$$
Here $q!\,a_q=\sum_{k=0}^q\frac{q!}{k!}$ and $p\,(q-1)!$ are integers, and the integer $p(q-1)!$ lies strictly between the integer $q!\,a_q$ and that integer plus $\frac1q\le1$. Impossible.

**Conclusion.** $e$ is irrational.

**Remark.** A famous argument, due to Fourier. It uses nothing but adjacent sequences.

## Statement

Let $a_n=\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n$ and $b_n=\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n+1}$.

1. Show that $(a_n)$ is increasing and $(b_n)$ is decreasing.
2. Deduce that both converge to the same limit (which is $e$).

## Hint

Apply the AM-GM inequality $\sqrt[m]{x_1\cdots x_m}\le\frac{x_1+\dots+x_m}m$ to well-chosen $n+1$ (resp. $n+2$) numbers.

## Solution

**1. $(a_n)$ increases.** Apply AM-GM to the $n+1$ numbers $\underbrace{1+\frac1n,\dots,1+\frac1n}_{n},\,1$. Their mean is $\frac{n+1+1}{n+1}=1+\frac1{n+1}$, so
$$\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n/(n+1)}\le1+\frac1{n+1}\ \Longrightarrow\ a_n\le a_{n+1}.$$

**$(b_n)$ decreases.** Apply AM-GM to the $n+2$ numbers $\underbrace{\frac n{n+1},\dots,\frac n{n+1}}_{n+1},\,1$. Their mean is $\frac{n+1}{n+2}$, so
$$\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^{(n+1)/(n+2)}\le\frac{n+1}{n+2}\ \Longrightarrow\ \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n+1}\le\left(\frac{n+1}{n+2}\right)^{n+2}.$$
Taking inverses, $\left(\frac{n+1}n\right)^{n+1}\ge\left(\frac{n+2}{n+1}\right)^{n+2}$, i.e. $b_n\ge b_{n+1}$.

**2.** We have $a_n\le b_n$ for all $n$, and 
$$ b_n-a_n=\frac1n a_n\le\frac{b_1}n\to0 $$ 
since $a_n\le b_n\le b_1$. Therefore $a_n$ is increasing and bounded above by $b_1$, so it converges. Also $b_n$ is decreasing and bounded below by $a_1$, so it converges. they have the same limit, which is called $e$.

## Statement

A sequence is **Cauchy** if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with $|u_m-u_n|<\varepsilon$ for all $m,n\ge N$. In $\mathbb{R}$, a sequence converges if and only if it is Cauchy.

1. Show that $u_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{k^2}$ is Cauchy, hence converges.
2. Show that $H_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1k$ is not Cauchy, hence diverges.

## Hint

For part 1, compare $\frac1{k^2}$ with $\frac1{k(k-1)}=\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k$. For part 2, look at $H_{2n}-H_n$.

## Solution

**1.** For $m>n\ge1$,
$$|u_m-u_n|=\sum_{k=n+1}^m\frac1{k^2}\le\sum_{k=n+1}^m\left(\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k\right)=\frac1n-\frac1m<\frac1n.$$
Given $\varepsilon$, take $N>\frac1\varepsilon$: for $m>n\ge N$, $|u_m-u_n|<\varepsilon$. The sequence is Cauchy, hence converges.

**2.** $H_{2n}-H_n=\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}\frac1k\ge n\cdot\frac1{2n}=\frac12$. So no matter how large $N$ is, taking $n\ge N$ and $m=2n$ gives $|H_m-H_n|\ge\frac12$: the sequence is not Cauchy, hence diverges.

**Remark.** The Cauchy criterion is powerful because it proves convergence *without knowing the limit*. (Here the limit of $u_n$ is $\frac{\pi^2}6$, which is much harder to find.) Note that in part 2, consecutive terms $H_{n+1}-H_n=\frac1{n+1}\to0$, yet the sequence diverges: the criterion needs *all* pairs $m,n$.

## Statement

Let $a_0=1$, $b_0=2$ and for $n\ge0$,
$$a_{n+1}=\frac{a_n+b_n}2,\qquad b_{n+1}=\sqrt{a_nb_n}.$$
Show that both sequences converge to the same limit.

## Hint

By AM-GM, $b_n\le a_n$ from $n=1$ on. Then compare $a_{n+1}$ with $a_n$, $b_{n+1}$ with $b_n$, and bound $a_{n+1}-b_{n+1}$ by $\frac12(a_n-b_n)$.

## Solution

**Ordering.** For $x,y>0$, $\sqrt{xy}\le\frac{x+y}2$. So $b_{n+1}\le a_{n+1}$ for all $n\ge0$, i.e. $b_n\le a_n$ for all $n\ge1$.

**Monotonicity.** For $n\ge1$:
- $a_{n+1}=\frac{a_n+b_n}2\le a_n$, so $(a_n)_{n\ge1}$ is decreasing;
- $b_{n+1}=\sqrt{a_nb_n}\ge\sqrt{b_n^2}=b_n$, so $(b_n)_{n\ge1}$ is increasing.

**Contracting gap.** For $x\ge y>0$,
$$\frac{x+y}2-\sqrt{xy}=\frac{(\sqrt x-\sqrt y)^2}2\le\frac{(\sqrt x-\sqrt y)(\sqrt x+\sqrt y)}2=\frac{x-y}2.$$
So $0\le a_{n+1}-b_{n+1}\le\frac12(a_n-b_n)$, hence $a_n-b_n\le2^{-n}(a_1-b_1)\to0$.

**Conclusion.** The sequences are adjacent, so they converge to a common limit $M(1,2)$, the **arithmetic–geometric mean** of $1$ and $2$.

**Numerics.**
- $n=1$: $a_1=1.5$, $b_1=1.41421$
- $n=2$: $a_2=1.45711$, $b_2=1.45648$
- $n=3$: $a_3=1.4567910$, $b_3=1.4567910$

So $M(1,2)\approx1.4567910$. Convergence is extremely fast (the number of correct digits doubles).

## Statement

Prove that for every integer $n\ge3$ there exist positive integers $a_1<\dots<a_n$ in arithmetic progression and positive integers $b_1<\dots<b_n$ in geometric progression such that
$$b_1<a_1<b_2<a_2<\cdots<b_n<a_n.$$
Give an explicit example with $n=5$.

## Hint

Take a geometric progression with ratio $\frac{c+1}c$ for a large even integer $c$, namely $b_k=c^{n-k}(c+1)^{k-1}$, and an arithmetic progression with common difference $\frac12c^{n-2}$.

## Solution

**Construction.** Let $c$ be an even integer with $c>2(n-1)^2$ (for example $c=2n^2$) and set, for $k=1,\dots,n$,
$$b_k=c^{n-k}(c+1)^{k-1},\qquad a_k=c^{n-1}+\left(k-\tfrac12\right)c^{n-2}.$$
All $b_k$ are integers, in geometric progression with ratio $\frac{c+1}c$. The $a_k$ are in arithmetic progression with difference $c^{n-2}$, and they are integers because $c$ is even and $n\ge3$, so $\frac12c^{n-2}$ is an integer.

**Normalising.** Dividing by $c^{n-1}$: the claim $b_k<a_k<b_{k+1}$ becomes, with $x=\frac1c$,
$$(1+x)^{k-1}<1+\left(k-\tfrac12\right)x<(1+x)^k.$$

**Right inequality.** By Bernoulli, $(1+x)^k\ge1+kx>1+\left(k-\frac12\right)x$.

**Left inequality.** Let $m=k-1\le n-1$, so $mx\le\frac{n-1}c\le1$. Using $1+t\le e^t$ and $e^t\le1+t+t^2$ for $t\le1$:
$$(1+x)^m\le e^{mx}\le1+mx+m^2x^2.$$
We need $m^2x^2<\frac x2$, that is $c>2m^2$. Since $c>2(n-1)^2\ge2m^2$, the left inequality holds.

**Example.** The values of $c$ below are smaller than the one used in the proof, but they work, as one checks directly. For $n=3$ and $c=4$: $b=(16,20,25)$ and $a=(18,22,26)$, giving
$$16<18<20<22<25<26.$$
For $n=5$ and $c=14$: $b=(38416,\ 41160,\ 44100,\ 47250,\ 50625)$ and $a=(39788,\ 42532,\ 45276,\ 48020,\ 50764)$, and one checks
$$38416<39788<41160<42532<44100<45276<47250<48020<50625<50764.$$

**Remark.** Both sequences have a similar size, but the geometric one eventually overtakes any arithmetic one. The construction makes them dance around each other for exactly $n$ steps by choosing a ratio very close to $1$.
