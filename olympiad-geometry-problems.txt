## Statement

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with orthocentre $H$, and let $H'$ be the reflection of $H$ in the line $BC$. Prove that $H'$ lies on the circumcircle of $ABC$.

## Hint

First compute the angle $\angle BHC$ in terms of $\angle A$. Then use that a reflection preserves angles and that the converse of the inscribed angle theorem characterises concyclic points.

## Solution

**Idea.** Show $\angle BH'C=180^\circ-\angle A$, with $A$ and $H'$ on opposite sides of $BC$: this says $ABH'C$ is a cyclic quadrilateral.

**The angle $\angle BHC$.** Let $E$ be the foot of the altitude from $B$ (on $AC$) and $F$ the foot of the altitude from $C$ (on $AB$). Since the triangle is acute, $H$ is inside the triangle, on segments $BE$ and $CF$. In the quadrilateral $AFHE$, the angles at $E$ and $F$ are $90^\circ$ and the angle at $A$ is $\angle A$, so
$$\angle FHE=360^\circ-90^\circ-90^\circ-\angle A=180^\circ-\angle A.$$
The angles $\angle BHC$ and $\angle FHE$ are vertical angles, hence $\angle BHC=180^\circ-\angle A$.

**Reflecting.** Reflection in $BC$ preserves angles, so $\angle BH'C=\angle BHC=180^\circ-\angle A$. The point $H$ is on the same side of $BC$ as $A$, so $H'$ is on the opposite side. Therefore $\angle BAC+\angle BH'C=180^\circ$ with $A,H'$ on opposite sides of $BC$: the quadrilateral $ABH'C$ is cyclic. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** Consequently the circle through $B$, $H$, $C$ is the reflection of the circumcircle in $BC$, so it has the same radius $R$. Also, if $D$ is the foot of the altitude from $A$, then $D$ is the midpoint of $HH'$, so the line $AD$ meets the circumcircle again exactly at $H'$.

**Lesson.** When an orthocentre appears, compute $\angle BHC=180^\circ-\angle A$; reflections of $H$ in the sides land on the circumcircle.

## Statement

Let $ABC$ be an equilateral triangle and let $P$ be a point on the arc $BC$ of its circumcircle that does not contain $A$. Prove that
$$PA=PB+PC.$$
Deduce that $PB+PC\le 2R$, where $R$ is the circumradius, and find when equality holds.

## Hint

You may use **Ptolemy's theorem**: in a cyclic quadrilateral $WXYZ$ (vertices in this order on the circle), $WY\cdot XZ=WX\cdot YZ+XY\cdot ZW$. Apply it to the quadrilateral $ABPC$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The quadrilateral $ABPC$ has three sides equal to the side $s$ of the triangle, and Ptolemy turns this into a linear relation.

**Applying Ptolemy.** The points $A,B,P,C$ lie on the circle in this order. Ptolemy gives
$$AP\cdot BC=AB\cdot PC+AC\cdot BP.$$
Since $AB=BC=CA=s$, we get $s\cdot PA=s\cdot PC+s\cdot PB$, so $PA=PB+PC$. $\blacksquare$

**The inequality.** $PA$ is a chord of the circle, so $PA\le 2R$. Hence $PB+PC=PA\le2R$. Equality holds iff $PA$ is a diameter, i.e. $P$ is the point diametrically opposite to $A$ on the arc $BC$; then $PB=PC=R$.

**Remark.** The identity $PA=PB+PC$ also shows that the three segments $PA,PB,PC$ always form a (degenerate) triangle.

**Lesson.** Look for Ptolemy whenever a cyclic quadrilateral has many equal sides; it converts products of lengths into linear relations.

## Statement

Let $P$ be a point on the circumcircle of a triangle $ABC$. Prove that the feet of the perpendiculars from $P$ to the three lines $BC$, $CA$, $AB$ are collinear.

## Hint

Place the circumcircle as the unit circle in the complex plane, with $A,B,C,P$ represented by the numbers $a,b,c,p$ of modulus $1$ (so $\bar a=1/a$ etc.). First find the foot of the perpendicular from $p$ to the chord through $b$ and $c$, then show that a certain ratio of differences is real.

## Solution

**Idea.** A complex-number computation avoids every configuration issue.

**Step 1: the foot of the perpendicular.** Let $z$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $p$ to the line through $b,c$ (with $|b|=|c|=1$, $b\ne c$).
- $z$ lies on line $bc$: $z+bc\,\bar z=b+c$.
- $z-p$ is perpendicular to $b-c$: $\frac{z-p}{b-c}+\overline{\left(\frac{z-p}{b-c}\right)}=0$, i.e. $z-p=bc(\bar z-\bar p)$.

Substituting $bc\bar z=b+c-z$: $z-p=b+c-z-bc\bar p$, so
$$z=\frac{p+b+c-bc\,\bar p}{2}=\frac{p+b+c-\frac{bc}{p}}2.$$

**Step 2: differences of feet.** Let $f_a,f_b,f_c$ be the feet on $BC,CA,AB$:
$$f_a=\frac{p+b+c-bc/p}2,\qquad f_b=\frac{p+c+a-ca/p}2,\qquad f_c=\frac{p+a+b-ab/p}2.$$
Then
$$f_a-f_b=\frac{(b-a)(p-c)}{2p},\qquad f_a-f_c=\frac{(c-a)(p-b)}{2p}.$$
So
$$\frac{f_a-f_b}{f_a-f_c}=\frac{(b-a)(p-c)}{(c-a)(p-b)}=:Q.$$

**Step 3: $Q$ is real.** Use $\overline{x-y}=\frac{y-x}{xy}$ for unit numbers:
$$\bar Q=\frac{(a-b)(c-p)}{(a-c)(b-p)}\cdot\frac{ac\cdot bp}{ab\cdot cp}=\frac{(a-b)(c-p)}{(a-c)(b-p)}=Q.$$
Thus $Q\in\mathbb R$, which means that $f_a,f_b,f_c$ are collinear. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The converse also holds. The Simson line of $P$ bisects the segment joining $P$ to the orthocentre $H$.

**Lesson.** On the unit circle, feet of perpendiculars have the neat formula $\frac{p+b+c-bc\bar p}2$; collinearity becomes "a quotient is real".

## Statement

Two circles intersect at two points $A$ and $B$. A line touches the first circle at $T_1$ and the second circle at $T_2$ (a common tangent). The line $AB$ meets the line $T_1T_2$ at a point $M$. Prove that $M$ is the midpoint of $T_1T_2$.

## Hint

Recall the **power of a point**: if a line through $M$ meets a circle at $X$ and $Y$, then $MX\cdot MY$ is the same for all such lines, and equals $MT^2$ if $MT$ is tangent to the circle at $T$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The point $M$ lies on the line $AB$, which is a secant for both circles, and on the tangent line $T_1T_2$.

**Power of $M$ with respect to the first circle.** The line $MT_1$ is tangent at $T_1$, and $MAB$ is a secant through $A$ and $B$. Hence
$$MT_1^2=MA\cdot MB.$$

**Power of $M$ with respect to the second circle.** In the same way, $MT_2^2=MA\cdot MB$.

**Conclusion.** $MT_1^2=MT_2^2$, so $MT_1=MT_2$. As $M$ is on the line $T_1T_2$, it is the midpoint of $T_1T_2$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The line $AB$ is the **radical axis** of the two circles: the set of points having equal power with respect to both. It is perpendicular to the line of centres.

**Lesson.** Whenever tangents and secants meet at a point, write the power of that point in two ways.

## Statement

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle, let $D,E,F$ be the feet of the altitudes from $A,B,C$ and let $H$ be its orthocentre. Prove that
$$HA\cdot HD=HB\cdot HE=HC\cdot HF.$$

## Hint

Consider the three circles whose diameters are the sides of the triangle. Which points do they pass through? What is the radical axis of two of them?

## Solution

**Idea.** The altitudes turn out to be the radical axes of three circles, hence their common point $H$ has equal powers.

**The circles.** Let $\omega_{AB},\omega_{BC},\omega_{CA}$ be the circles with diameters $AB$, $BC$, $CA$. Since $\angle ADB=\angle ADC=90^\circ$, $D$ lies on $\omega_{AB}$ and on $\omega_{CA}$; similarly $E$ lies on $\omega_{AB}$ and $\omega_{BC}$, and $F$ lies on $\omega_{BC}$ and $\omega_{CA}$.

**Radical axes.** The circles $\omega_{AB}$ and $\omega_{CA}$ both pass through $A$ and $D$, so their radical axis is the line $AD$. Likewise the radical axis of $\omega_{AB},\omega_{BC}$ is $BE$, and that of $\omega_{BC},\omega_{CA}$ is $CF$. These three lines meet at $H$, which therefore has the same power with respect to the three circles.

**Computing the power.** The line $AD$ meets $\omega_{AB}$ at $A$ and $D$, so the power of $H$ with respect to $\omega_{AB}$ is $HA\cdot HD$. The line $BE$ meets $\omega_{AB}$ at $B$ and $E$, so it is also $HB\cdot HE$. Likewise $HB\cdot HE=HC\cdot HF$. Therefore
$$HA\cdot HD=HB\cdot HE=HC\cdot HF.\ \blacksquare$$

**Remark.** The three altitudes concurring is itself a consequence of the radical centre theorem.

**Lesson.** Circles on diameters give right angles for free; radical axes then produce concurrency and equal products.

## Statement

The incircle of triangle $ABC$ touches $BC,CA,AB$ at $D,E,F$ respectively.

1. Prove that $AD$, $BE$, $CF$ are concurrent.
2. For the triangle with $BC=14$, $CA=15$, $AB=13$, find the ratio $BD:DC$.

## Hint

You may use **Ceva's theorem (converse)**: if $D,E,F$ are on the sides $BC,CA,AB$ and $\frac{AF}{FB}\cdot\frac{BD}{DC}\cdot\frac{CE}{EA}=1$, then $AD,BE,CF$ are concurrent. Two tangent segments from the same point to a circle are equal.

## Solution

**1. Tangent lengths.** Let $x=AE=AF$, $y=BF=BD$, $z=CD=CE$. Then
$$\frac{AF}{FB}\cdot\frac{BD}{DC}\cdot\frac{CE}{EA}=\frac xy\cdot\frac yz\cdot\frac zx=1,$$
and the points $D,E,F$ are interior to the sides. By Ceva's converse, $AD,BE,CF$ concur (at the Gergonne point). $\blacksquare$

**2. The numbers.** Adding, $x+y=AB=13$, $y+z=BC=14$, $z+x=CA=15$. The semi-perimeter is $s=21$ and $x=s-a=7$, $y=s-b=6$, $z=s-c=8$, where $a=BC,b=CA,c=AB$. Then
$$BD:DC=y:z=6:8=3:4.$$

**Remark.** In general $AE=AF=s-a$, $BD=BF=s-b$, $CD=CE=s-c$.

**Lesson.** Name the three tangent lengths $x,y,z$; most incircle problems reduce to linear equations in them.

## Statement

Let $ABC$ be a triangle with three different side lengths. The external bisector of the angle at $A$ meets the line $BC$ at $D$; the external bisector at $B$ meets $CA$ at $E$; the external bisector at $C$ meets $AB$ at $F$. Prove that $D$, $E$, $F$ are collinear.

## Hint

Use the external angle bisector theorem: $\dfrac{DB}{DC}=\dfrac{AB}{AC}$, with $D$ outside the segment $BC$. Then apply the **converse of Menelaus's theorem**.

## Solution

**Idea.** Compute the three ratios with the bisector theorem and check that the product is $+1$.

**Existence.** The external bisector at $A$ is parallel to $BC$ iff $AB=AC$; since the sides are pairwise different, the three points exist.

**The ratios.** By the external bisector theorem,
$$\frac{DB}{DC}=\frac{c}{b},\qquad\frac{EC}{EA}=\frac{a}{c},\qquad\frac{FA}{FB}=\frac{b}{a},$$
where $a=BC,b=CA,c=AB$. The product is $1$.

**Signs.** Each of $D,E,F$ is *outside* the corresponding segment, so each signed ratio is positive. Thus the signed product is $+1$.

**Menelaus.** The standard form says $D,E,F$ are collinear iff $\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}\cdot\frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}\cdot\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}=-1$. Since $\overline{DB}=-\overline{BD}$, etc., our product equals $-$ of this standard product; "our product $=+1$" is equivalent, so $D,E,F$ are collinear. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** For *internal* bisectors the feet are inside the sides and the lines concur (Ceva); for *external* ones the feet are collinear (Menelaus).

**Lesson.** Ceva = concurrency of cevians, Menelaus = collinearity of points on the side lines; both reduce to a product of ratios equal to $\pm1$.

## Statement

Let $O$, $G$, $H$ be the circumcentre, centroid and orthocentre of a triangle $ABC$. Prove that $O,G,H$ are collinear with $\overrightarrow{OH}=3\overrightarrow{OG}$. Deduce that $AH=2\cdot OM$, where $M$ is the midpoint of $BC$.

## Hint

Take $O$ as the origin and denote by $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$ the position vectors of $A,B,C$, which all have the same length $R$. Show that the point with vector $\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c$ lies on all three altitudes.

## Solution

**Idea.** With the circumcentre as origin, the orthocentre has the very simple vector $\mathbf h=\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c$.

**The orthocentre.** Let $\mathbf h=\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c$. Then
$$(\mathbf h-\mathbf a)\cdot(\mathbf b-\mathbf c)=(\mathbf b+\mathbf c)\cdot(\mathbf b-\mathbf c)=|\mathbf b|^2-|\mathbf c|^2=R^2-R^2=0.$$
So $H'$ (the point with vector $\mathbf h$) lies on the altitude from $A$. By symmetry it lies on the altitudes from $B$ and $C$. Hence $H'=H$.

**The centroid.** $\mathbf g=\frac{\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c}3=\frac{\mathbf h}3$, i.e. $\overrightarrow{OH}=3\overrightarrow{OG}$. So $O,G,H$ are collinear and $G$ divides $OH$ in the ratio $1:2$.

**The second statement.** $\overrightarrow{AH}=\mathbf h-\mathbf a=\mathbf b+\mathbf c=2\overrightarrow{OM}$, hence $AH=2\,OM$ and $AH\parallel OM$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The line through $O,G,H$ is the **Euler line**. The relation $AH=2R|\cos A|$ also follows.

**Lesson.** Choose the origin at the circumcentre: the orthocentre is then the sum of the vertices.

## Statement

In a triangle $ABC$ with orthocentre $H$ and circumradius $R$, prove that the following nine points lie on one circle of radius $R/2$:
- the midpoints of the three sides,
- the feet of the three altitudes,
- the midpoints of the segments $AH$, $BH$, $CH$.

## Hint

Place the circumcircle as the unit circle ($R=1$) with vertices $a,b,c$. The orthocentre is $h=a+b+c$. Show that every one of the nine points is at distance $\tfrac12$ from $n=\tfrac{a+b+c}2$. The foot of the perpendicular from $a$ to $BC$ is $\frac{a+b+c-bc\bar a}2$.

## Solution

**Idea.** All nine points are at distance $\frac12$ from $n=\frac{a+b+c}2$, the midpoint of $OH$.

**Midpoints of the sides.** For $BC$: $\frac{b+c}2-n=-\frac a2$, distance $\frac12$. Same for the others.

**Midpoints of $AH$, $BH$, $CH$.** Midpoint of $AH$ is $\frac{a+h}2=\frac{2a+b+c}2$; minus $n$ gives $\frac a2$, distance $\frac12$. Same for $B$ and $C$.

**Feet of the altitudes.** The foot from $A$ is $d=\frac{a+b+c-bc\bar a}2$, so
$$d-n=-\frac{bc\bar a}2,\qquad|d-n|=\frac{|b||c||\bar a|}2=\frac12.$$
Same for the feet from $B$ and $C$.

**Conclusion.** The nine points lie on the circle of centre $n$ and radius $\frac12$; for a general $R$, radius $\frac R2$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The nine-point circle is the image of the circumcircle under the homothety of centre $G$ and ratio $-\frac12$ (and also of centre $H$ and ratio $\frac12$).

**Lesson.** Complex numbers on the unit circle turn "nine points on a circle" into nine one-line modulus computations.

## Statement

Let $A_1A_2\dots A_7$ be a regular heptagon with $a=A_1A_2$, $b=A_1A_3$, $c=A_1A_4$. Prove that
$$\frac1a=\frac1b+\frac1c.$$

## Hint

Two chords of a regular heptagon are equal when they span the same number of sides (or $7$ minus that number). Apply Ptolemy to the quadrilateral $A_1A_2A_3A_5$.

## Solution

**Chord lengths.** A chord joining two vertices $k$ steps apart has length depending only on $\min(k,7-k)$:
- $1$ step: length $a$;
- $2$ steps: length $b$;
- $3$ steps: length $c$; and $A_5A_1$ spans $4$ steps $=3$ the other way, so $A_5A_1=c$.

**Ptolemy.** The quadrilateral $A_1A_2A_3A_5$ is cyclic with vertices in this order. Its sides are $a,a,b,c$; its diagonals are $b,c$. Ptolemy:
$$A_1A_3\cdot A_2A_5=A_1A_2\cdot A_3A_5+A_2A_3\cdot A_5A_1,$$
so $bc=ab+ac$. Divide by $abc$:
$$\frac1a=\frac1c+\frac1b.\ \blacksquare$$

**Remark.** Numerically with circumradius $1$: $a\approx0.868$, $b\approx1.564$, $c\approx1.950$, and $\frac1b+\frac1c\approx1.152=\frac1a$.

**Lesson.** In a regular polygon, many chords are equal; Ptolemy on a well-chosen quadrilateral gives algebraic relations between the distinct chord lengths.

## Statement

On each side of an arbitrary triangle $ABC$, an equilateral triangle is erected outwards. Prove that the centres of these three equilateral triangles are the vertices of an equilateral triangle.

## Hint

Orient $ABC$ counter-clockwise and use complex numbers. The third vertex of the outward equilateral triangle on $BC$ is obtained by rotating $C$ about $B$ by $-60^\circ$. To show $z_1,z_2,z_3$ form an equilateral triangle, it is enough to show $z_1+\omega z_2+\omega^2z_3=0$, where $\omega=e^{2i\pi/3}$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The centre of each small triangle is a complex-linear combination of two vertices of $ABC$; a root-of-unity identity finishes.

**Third vertices.** Write $\varepsilon=e^{i\pi/3}$. For counter-clockwise $ABC$, the outer vertex across $BC$ is
$$A'=B+(C-B)\varepsilon^{-1}=\varepsilon B+\varepsilon^{-1}C.$$
The centre of $BCA'$ is
$$G_a=\frac{B+C+A'}3=\frac{uB+\bar uC}{\sqrt3},\qquad u=e^{i\pi/6}.$$
By cyclic symmetry,
$$G_b=\frac{uC+\bar uA}{\sqrt3},\qquad G_c=\frac{uA+\bar uB}{\sqrt3}.$$

**The key identity.** With $\omega=e^{2i\pi/3}$, each of the three brackets in
$$\sqrt3\,(G_a+\omega G_b+\omega^2G_c)=A(\omega\bar u+\omega^2u)+B(u+\omega^2\bar u)+C(\bar u+\omega u)$$
vanishes (e.g. $\omega\bar u=i$, $\omega^2u=-i$). So $G_a+\omega G_b+\omega^2G_c=0$.

**Conclusion.** Using $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$, $(G_a-G_c)+\omega(G_b-G_c)=0$, i.e. $G_a-G_c=e^{-i\pi/3}(G_b-G_c)$. So $G_aG_bG_c$ is equilateral. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** Holds with inward triangles too. The centre of the Napoleon triangle is the centroid of $ABC$.

**Lesson.** Rotations by $\pm60^\circ$ and $\pm90^\circ$ are multiplications by fixed unit complex numbers; equilateral triangles are detected by $z_1+\omega z_2+\omega^2z_3=0$.

## Statement

Two circles $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$ intersect at two points $A$ and $B$. A line through $A$ meets $\Gamma_1$ again at $P$ and $\Gamma_2$ again at $Q$. A line through $B$ meets $\Gamma_1$ again at $P'$ and $\Gamma_2$ again at $Q'$. Prove that $PP'\parallel QQ'$.

## Hint

Use **directed angles modulo $180^\circ$**. Four points $W,X,Y,Z$ are concyclic iff $\angle(YW,YX)=\angle(ZW,ZX)$.

## Solution

**Chase.** $A,B,P,P'$ are concyclic (on $\Gamma_1$), so
$$\angle(P'P,P'B)=\angle(AP,AB).$$
$P,A,Q$ are collinear, so $\angle(AP,AB)=\angle(AQ,AB)$.
$A,B,Q,Q'$ are concyclic (on $\Gamma_2$), so
$$\angle(AQ,AB)=\angle(Q'Q,Q'B).$$
Altogether, $\angle(P'P,P'B)=\angle(Q'Q,Q'B)$. Since $P'B$ and $Q'B$ are the same line $\ell$, $\angle(PP',\ell)=\angle(QQ',\ell)$, so $PP'\parallel QQ'$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** If the second line is tangent, replace the inscribed angle by the tangent-chord angle.

**Lesson.** Directed angles mod $180^\circ$ remove every "inside or outside?" worry.

## Statement

Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral. Find the point $P$ of the plane for which $PA+PB+PC+PD$ is minimal, and the minimal value.

## Hint

Group the terms in pairs: $PA+PC$ and $PB+PD$. What does the triangle inequality say about each pair?

## Solution

**Lower bound.** For every $P$, the triangle inequality gives
$$PA+PC\ge AC,\qquad PB+PD\ge BD.$$
Therefore $PA+PB+PC+PD\ge AC+BD$.

**Equality.** The first inequality is an equality iff $P$ lies on segment $AC$; the second iff $P$ lies on segment $BD$. Since the quadrilateral is convex, its diagonals intersect at a single point $X$ inside it. This point lies on both segments, so for $P=X$ both inequalities are equalities.

**Answer.** The minimum is $AC+BD$, attained exactly at the intersection $X$ of the diagonals. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** For a non-convex quadrilateral, one of the vertices (the reflex one) gives the minimum instead.

**Lesson.** To minimise a sum of distances, pair the terms so that the triangle inequality gives a constant lower bound, then check when it is attained.

## Statement

In triangle $ABC$, the points $D,E,F$ lie on the sides $BC,CA,AB$ with
$$\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{CE}{EA}=\frac{AF}{FB}=2.$$
The cevians $AD$, $BE$, $CF$ form a small triangle. Prove that its area is $\frac17$ of the area of $ABC$.

## Hint

Ratios of areas are preserved by affine maps, and every triangle is the image of $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$ under an affine map. Reduce to coordinates.

## Solution

**Reduction.** An affine map multiplies all areas by the same constant and preserves ratios along lines. Assume $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(0,1)$, area $\frac12$.

**The points.**
- $D=B+\frac23(C-B)=\left(\frac13,\frac23\right)$.
- $E=C+\frac23(A-C)=\left(0,\frac13\right)$.
- $F=\left(\frac23,0\right)$.

**The cevians.**
- $AD$: $y=2x$.
- $BE$: $y=\frac{1-x}3$.
- $CF$: $y=1-\frac{3x}2$.

**Intersections.**
- $AD\cap BE$: $2x=\frac{1-x}3\Rightarrow x=\frac17$: $P_1=\left(\frac17,\frac27\right)$.
- $BE\cap CF$: $\frac{1-x}3=1-\frac{3x}2\Rightarrow x=\frac47$: $P_2=\left(\frac47,\frac17\right)$.
- $CF\cap AD$: $2x=1-\frac{3x}2\Rightarrow x=\frac27$: $P_3=\left(\frac27,\frac47\right)$.

**Area.** With $\overrightarrow{P_1P_2}=\left(\frac37,-\frac17\right)$ and $\overrightarrow{P_1P_3}=\left(\frac17,\frac27\right)$:
$$[P_1P_2P_3]=\frac12\left|\frac37\cdot\frac27-\left(-\frac17\right)\cdot\frac17\right|=\frac12\cdot\frac7{49}=\frac1{14}.$$
Since $[ABC]=\frac12$, the ratio is $\frac{1/14}{1/2}=\frac17$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** This is a special case of **Routh's theorem**: for ratios $r$ in place of $2$, the area ratio is $\dfrac{(r-1)^2}{r^2+r+1}$; for $r=2$ it gives $\frac17$, for $r=1$ (medians) it gives $0$.

**Lesson.** Area-ratio problems often reduce, by affine invariance, to a computation in the coordinate triangle $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$.
