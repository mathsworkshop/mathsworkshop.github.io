## Statement

Let $A(1,2,3)$ and $B(4,6,8)$. Compute the distance $AB$ and the midpoint $M$ of $AB$.

## Hint

$AB=\sqrt{(x_B-x_A)^2+(y_B-y_A)^2+(z_B-z_A)^2}$.

## Solution

**Distance.**
$$AB=\sqrt{(4-1)^2+(6-2)^2+(8-3)^2}=\sqrt{9+16+25}=\sqrt{50}=5\sqrt2.$$

**Midpoint.**
$$M=\left(\frac{1+4}{2},\frac{2+6}{2},\frac{3+8}{2}\right)=\left(\frac52,4,\frac{11}{2}\right).$$

**Conclusion.** $AB=5\sqrt2$, $M=(5/2,4,11/2)$.

## Statement

Let $\vec u=(1,-2,3)$ and $\vec v=(4,0,-1)$. Compute $\vec u+\vec v$, $3\vec u-2\vec v$, $\|\vec u\|$, and $\vec u\cdot\vec v$.

## Hint

Operations are componentwise; $\|\vec u\|=\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2}$.

## Solution

**Sum.** $\vec u+\vec v=(5,-2,2)$.

**Combination.** $3\vec u-2\vec v=(3,-6,9)-(8,0,-2)=(-5,-6,11)$.

**Norm.** $\|\vec u\|=\sqrt{1+4+9}=\sqrt{14}$.

**Dot product.** $\vec u\cdot\vec v=4+0-3=1$.

**Conclusion.** $\vec u+\vec v=(5,-2,2)$, $3\vec u-2\vec v=(-5,-6,11)$, $\|\vec u\|=\sqrt{14}$, $\vec u\cdot\vec v=1$.

## Statement

Compute $\vec u\times\vec v$ for $\vec u=(1,2,3)$ and $\vec v=(4,5,6)$. Interpret geometrically.

## Hint

$\vec u\times\vec v=(u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\, u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\, u_1v_2-u_2v_1)$.

## Solution

**Compute.**
$$\vec u\times\vec v=(2\cdot6-3\cdot5,\ 3\cdot4-1\cdot6,\ 1\cdot5-2\cdot4)=(-3,6,-3).$$

**Orthogonality.** $(-3,6,-3)\cdot(1,2,3)=-3+12-9=0$ and $(-3,6,-3)\cdot(4,5,6)=-12+30-18=0$. ✓

**Area.** $\|\vec u\times\vec v\|=\sqrt{9+36+9}=\sqrt{54}=3\sqrt6$: area of the parallelogram spanned by $\vec u,\vec v$.

**Conclusion.** $\vec u\times\vec v=(-3,6,-3)$, area $3\sqrt6$.

## Statement

## Statement

Let $ABC$ be an acute triangle with orthocentre $H$, and let $H'$ be the reflection of $H$ in the line $BC$. Prove that $H'$ lies on the circumcircle of $ABC$.

## Hint

First compute the angle $\angle BHC$ in terms of $\angle A$. Then use that a reflection preserves angles and that the converse of the inscribed angle theorem characterises concyclic points.

## Solution

**Idea.** Show $\angle BH'C=180^\circ-\angle A$, with $A$ and $H'$ on opposite sides of $BC$: this says $ABH'C$ is a cyclic quadrilateral.

**The angle $\angle BHC$.** Let $E$ be the foot of the altitude from $B$ (on $AC$) and $F$ the foot of the altitude from $C$ (on $AB$). Since the triangle is acute, $H$ is inside the triangle, on segments $BE$ and $CF$. In the quadrilateral $AFHE$, the angles at $E$ and $F$ are $90^\circ$ and the angle at $A$ is $\angle A$, so
$$\angle FHE=360^\circ-90^\circ-90^\circ-\angle A=180^\circ-\angle A.$$
The angles $\angle BHC$ and $\angle FHE$ are vertical angles (the lines $BE$ and $CF$ cross at $H$), hence $\angle BHC=180^\circ-\angle A$.

**Reflecting.** Reflection in $BC$ preserves angles, so $\angle BH'C=\angle BHC=180^\circ-\angle A$. The point $H$ is on the same side of $BC$ as $A$ (it is inside the triangle), so $H'$ is on the opposite side. Therefore $\angle BAC+\angle BH'C=180^\circ$ with $A,H'$ on opposite sides of $BC$: the quadrilateral $ABH'C$ is cyclic, i.e. $H'$ lies on the circumcircle of $ABC$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** Consequently the circle through $B$, $H$, $C$ is the reflection of the circumcircle in $BC$, so it has the same radius $R$. Also, if $D$ is the foot of the altitude from $A$, then $D$ is the midpoint of $HH'$, so the line $AD$ meets the circumcircle again exactly at $H'$.

**Lesson.** When an orthocentre appears, compute $\angle BHC=180^\circ-\angle A$; reflections of $H$ in the sides land on the circumcircle.

## Statement

Let $ABC$ be an equilateral triangle and let $P$ be a point on the arc $BC$ of its circumcircle that does not contain $A$. Prove that
$$PA=PB+PC.$$
Deduce that $PB+PC\le 2R$, where $R$ is the circumradius, and find when equality holds.

## Hint

You may use **Ptolemy's theorem**: in a cyclic quadrilateral $WXYZ$ (vertices in this order on the circle), $WY\cdot XZ=WX\cdot YZ+XY\cdot ZW$. Apply it to the quadrilateral $ABPC$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The quadrilateral $ABPC$ has three sides equal to the side $s$ of the triangle, and Ptolemy turns this into a linear relation.

**Applying Ptolemy.** The points $A,B,P,C$ lie on the circle in this order (because $P$ is on the arc $BC$ not containing $A$). Ptolemy gives
$$AP\cdot BC=AB\cdot PC+AC\cdot BP.$$
Since $AB=BC=CA=s$, we get $s\cdot PA=s\cdot PC+s\cdot PB$, so $PA=PB+PC$. $\blacksquare$

**The inequality.** $PA$ is a chord of the circle, so $PA\le 2R$. Hence $PB+PC=PA\le2R$. Equality holds iff $PA$ is a diameter, i.e. $P$ is the point of the circle diametrically opposite to $A$. That point is the midpoint of the arc $BC$ (it lies on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ through $A$ and the centre), and then $PB=PC=R$.

**Remark.** The extreme point $P$ is the point on the arc for which $PB=PC=R$; the identity $PA=PB+PC$ also shows that the three segments $PA,PB,PC$ always form a (degenerate) triangle.

**Lesson.** Look for Ptolemy whenever a cyclic quadrilateral has many equal sides; it converts products of lengths into linear relations.

**Idea.** A complex-number computation avoids every configuration issue (inside/outside, obtuse angles).

**Step 1: the foot of the perpendicular.** Let $z$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $p$ to the line through $b,c$ (with $|b|=|c|=1$, $b\ne c$).
- $z$ lies on line $bc$: $z+bc\,\bar z=b+c$. (Indeed for $z=b$: $b+bc\cdot\frac1b=b+c$.)
- $z-p$ is perpendicular to $b-c$: the number $\frac{z-p}{b-c}$ is purely imaginary, i.e. $\frac{z-p}{b-c}+\overline{\left(\frac{z-p}{b-c}\right)}=0$. Since $\overline{b-c}=\frac1b-\frac1c=\frac{c-b}{bc}$, this reads $\frac{z-p}{b-c}-\frac{bc(\bar z-\bar p)}{b-c}=0$, i.e. $z-p=bc(\bar z-\bar p)$.

Substituting $bc\bar z=b+c-z$ into the second relation: $z-p=b+c-z-bc\bar p$, so
$$z=\frac{p+b+c-bc\,\bar p}{2}=\frac{p+b+c-\frac{bc}{p}}2.$$

**Step 2: differences of feet.** Let $f_a,f_b,f_c$ be the feet on $BC,CA,AB$:
$$f_a=\frac{p+b+c-bc/p}2,\qquad f_b=\frac{p+c+a-ca/p}2,\qquad f_c=\frac{p+a+b-ab/p}2.$$
Then
$$f_a-f_b=\frac{(b-a)-\frac cp(b-a)}2=\frac{(b-a)(p-c)}{2p},\qquad f_a-f_c=\frac{(c-a)(p-b)}{2p}.$$
So
$$\frac{f_a-f_b}{f_a-f_c}=\frac{(b-a)(p-c)}{(c-a)(p-b)}=:Q.$$

**Step 3: $Q$ is real.** Use $\overline{x-y}=\frac{y-x}{xy}$ for unit numbers:
$$\bar Q=\frac{\frac{a-b}{ab}\cdot\frac{c-p}{cp}}{\frac{a-c}{ac}\cdot\frac{b-p}{bp}}=\frac{(a-b)(c-p)}{(a-c)(b-p)}\cdot\frac{ac\cdot bp}{ab\cdot cp}=\frac{(a-b)(c-p)}{(a-c)(b-p)}=Q.$$
Thus $Q\in\mathbb R$, which means that $f_a,f_b,f_c$ are collinear. (If $P$ coincides with a vertex, say $P=B$, then $f_a=f_c=B$ and the three feet are trivially collinear; otherwise $p\ne b$ and $p\ne c$, so $f_a\ne f_b$ and $f_a\ne f_c$, and $Q$ is defined and nonzero.) $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The converse also holds: if the feet are collinear, $P$ lies on the circumcircle. The Simson line of $P$ bisects the segment joining $P$ to the orthocentre $H$.

**Lesson.** On the unit circle, feet of perpendiculars have the neat formula $\frac{p+b+c-bc\bar p}2$; collinearity becomes "a quotient is real".

## Statement

## Hint

Use the external angle bisector theorem: $\dfrac{DB}{DC}=\dfrac{AB}{AC}$, with $D$ outside the segment $BC$. Then apply the **converse of Menelaus's theorem**: points $D,E,F$ on the lines $BC,CA,AB$ are collinear iff the signed ratios satisfy $\dfrac{DB}{DC}\cdot\dfrac{EC}{EA}\cdot\dfrac{FA}{FB}=1$ (equivalently, with the other orientation, $-1$).

## Solution

**Idea.** Compute the three ratios with the bisector theorem and check that the product is $\pm1$ as Menelaus requires.

**Existence.** The external bisector at $A$ is parallel to $BC$ iff $AB=AC$; since the sides are pairwise different, the three points exist.

**The ratios (unsigned).** By the external bisector theorem,
$$\frac{DB}{DC}=\frac{c}{b},\qquad\frac{EC}{EA}=\frac{a}{c},\qquad\frac{FA}{FB}=\frac{b}{a},$$
where $a=BC,b=CA,c=AB$. The product of the lengths is $\frac cb\cdot\frac ac\cdot\frac ba=1$.

**Signs.** Each of $D,E,F$ is *outside* the corresponding segment (external division), so each of the three signed ratios $\frac{\overline{DB}}{\overline{DC}}$, etc. is positive: for a point $D$ outside segment $BC$, the vectors $\overrightarrow{DB}$ and $\overrightarrow{DC}$ point the same way. Thus the signed product is $+1$.

**Menelaus.** The standard form of Menelaus's theorem says that $D,E,F$ (on the lines $BC,CA,AB$, none a vertex) are collinear iff $\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}\cdot\frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}\cdot\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}=-1$ (signed lengths). Since $\overline{DB}=-\overline{BD}$, $\overline{EC}=-\overline{CE}$, $\overline{FA}=-\overline{AF}$, the product above equals $-$ of this standard product, so the condition reads "our product $=+1$", which holds. Hence $D,E,F$ are collinear. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** For the *internal* bisectors the feet are inside the sides and the lines $AD',BE',CF'$ concur (Ceva); for the *external* ones the feet are collinear (Menelaus): a typical Ceva/Menelaus duality.

**Lesson.** Ceva = concurrency of cevians, Menelaus = collinearity of points on the side lines; both reduce to a product of ratios equal to $\pm1$, which you check with the bisector theorem or tangent lengths.

## Statement

$$\frac1a=\frac1b+\frac1c.$$

## Statement

On each side of an arbitrary triangle $ABC$, an equilateral triangle is erected outwards. Prove that the centres of these three equilateral triangles are the vertices of an equilateral triangle.

## Hint

Orient $ABC$ counter-clockwise and use complex numbers. The third vertex of the outward equilateral triangle on $BC$ is obtained by rotating $C$ about $B$ by $-60^\circ$. To show that $z_1,z_2,z_3$ form an equilateral triangle, it is enough to show $z_1+\omega z_2+\omega^2z_3=0$, where $\omega=e^{2i\pi/3}$.

## Solution

**Idea.** The centre of each small triangle is an explicit complex-linear combination of two vertices of $ABC$; a root-of-unity identity finishes.

**Third vertices.** Write $\varepsilon=e^{i\pi/3}$, so $1-\varepsilon^{-1}=\varepsilon$. For a counter-clockwise triangle $ABC$, the exterior of $ABC$ across $BC$ is on the right of the vector $\overrightarrow{BC}$, so the outer vertex is
$$A'=B+(C-B)\varepsilon^{-1}=\varepsilon B+\varepsilon^{-1}C.$$
The centre of triangle $BCA'$ is
$$G_a=\frac{B+C+A'}3=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)B+(1+\varepsilon^{-1})C}3=\frac{uB+\bar uC}{\sqrt3},\qquad u=e^{i\pi/6},$$
since $1+\varepsilon=\sqrt3\,e^{i\pi/6}$. By cyclic symmetry (same orientation),
$$G_b=\frac{uC+\bar uA}{\sqrt3},\qquad G_c=\frac{uA+\bar uB}{\sqrt3}.$$

**The key identity.** Let $\omega=e^{2i\pi/3}$. Then $\sqrt3\,(G_a+\omega G_b+\omega^2G_c)$ equals
$$A\,(\omega\bar u+\omega^2u)+B\,(u+\omega^2\bar u)+C\,(\bar u+\omega u).$$
Each bracket vanishes:
- $\omega\bar u=e^{i\pi/2}=i$ and $\omega^2u=e^{i3\pi/2}=-i$, sum $0$;
- $u=e^{i\pi/6}$ and $\omega^2\bar u=e^{i7\pi/6}=-e^{i\pi/6}$, sum $0$;
- $\bar u=e^{-i\pi/6}$ and $\omega u=e^{i5\pi/6}=-e^{-i\pi/6}$, sum $0$.

So $G_a+\omega G_b+\omega^2G_c=0$.

**From the identity to the equilateral triangle.** Using $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$, the relation $G_a+\omega G_b+\omega^2 G_c=0$ becomes $(G_a-G_c)+\omega(G_b-G_c)=0$, i.e. $G_a-G_c=-\omega\,(G_b-G_c)=e^{-i\pi/3}(G_b-G_c)$. So the vector $\overrightarrow{G_cG_a}$ is obtained from $\overrightarrow{G_cG_b}$ by a rotation of $-60^\circ$: the triangle $G_aG_bG_c$ is equilateral. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The statement also holds with the triangles erected inwards. The centre of the Napoleon triangle is the centroid of $ABC$.

**Lesson.** Rotations by $\pm60^\circ$ and $\pm90^\circ$ are multiplications by fixed unit complex numbers; equilateral triangles are detected by $z_1+\omega z_2+\omega^2z_3=0$.

## Hint

Use **directed angles modulo $180^\circ$**: $\angle(\ell,m)$ is the angle by which one must rotate the line $\ell$ to make it parallel to $m$. Four points $W,X,Y,Z$ are concyclic iff $\angle(YW,YX)=\angle(ZW,ZX)$. Directed angles make the proof independent of the figure.

## Solution

**Idea.** Chase the angle between $PP'$ and the line $BP'Q'$ (which contains $B,P',Q'$) through the two circles.

**Chase.** The points $A,B,P,P'$ are concyclic (on $\Gamma_1$), so
$$\angle(P'P,P'B)=\angle(AP,AB).$$
The points $P,A,Q$ are collinear, so $\angle(AP,AB)=\angle(AQ,AB)$.
The points $A,B,Q,Q'$ are concyclic (on $\Gamma_2$), so
$$\angle(AQ,AB)=\angle(Q'Q,Q'B).$$
Altogether, $\angle(P'P,P'B)=\angle(Q'Q,Q'B)$. The lines $P'B$ and $Q'B$ are the same line $\ell$ (through $B,P',Q'$), so
$$\angle(PP',\ell)=\angle(QQ',\ell),$$
which means $PP'\parallel QQ'$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** If the second line is tangent to a circle at $B$, the same proof works with the tangent-chord angle replacing the inscribed angle. When $P'=B$ or $Q'=B$, the line $BP'$ is understood as the tangent.

**Lesson.** Directed angles mod $180^\circ$ remove every "inside or outside?" worry from angle chasing; learn them early.

## Statement

Let $ABCD$ be a convex quadrilateral. Find the point $P$ of the plane for which $PA+PB+PC+PD$ is minimal, and the minimal value.

## Hint

Group the terms in pairs: $PA+PC$ and $PB+PD$. What does the triangle inequality say about each pair?

## Solution

**Lower bound.** For every point $P$, the triangle inequality gives
$$PA+PC\ge AC,\qquad PB+PD\ge BD.$$
Therefore $PA+PB+PC+PD\ge AC+BD$.

**Equality.** The first inequality is an equality iff $P$ lies on the segment $AC$, the second iff $P$ lies on the segment $BD$. Since the quadrilateral is convex, its diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ intersect at a single point $X$ inside it. This point lies on both segments, so for $P=X$ both inequalities are equalities.

**Answer.** The minimum is $AC+BD$ and it is attained exactly at the intersection point $X$ of the diagonals (it is the only point on both segments, since the diagonals are not collinear).

**Remark.** For a non-convex quadrilateral the diagonals' lines still meet, but not necessarily at a point of both segments; then one of the vertices (the reflex one, inside the triangle formed by the other three) gives the minimum instead.

**Lesson.** To minimise a sum of distances, pair the terms so that the triangle inequality gives a constant lower bound, then check when it is attained.

## Statement

In triangle $ABC$, the points $D,E,F$ lie on the sides $BC,CA,AB$ with
$$\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{CE}{EA}=\frac{AF}{FB}=2.$$
The cevians $AD$, $BE$, $CF$ form a small triangle (by their pairwise intersections). Prove that its area is $\frac17$ of the area of $ABC$.

## Hint

Ratios of areas are preserved by affine maps, and every triangle is the image of the triangle with vertices $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$ under an affine map. So it is enough to do the computation in coordinates for this particular triangle.

## Solution

**Reduction.** An affine map multiplies all areas by the same constant and maps lines to lines and midpoints/ratios along lines to the same ratios. Hence we may assume $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(0,1)$, of area $\frac12$.

**The points.**
- $D$ on $BC$ with $BD:DC=2:1$: $D=B+\frac23(C-B)=\left(\frac13,\frac23\right)$.
- $E$ on $CA$ with $CE:EA=2:1$: $E=C+\frac23(A-C)=\left(0,\frac13\right)$.
- $F$ on $AB$ with $AF:FB=2:1$: $F=\left(\frac23,0\right)$.

**The cevians.**
- $AD$: $y=2x$.
- $BE$: through $(1,0)$ and $\left(0,\frac13\right)$: $y=\frac{1-x}3$.
- $CF$: through $(0,1)$ and $\left(\frac23,0\right)$: $y=1-\frac{3x}2$.

**Intersections.**
- $AD\cap BE$: $2x=\frac{1-x}3\Rightarrow x=\frac17$: the point $P_1=\left(\frac17,\frac27\right)$.
- $BE\cap CF$: $\frac{1-x}3=1-\frac{3x}2\Rightarrow\frac{7x}2=2\Rightarrow x=\frac47$: $P_2=\left(\frac47,\frac17\right)$.
- $CF\cap AD$: $2x=1-\frac{3x}2\Rightarrow x=\frac27$: $P_3=\left(\frac27,\frac47\right)$.

**Area.** With $\overrightarrow{P_1P_2}=\left(\frac37,-\frac17\right)$ and $\overrightarrow{P_1P_3}=\left(\frac17,\frac27\right)$:
$$[P_1P_2P_3]=\frac12\left|\frac37\cdot\frac27-\left(-\frac17\right)\cdot\frac17\right|=\frac12\cdot\frac7{49}=\frac1{14}.$$
Since $[ABC]=\frac12$, the ratio is $\frac{1/14}{1/2}=\frac17$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** This is a special case of **Routh's theorem**: for ratios $r$ in place of $2$, the area ratio is $\dfrac{(r-1)^2}{r^2+r+1}$; for $r=2$ it gives $\frac17$, for $r=1$ (medians) it gives $0$.

**Lesson.** Area-ratio problems often reduce, by affine invariance, to a computation in the coordinate triangle $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$.
