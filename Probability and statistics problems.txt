## Statement

A fair six-sided die is rolled once. Let $A$ be the event "the result is even" and $B$ the event "the result is at least 4".

Compute $P(A)$, $P(B)$, $P(A\cap B)$, $P(A\cup B)$, and $P(A\mid B)$.

## Hint

List the outcomes in each event. For the conditional probability, use $P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)$.

## Solution

**Universe.** $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, each outcome has probability $\frac16$.

**Events.** $A=\{2,4,6\}$, $B=\{4,5,6\}$, $A\cap B=\{4,6\}$.

**Probabilities.**
$$P(A)=\frac36=\frac12,\qquad P(B)=\frac36=\frac12,\qquad P(A\cap B)=\frac26=\frac13.$$
By inclusion–exclusion:
$$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)=\frac12+\frac12-\frac13=\frac23.$$
Finally,
$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}=\frac{1/3}{1/2}=\frac23.$$

**Check.** Given that the result is at least 4, the possible outcomes are $4,5,6$; two of them ($4$ and $6$) are even, so $\frac23$ is consistent.

## Statement

Two fair six-sided dice are rolled independently. Compute the probability that their sum equals $7$, then the probability that the sum is at least $10$.

## Hint

There are $36$ equally likely ordered pairs. Count the pairs $(i,j)$ with $i+j=7$.

## Solution

**Universe.** $\Omega=\{1,\ldots,6\}^2$ has $36$ equally likely outcomes.

**Sum equal to 7.** The favourable pairs are $(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)$: there are $6$. Hence
$$P(S=7)=\frac{6}{36}=\frac16.$$

**Sum at least 10.** Count:
- sum $=10$: $(4,6),(5,5),(6,4)$ ? $3$ pairs;
- sum $=11$: $(5,6),(6,5)$ ? $2$ pairs;
- sum $=12$: $(6,6)$ ? $1$ pair.

Total $3+2+1=6$, so
$$P(S\ge10)=\frac{6}{36}=\frac16.$$

**Remark.** The sum $7$ is the most likely value, because it has the largest number of decompositions into two integers between $1$ and $6$.

## Statement

A biased coin gives heads with probability $p=0.3$. It is tossed $10$ times independently. Let $X$ be the number of heads.

1. What is the law of $X$?

## Statement

In a group of $n$ people, assume all birthdays are independent and uniformly distributed among $365$ days. Compute the probability $p_n$ that at least two people share a birthday. Evaluate it for $n=23$ and $n=50$.

## Hint

Compute the complement: all $n$ birthdays are distinct. The number of injective assignments is $365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1)$.

## Solution

**Complement.** The probability that all $n$ birthdays are distinct is
$$q_n=\frac{365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1)}{365^n}.$$
Therefore
$$p_n=1-q_n.$$

**Computation for $n=23$.**
$$q_{23}=\prod_{k=0}^{22}\left(1-\frac{k}{365}\right)\approx0.4927,$$
so $p_{23}\approx1-0.4927=0.5073$. With $23$ people, it is already more likely than not that two share a birthday.

**Computation for $n=50$.** $q_{50}\approx0.0296$, so $p_{50}\approx0.9704$: almost certainty.

**Remark.** The threshold $n=23$ is surprisingly small because the number of *pairs* is $\binom{n}{2}$, which grows quadratically: for $n=23$, there are $253$ pairs, each with a small chance of matching.

## Statement

A disease affects $1\%$ of a population. A test is positive for $99\%$ of sick people and for $5\%$ of healthy people. A person tests positive. What is the probability that the person is actually sick?

## Hint

Apply Bayes' formula: $P(\text{sick}\mid+)=P(+\mid\text{sick})P(\text{sick})/P(+)$, and compute $P(+)$ by the law of total probability.

## Solution

**Notation.** $S$ = sick, $H$ = healthy, $+$ = positive test. We know
$$P(S)=0.01,\quad P(H)=0.99,\quad P(+\mid S)=0.99,\quad P(+\mid H)=0.05.$$

**Total probability.**
$$P(+)=P(+\mid S)P(S)+P(+\mid H)P(H)=0.99\cdot0.01+0.05\cdot0.99=0.0099+0.0495=0.0594.$$

**Bayes.**
$$P(S\mid+)=\frac{0.0099}{0.0594}\approx0.1667.$$

**Conclusion.** Even after a positive test, the probability of being sick is only about $16.7\%$. This is because the disease is rare: the many false positives among the healthy dominate.

**Remark.** This counter-intuitive result is the classical "base rate fallacy".

## Statement

A lot of $100$ fuses contains $5\%$ defective fuses ($5$ defective, $95$ good). A sample of $6$ fuses is drawn *without replacement*. Let $X$ be the number of defective fuses in the sample.

1. What is the law of $X$?
2. Compute $P(X=0)$ exactly.
3. Approximate by a binomial law and compare.

**1.** $X$ follows the hypergeometric distribution $\mathcal{H}(N=100,n=6,p=0.05)$.

The approximation is accurate to about $0.6$ percentage point.

**Remark.** This is a classical acceptance-sampling computation: the lot is accepted if no defective fuse is found, which happens with probability about $73\%$.

## Statement

An urn contains $12$ balls: $4$ red, $4$ white, $4$ green, numbered $1$ to $4$ within each colour (so there are three balls with each number $1,\ldots,4$). Four balls are drawn simultaneously.

Compute:
(a) the probability of exactly three balls of the same colour;
(b) the probability of at least one ball numbered $1$;
(c) the probability of exactly one ball numbered $1$ and exactly three red balls.

## Hint

The total number of draws is $\binom{12}{4}=495$. Count favourable cases with combinations.

## Solution

**(a)** Choose the colour with three balls ($3$ ways), choose $3$ among $4$ balls of that colour ($\binom43=4$), and choose the fourth ball among the $8$ balls of the other two colours ($\binom81=8$):
$$P=\frac{3\cdot4\cdot8}{495}=\frac{96}{495}\approx0.1939.$$

**(b)** Complement: no ball numbered $1$ means choosing $4$ balls among the $9$ balls numbered $2,3,4$:
$$P=1-\frac{\binom94}{\binom{12}{4}}=1-\frac{126}{495}=\frac{41}{55}\approx0.7455.$$

**(c)** Split according to whether the ball numbered $1$ is red or not.

- If the ball numbered $1$ is red: it is one of the $4$ red balls, and we need $2$ more red balls among the $3$ remaining red ones ($\binom32=3$) and $1$ ball among the $8$ non-red balls not numbered $1$... careful: the other balls numbered $1$ are excluded. There are $2$ other balls numbered $1$ (white and green), excluded. So among the $12$ balls: $4$ red, of which one is numbered $1$; the other $8$ non-red balls are split into $2$ balls numbered $1$ (excluded) and $6$ balls numbered $2,3,4$ non-red. We need $3$ red balls total (including the red numbered $1$) and no other $1$: choose $2$ other red among $3$ ($\binom32=3$), and $1$ ball among the $6$ non-red balls not numbered $1$. Count: $1\cdot3\cdot6=18$.
- If the ball numbered $1$ is not red: choose it among the $2$ non-red balls numbered $1$ ($2$ ways), then $3$ red balls among the $4$ red ($\binom43=4$). Count: $2\cdot4=8$.

Total: $18+8=26$, so
$$P=\frac{26}{495}\approx0.0525.$$

**Remark.** Part (c) is delicate because the two conditions overlap: the ball numbered $1$ may itself be red.

## Statement

In a group of $n$ people, all birthdays are independent and uniform over $365$ days. Given that at least two people share a birthday, what is the probability that exactly two people share a birthday (and all others have distinct birthdays)?

## Hint

Count the favourable configurations: choose the pair sharing a birthday, choose their common birthday, then assign distinct birthdays to the remaining $n-2$ people avoiding that birthday.

## Solution

**Favourable count.** Choose the pair: $\binom n2$. Choose their common birthday: $365$. Assign the remaining $n-2$ people distinct birthdays among the remaining $364$ days: $364\cdot363\cdots(364-n+3)$.

So the number of favourable outcomes is
$$N_{\text{fav}}=\binom n2\cdot365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+2).$$
The probability of "exactly one pair" is $N_{\text{fav}}/365^n$.

**Conditional probability.** Let $E$ = "at least one shared birthday" and $F$ = "exactly one pair". Then
$$P(F\mid E)=\frac{P(F)}{P(E)}=\frac{N_{\text{fav}}/365^n}{1-q_n},$$
where $q_n$ is the probability that all birthdays are distinct.

**Remark.** For $n=23$, this conditional probability is roughly $0.7$: most collisions are single pairs.

## Statement

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two random variables with finite variance. Prove that
$$\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).$$

## Hint

Expand $E((X+Y)^2)-(E(X+Y))^2$ and use $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E(XY)-E(X)E(Y)$.

## Solution

**Computation.**
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)&=E((X+Y)^2)-(E(X+Y))^2\\
&=E(X^2)+2E(XY)+E(Y^2)-(E(X)+E(Y))^2\\
&=E(X^2)-E(X)^2+E(Y^2)-E(Y)^2+2(E(XY)-E(X)E(Y))\\
&=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).
\end{align*}

## Statement

Therefore
$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{2}{\lambda^2}-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}.$$

## Statement

$$\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\right)=n\sigma^2+n(n-1)\left(-\frac{\sigma^2}{N-1}\right)=n\sigma^2\left(1-\frac{n-1}{N-1}\right)=n\sigma^2\cdot\frac{N-n}{N-1}.$$

$$Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).$$

**Interpretation.** If we repeat the experiment many times, about $95\%$ of the intervals produced will contain the true $\mu$. The interval itself is random; the parameter is fixed.

## Statement

Let $(X,Y)$ be a pair with finite variances. Define the Pearson correlation coefficient
$$\rho=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}.$$
Show that $|\rho|\le1$, with equality iff $Y=aX+b$ almost surely for some $a,b$.

## Hint

Apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the centred variables.

## Solution

**Cauchy–Schwarz.** Let $\tilde X=X-E(X)$ and $\tilde Y=Y-E(Y)$. Then
$$|\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)|=|E(\tilde X\tilde Y)|\le\sqrt{E(\tilde X^2)E(\tilde Y^2)}=\sigma_X\sigma_Y.$$
Dividing by $\sigma_X\sigma_Y$ gives $|\rho|\le1$.

**Equality case.** In Cauchy–Schwarz, equality holds iff $\tilde Y=\lambda\tilde X$ almost surely for some $\lambda$. That is, $Y-E(Y)=\lambda(X-E(X))$, i.e. $Y=\lambda X+(E(Y)-\lambda E(X))$. So $Y$ is an affine function of $X$.

**Interpretation.** $\rho$ measures the strength of the *linear* relationship between $X$ and $Y$. Values near $\pm1$ indicate an almost perfect linear relation; values near $0$ indicate no linear relation (but possibly a nonlinear one).

## Statement

Given $n$ points $(x_i,y_i)$, find the line $y=ax+b$ minimizing $\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-ax_i-b)^2$.

## Hint

Take partial derivatives with respect to $a$ and $b$, set them to zero, and solve the normal equations.

## Solution

**Objective.** 
$$Q(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-ax_i-b)^2.$$

**Normal equations.** 
$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial b}=-2\sum(y_i-ax_i-b)=0\implies b=\bar y-a\bar x.$$
$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial a}=-2\sum x_i(y_i-ax_i-b)=0\implies \sum x_iy_i=a\sum x_i^2+b\sum x_i.$$

$$\sum x_iy_i-n\bar x\bar y=a\left(\sum x_i^2-n\bar x^2\right).$$
Hence
$$a=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)},\qquad b=\bar y-a\bar x.$$

**Interpretation.** The slope $a$ is the covariance of $x$ and $y$ divided by the variance of $x$. The line passes through the centroid $(\bar x,\bar y)$.

## Statement

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with both parameters unknown. Show that $(\bar X,S^2)$ is a sufficient statistic for $(\mu,\sigma^2)$, and that it is minimal.

## Hint

Use the factorization criterion: the joint density should factor as $g(T;\theta)h(x)$.

## Solution

**Joint density.**
$$f(x_1,\ldots,x_n;\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)^2\right).$$

**Factorization.** Write
$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)^2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar x)^2+n(\bar x-\mu)^2.$$
Then
$$f=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(n-1)s^2}{2\sigma^2}-\frac{n(\bar x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).$$
This has the form $g(\bar x,s^2;\mu,\sigma^2)\cdot1$, so $(\bar X,S^2)$ is sufficient by the factorization criterion.

**Minimality.** The statistic $(\bar X,S^2)$ is complete for the normal family (a classical result), hence it is minimal sufficient.

**Interpretation.** All the information about $(\mu,\sigma^2)$ contained in the sample is captured by the pair $(\bar X,S^2)$. No information is lost by discarding the individual order or the higher moments.

title: The Rao–Blackwell theorem
category: statistics/estimation
level: m1
difficulty: 5
tags: Rao–Blackwell, sufficiency, variance reduction
date: 2026-11-02
---

## Statement

Let $T$ be a sufficient statistic for a parameter $\theta$, and let $U$ be any unbiased estimator of $\theta$ with finite variance. Show that $W=E(U\mid T)$ is an unbiased estimator of $\theta$ with $\operatorname{Var}(W)\le\operatorname{Var}(U)$.

## Hint

Use the tower property $E(E(U\mid T))=E(U)$ and the conditional variance formula.

## Solution

**Unbiasedness.** By the tower property,
$$E(W)=E(E(U\mid T))=E(U)=\theta.$$

**Variance.** The conditional variance formula gives
$$\operatorname{Var}(U)=E(\operatorname{Var}(U\mid T))+\operatorname{Var}(E(U\mid T))=E(\operatorname{Var}(U\mid T))+\operatorname{Var}(W).$$
Since $E(\operatorname{Var}(U\mid T))\ge0$, we get
$$\operatorname{Var}(W)\le\operatorname{Var}(U).$$

**Conclusion.** Conditioning on a sufficient statistic cannot increase the variance. Starting from any unbiased estimator, we can improve it by Rao–Blackwellization.

**Remark.** If $T$ is complete, the resulting estimator is the unique best unbiased estimator (Lehmann–Scheffé theorem).

title: The Cramér–Rao bound

date: 2026-11-02
---

## Statement

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with density $f(x;\theta)$. State and prove (sketch) the Cramér–Rao bound:
$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\ge\frac{1}{nI(\theta)},$$
where $I(\theta)=E\left[\left(\frac{\partial\ln f}{\partial\theta}\right)^2\right]$ is the Fisher information.

## Hint

Use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on the score function and the estimator.

## Solution

**Score function.** Let $s(x;\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(x;\theta)$.

**Fisher information.** $I(\theta)=E(s^2)$ (and under regularity, $I(\theta)=-E(\partial s/\partial\theta)$).

**Unbiasedness.** For an unbiased estimator $\hat\theta$,
$$E(\hat\theta)=\theta\implies\int\hat\theta(x)f(x;\theta)\,dx=\theta.$$
Differentiating with respect to $\theta$:
$$\int\hat\theta\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\,dx=1.$$
Since $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}=f\cdot s$, this is
$$E(\hat\theta\,s)=1.$$

**Cauchy–Schwarz.** 
$$1=E(\hat\theta\,s)^2\le E(\hat\theta^2)E(s^2)=E(\hat\theta^2)I(\theta).$$
But $E(\hat\theta^2)=\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)+\theta^2$ and $E(s)=0$ gives $\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta,s)=1$. Applying Cauchy–Schwarz to centred variables:
$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\cdot I(\theta)\ge1.$$

**Conclusion.** $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\ge1/I(\theta)$. For $n$ i.i.d. observations, the information is $nI(\theta)$, giving the bound $1/(nI(\theta))$.

## Solution

$$p_k=\frac12p_{k-1}+\frac12p_{k+1}.$$

**Boundary conditions.** 
$$p_0=1\implies A=1,\qquad p_N=0\implies 1+BN=0\implies B=-\frac1N.$$
Hence
$$p_k=1-\frac{k}{N}=\frac{N-k}{N}.$$

**Conclusion.** The probability of ruin starting from $k$ is $\frac{N-k}{N}$
