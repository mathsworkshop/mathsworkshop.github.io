## Statement

Real numbers $a,b,c$ satisfy $a+b+c=2$, $a^2+b^2+c^2=6$ and $a^3+b^3+c^3=8$. Find $a^4+b^4+c^4$.

## Hint

Write $e_1=a+b+c$, $e_2=ab+bc+ca$, $e_3=abc$. The numbers $a,b,c$ are the roots of $t^3-e_1t^2+e_2t-e_3$, so each satisfies $x^3=e_1x^2-e_2x+e_3$. Multiply by $x$ and sum.

## Solution

**Idea.** Compute $e_1,e_2,e_3$, then use the polynomial satisfied by each root to push the power sums up one degree at a time.

**Step 1: $e_2$.** From $(a+b+c)^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2e_2$ we get $4=6+2e_2$, so $e_2=-1$.

**Step 2: $e_3$.** Each root satisfies $x^3=2x^2+x+e_3$ (since $e_1=2$, $e_2=-1$). Summing over $x=a,b,c$:
$$8=p_3=2p_2+p_1+3e_3=12+2+3e_3,\qquad\text{so } e_3=-2.$$

**Step 3: $p_4$.** Multiply $x^3=2x^2+x-2$ by $x$: $x^4=2x^3+x^2-2x$. Summing,
$$p_4=2p_3+p_2-2p_1=16+6-4=18.$$

**Check.** The polynomial is $t^3-2t^2-t+2=(t-2)(t-1)(t+1)$, with roots $2,1,-1$: indeed $p_1=2$, $p_2=6$, $p_3=8$, $p_4=16+1+1=18$.

**Lesson.** Newton's identities: $p_{k+3}=e_1p_{k+2}-e_2p_{k+1}+e_3p_k$. You never need the roots themselves, only the elementary symmetric polynomials.

## Statement

1. A polynomial $P$ satisfies $P(1)=2$ and $P(2)=3$. Find the remainder of $P$ divided by $(x-1)(x-2)$.
2. Find the remainder of $x^{2026}+x+1$ divided by $x^2+x+1$.

## Hint

The remainder of a division by a polynomial of degree $n$ has degree $<n$. For part 2, notice that $x^3-1=(x-1)(x^2+x+1)$.

## Solution

**1.** The remainder has degree at most $1$: $P(x)=(x-1)(x-2)Q(x)+ax+b$. Evaluating at $1$ and $2$ gives $a+b=2$ and $2a+b=3$, so $a=1$, $b=1$. The remainder is $x+1$.

**2.** Since $x^3-1$ is divisible by $x^2+x+1$, we have $x^3\equiv1$ modulo $x^2+x+1$. Now $2026=3\cdot675+1$, so
$$x^{2026}=\left(x^3\right)^{675}x\equiv x.$$
Therefore $x^{2026}+x+1\equiv2x+1$. As $2x+1$ has degree $<2$, it is the remainder.

**Remark.** Equivalently, with $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$ (a root of $x^2+x+1$): $\omega^{2026}+\omega+1=2\omega+1$, and the remainder is the unique polynomial of degree $\le1$ taking the same values at $\omega$ and $\bar\omega$.

**Lesson.** Work "modulo" the divisor: replace $x^2+x+1$ by $0$ (or $x^3$ by $1$) and reduce the exponents.

## Statement

Let $P$ be a polynomial with integer coefficients.

1. Show that for all integers $a\ne b$, $a-b$ divides $P(a)-P(b)$. Deduce that no such $P$ satisfies $P(7)=11$ and $P(11)=13$.
2. Suppose that $P(a)=P(b)=P(c)=-1$ for three distinct integers $a,b,c$. Show that $P$ has no integer root.

## Hint

For part 1, use $x^k-y^k=(x-y)(x^{k-1}+\dots+y^{k-1})$. For part 2, write $P(x)+1=(x-a)(x-b)(x-c)Q(x)$ and look at an integer root $n$.

## Solution

**1.** If $P(x)=\sum c_kx^k$, then $P(a)-P(b)=\sum c_k(a^k-b^k)$ and each $a^k-b^k$ is a multiple of $a-b$. So $(a-b)\mid P(a)-P(b)$.

If $P(7)=11$ and $P(11)=13$, then $7-11=-4$ must divide $11-13=-2$: false. So no such polynomial exists.

**2.** The polynomial $P(x)+1$ has the integer roots $a,b,c$, so $P(x)+1=(x-a)(x-b)(x-c)Q(x)$ where $Q$ has integer coefficients (division by a monic polynomial keeps coefficients in $\mathbb{Z}$). If $n$ were an integer root of $P$, then
$$(n-a)(n-b)(n-c)Q(n)=P(n)+1=1.$$
So $n-a,n-b,n-c$ are three integers whose product divides $1$: each of them is $\pm1$. But they are pairwise distinct, and there are only two values $\pm1$. Contradiction.

**Lesson.** For integer polynomials, always think of the divisibility $a-b\mid P(a)-P(b)$ and of factoring $P(x)-k$ over the integers.

## Statement

Prove that $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ is irrational, by finding a monic polynomial with integer coefficients that vanishes at this number.

## Hint

Isolate the radicals step by step: square $x=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$, move the rational part to the left, and square again.

## Solution

**Polynomial.** If $x=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$, then $x^2=5+2\sqrt6$, so $x^2-5=2\sqrt6$ and $(x^2-5)^2=24$, i.e.
$$x^4-10x^2+1=0.$$

**Rational root theorem.** If a rational number $p/q$ in lowest terms is a root of a polynomial with integer coefficients, then $p$ divides the constant term and $q$ divides the leading coefficient. Here the polynomial is monic with constant term $1$, so the only rational candidates are $\pm1$. But
$$1-10+1=-8\neq0\quad\text{(for both }x=1\text{ and }x=-1\text{)}.$$
So the polynomial has no rational root. Since $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ is a root, it is irrational.

**Remark.** The same method works for many sums of radicals, e.g. $\sqrt2+\sqrt[3]3$ (try it: it gives a monic degree-$6$ polynomial with constant term $1$, so again only $\pm1$ must be excluded).

**Lesson.** To prove irrationality of an algebraic number, build an integer polynomial it satisfies, then apply the rational root theorem (a monic integer polynomial has only integer rational roots).

title: Irreducibility with Eisenstein's criterion
category: olympiad/oly-algebra

difficulty: 4
tags: Eisenstein, irreducibility, cyclotomic polynomial, shift trick
date: 2026-11-04

## Statement

**Eisenstein's criterion.** Let $f(x)=a_nx^n+\dots+a_0$ have integer coefficients and let $p$ be a prime with $p\nmid a_n$, $p\mid a_i$ for $i<n$, and $p^2\nmid a_0$. Then $f$ cannot be written as a product of two non-constant polynomials with integer coefficients (and so it is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by Gauss's lemma).

Use it to prove:
1. $x^4+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.
2. For a prime $p$, $\Phi_p(x)=x^{p-1}+x^{p-2}+\dots+x+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

## Hint

Eisenstein does not apply directly. Replace $x$ by $x+1$: if $f(x+1)$ is irreducible, so is $f(x)$ (a factorisation of one gives a factorisation of the other).

## Solution

**Shift trick.** A factorisation $f(x)=g(x)h(x)$ gives $f(x+1)=g(x+1)h(x+1)$ with the same degrees, and conversely. So $f$ and $f(x+1)$ are irreducible simultaneously.

**1.** $(x+1)^4+1=x^4+4x^3+6x^2+4x+2$. With $p=2$: the leading coefficient $1$ is odd; $4,6,4,2$ are all even; and the constant term $2$ is not divisible by $4$. Eisenstein applies: irreducible.

**2.** Since $\Phi_p(x)=\dfrac{x^p-1}{x-1}$,
$$\Phi_p(x+1)=\frac{(x+1)^p-1}{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\binom pkx^{k-1}=x^{p-1}+\binom p{p-1}x^{p-2}+\dots+\binom p2x+p.$$
The leading coefficient is $1$. For $1\le k\le p-1$, $p\mid\binom pk$ because $p$ divides $p!$ but not $k!(p-k)!$. The constant term is $\binom p1=p$, not divisible by $p^2$. By Eisenstein, $\Phi_p(x+1)$ is irreducible, hence so is $\Phi_p(x)$.

**Remark.** Thus $\Phi_p$ is the minimal polynomial of $e^{2\pi i/p}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$: a rational polynomial vanishing at this number must be a multiple of $\Phi_p$.

**Lesson.** If a polynomial looks irreducible but Eisenstein fails, try the shifts $x\mapsto x+1$ or $x\mapsto x-1$.

## Statement

Prove that for every integer $n\ge2$,
$$\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\sin\frac{k\pi}n=\frac n{2^{n-1}}.$$

## Hint

The numbers $\omega^k=e^{2\pi ik/n}$, $1\le k\le n-1$, are the roots of $x^{n-1}+x^{n-2}+\dots+1$. Evaluate this polynomial at $x=1$, and compute $|1-e^{i\theta}|$.

## Solution

**Factorisation.** The $n$-th roots of unity other than $1$ are the roots of $\dfrac{x^n-1}{x-1}=x^{n-1}+\dots+1$, so
$$x^{n-1}+\dots+x+1=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(x-\omega^k).$$
At $x=1$: $n=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(1-\omega^k)$.

**Moduli.** For $\theta\in(0,2\pi)$,
$$|1-e^{i\theta}|=\left|e^{i\theta/2}\right|\left|e^{-i\theta/2}-e^{i\theta/2}\right|=2\sin\frac\theta2.$$
With $\theta=\frac{2\pi k}n$ this is $2\sin\frac{k\pi}n>0$. Taking moduli in the product,
$$n=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}2\sin\frac{k\pi}n=2^{n-1}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\sin\frac{k\pi}n,$$
which is the claim.

**Check.** $n=3$: $\sin\frac\pi3\sin\frac{2\pi}3=\frac34=\frac3{2^2}$ ✓. $n=4$: $\frac{\sqrt2}2\cdot1\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}2=\frac12=\frac4{2^3}$ ✓.

**Lesson.** A trigonometric product over equally spaced angles often comes from evaluating $\prod(x-\omega^k)$ at a well-chosen point.

## Statement

Let $P$ be a polynomial of degree at most $n$ such that $P(k)=\dfrac k{k+1}$ for $k=0,1,\dots,n$. Compute $P(n+1)$.

## Hint

Consider $Q(x)=(x+1)P(x)-x$. What is its degree, and what are its roots? Then evaluate $Q$ at $x=-1$ to find its leading coefficient.

## Solution

**Auxiliary polynomial.** $Q(x)=(x+1)P(x)-x$ has degree at most $n+1$, and $Q(k)=(k+1)\frac k{k+1}-k=0$ for $k=0,\dots,n$. These are $n+1$ distinct roots, so
$$Q(x)=c\,x(x-1)\cdots(x-n)$$
for some constant $c$ (a polynomial of degree $\le n+1$ with $n+1$ given roots is $c$ times their product).

**The constant.** At $x=-1$: $Q(-1)=0\cdot P(-1)+1=1$, while the right side is
$$c(-1)(-2)\cdots(-1-n)=c\,(-1)^{n+1}(n+1)!.$$
Therefore $c=\dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$.

**At $x=n+1$.**
$$(n+2)P(n+1)-(n+1)=c\,(n+1)!=(-1)^{n+1},$$
so
$$P(n+1)=\frac{n+1+(-1)^{n+1}}{n+2}.$$
In particular $P(n+1)=1$ for $n$ odd and $\dfrac{n}{n+2}$ for $n$ even.

**Check.** $n=2$: the polynomial through $(0,0),(1,\frac12),(2,\frac23)$ takes the value $\frac12$ at $3$, and the formula gives $\frac{3-1}4=\frac12$ ✓.

**Lesson.** When values of $P$ are given on $n+1$ points, build an auxiliary polynomial vanishing there; its degree forces its shape up to a constant, which one evaluates at a convenient extra point.

## Statement

Define $a_0=0$, $a_1=1$ and $a_{n+2}=4a_{n+1}-a_n$. The first terms are $0,1,4,15,56,209,\dots$

1. Show that $a_na_{n+2}-a_{n+1}^2=-1$ for all $n\ge0$.
2. Find a closed formula for $a_n$.

## Hint

For part 1, show that the quantity $D_n=a_na_{n+2}-a_{n+1}^2$ does not depend on $n$. For part 2, solve $r^2=4r-1$.

## Solution

**1. An invariant.** Using $a_{n+3}=4a_{n+2}-a_{n+1}$ and then $4a_{n+1}-a_{n+2}=a_n$,
$$D_{n+1}=a_{n+1}a_{n+3}-a_{n+2}^2=a_{n+1}\bigl(4a_{n+2}-a_{n+1}\bigr)-a_{n+2}^2=a_{n+2}\bigl(4a_{n+1}-a_{n+2}\bigr)-a_{n+1}^2=a_{n+2}a_n-a_{n+1}^2=D_n.$$
So $D_n=D_0=a_0a_2-a_1^2=0\cdot4-1=-1$.

**2. Closed form.** The roots of $r^2-4r+1=0$ are $r_\pm=2\pm\sqrt3$. A general solution is $a_n=Ar_+^n+Br_-^n$. From $a_0=0$ we get $B=-A$, and $a_1=1$ gives $A(r_+-r_-)=2\sqrt3A=1$. So
$$a_n=\frac{(2+\sqrt3)^n-(2-\sqrt3)^n}{2\sqrt3}.$$

**Checks.** $n=2$: $(2+\sqrt3)^2-(2-\sqrt3)^2=8\sqrt3$, so $a_2=\frac{8\sqrt3}{2\sqrt3}=4$ ✓. Also $r_+r_-=1$ makes part 1 transparent: $a_na_{n+2}-a_{n+1}^2=-\frac{(r_+r_-)^n(r_+-r_-)^2}{12}=-1$.

**Remark.** The numbers $a_n$ are the $y$-values of the solutions of the Pell-type equation $x^2-3y^2=1$: $(x,y)=(a_{n+1}-2a_n,\ a_n)$, e.g. $(2,1),(7,4),(26,15)$.

**Lesson.** For a linear recurrence, look for a conserved quantity (a determinant-like expression) by checking that it does not change when $n\to n+1$; and always solve the characteristic equation $r^2=4r-1$ for a closed form.

## Statement

1. Compute $\displaystyle\prod_{k=2}^n\left(1-\frac1{k^2}\right)$.
2. Prove that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n\arctan\frac1{2k^2}=\frac\pi4-\arctan\frac1{2n+1}$, and deduce the limit as $n\to\infty$.

## Hint

For part 1, factor $1-\frac1{k^2}=\frac{(k-1)(k+1)}{k^2}$. For part 2, show that $\arctan\frac1{2k^2}=\arctan\frac1{2k-1}-\arctan\frac1{2k+1}$ using the tangent subtraction formula.

## Solution

**1.** $1-\frac1{k^2}=\frac{k-1}k\cdot\frac{k+1}k$, so
$$\prod_{k=2}^n\frac{k-1}k\cdot\prod_{k=2}^n\frac{k+1}k=\frac1n\cdot\frac{n+1}2=\frac{n+1}{2n}.$$
(Both products telescope: $\frac12\cdot\frac23\cdots\frac{n-1}n=\frac1n$ and $\frac32\cdot\frac43\cdots\frac{n+1}n=\frac{n+1}2$.)

**2. Key identity.** Let $\alpha=\arctan\frac1{2k-1}$ and $\beta=\arctan\frac1{2k+1}$, both in $\left(0,\frac\pi2\right)$ with $\alpha>\beta$. Then
$$\tan(\alpha-\beta)=\frac{\frac1{2k-1}-\frac1{2k+1}}{1+\frac1{(2k-1)(2k+1)}}=\frac{\frac2{4k^2-1}}{\frac{4k^2}{4k^2-1}}=\frac1{2k^2}.$$
Since $\alpha-\beta\in\left(0,\frac\pi2\right)$, we get $\alpha-\beta=\arctan\frac1{2k^2}$.

**Summing.**
$$\sum_{k=1}^n\arctan\frac1{2k^2}=\sum_{k=1}^n\left(\arctan\frac1{2k-1}-\arctan\frac1{2k+1}\right)=\arctan1-\arctan\frac1{2n+1}=\frac\pi4-\arctan\frac1{2n+1}.$$
As $n\to\infty$, the sum tends to $\dfrac\pi4$.

**Check.** $n=1$: $\arctan\frac12=\frac\pi4-\arctan\frac13$ since $\tan\left(\frac\pi4-\arctan\frac13\right)=\frac{1-1/3}{1+1/3}=\frac12$ ✓.

**Lesson.** Telescoping: try to write each term as $f(k)-f(k+1)$, using factorisations (products) or addition formulas (arctangents).

## Statement

1. Prove Hermite's identity: for every real $x$ and integer $n\ge1$,
$$\lfloor x\rfloor+\left\lfloor x+\tfrac1n\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor x+\tfrac2n\right\rfloor+\dots+\left\lfloor x+\tfrac{n-1}n\right\rfloor=\lfloor nx\rfloor.$$
2. Deduce that for integers $m\ge0$ and $n\ge1$: $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\lfloor\frac{m+k}n\right\rfloor=m$.

## Hint

Write $x=\lfloor x\rfloor+f$ with $0\le f<1$. In the sum, the terms are $\lfloor x\rfloor$ or $\lfloor x\rfloor+1$: find from which index $k$ the term jumps.

## Solution

**1.** Put $x=N+f$ with $N=\lfloor x\rfloor$ and $0\le f<1$. Then $\lfloor x+\frac kn\rfloor=N+\lfloor f+\frac kn\rfloor$, and $\lfloor f+\frac kn\rfloor\in\{0,1\}$ since $0\le f+\frac kn<2$. It equals $1$ iff $f+\frac kn\ge1$, i.e. $k\ge n-nf$. As $k$ is an integer, this is equivalent to $k\ge n-\lfloor nf\rfloor$ (because $n-k\le nf\iff n-k\le\lfloor nf\rfloor$ for the integer $n-k$).

So among $k=0,\dots,n-1$, the number of terms equal to $N+1$ is the number of $k$ with $n-\lfloor nf\rfloor\le k\le n-1$, namely $\lfloor nf\rfloor$ (note $\lfloor nf\rfloor\le n-1$). Hence the left side is
$$nN+\lfloor nf\rfloor=\lfloor nN+nf\rfloor=\lfloor nx\rfloor.$$

**2.** Take $x=\frac mn$: then $\lfloor nx\rfloor=m$, and the identity becomes $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\lfloor\frac{m+k}n\right\rfloor=m$.

**Example.** $m=100$, $n=7$: the terms $\lfloor\frac{100+k}7\rfloor$ for $k=0,\dots,6$ are $14,14,14,14,14,15,15$, whose sum is $100$ ✓.

**Lesson.** To handle floor functions, split into integer and fractional parts and find where the fractional part crosses an integer threshold.

Part 1: induction and $2\cos((n+1)\theta)+2\cos((n-1)\theta)=2\cos\theta\cdot2\cos n\theta$. Part 2: write $r=\frac pq$ and apply part 1 with $n=2q$: $2\cos(r\pi)$ is a root of the monic integer polynomial $P_{2q}(x)-2$.

## Solution

**1. Induction.** $P_0=2$ and $P_1=x$ have integer coefficients, and so does $P_{n+1}=xP_n-P_{n-1}$. If $P_{n-1}$ has degree $n-1$ and $P_n$ is monic of degree $n$, then $xP_n$ is monic of degree $n+1$ and $P_{n+1}$ is monic of degree $n+1$.

For the trigonometric identity: $P_0(2\cos\theta)=2=2\cos0$ and $P_1(2\cos\theta)=2\cos\theta$. Assume it holds for $n-1,n$. The sum-to-product formula $\cos((n+1)\theta)+\cos((n-1)\theta)=2\cos\theta\cos n\theta$ gives
$$2\cos((n+1)\theta)=2\cos\theta\cdot2\cos n\theta-2\cos((n-1)\theta)=2\cos\theta\,P_n(2\cos\theta)-P_{n-1}(2\cos\theta)=P_{n+1}(2\cos\theta).$$

**2.** Let $r=\frac pq$ with $q\ge1$ and put $\theta=r\pi$, $x=2\cos\theta$. Then
$$P_{2q}(x)=2\cos(2q\theta)=2\cos(2p\pi)=2,$$
so $x$ is a root of the **monic** integer polynomial $P_{2q}(t)-2$. If $x$ is rational, the rational root theorem forces $x$ to be an integer. Since $|x|\le2$, $x\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$, i.e. $\cos(r\pi)\in\left\{-1,-\frac12,0,\frac12,1\right\}$.

**Remark.** This is Niven's theorem. In particular $\cos1^\circ=\cos\frac\pi{180}$ is irrational, and so is $\cos\frac\pi5=\frac{1+\sqrt5}4$. Also $2\cos\frac{2\pi}7$ is a root of the monic polynomial $x^3+x^2-2x-1$.

**Lesson.** Express $2\cos n\theta$ as a monic integer polynomial in $2\cos\theta$: it turns trigonometric rationality questions into rational root theorem questions.

## Statement

1. Let $z,w$ be complex numbers of modulus $1$ with $zw\ne-1$. Show that $\dfrac{z+w}{1+zw}$ is real.
2. Let $z_1,z_2,z_3$ be complex numbers of modulus $1$ with $z_1+z_2+z_3=0$. Show that they form an equilateral triangle.

## Hint

For unit complex numbers, $\bar z=\frac1z$. For part 2, take $|z_1+z_2|=|z_3|$ and expand the square of the modulus.

## Solution

**1.** Let $u=\dfrac{z+w}{1+zw}$. Using $\bar z=\frac1z$ and $\bar w=\frac1w$:
$$\bar u=\frac{\frac1z+\frac1w}{1+\frac1{zw}}=\frac{\frac{w+z}{zw}}{\frac{zw+1}{zw}}=\frac{z+w}{1+zw}=u.$$
So $u=\bar u$, i.e. $u$ is real.

**2.** From $z_1+z_2=-z_3$ we get $|z_1+z_2|^2=1$. Expanding,
$$|z_1+z_2|^2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2\,\mathrm{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)=2+2\,\mathrm{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)=1,$$
so $\mathrm{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)=-\frac12$. Then
$$|z_1-z_2|^2=2-2\,\mathrm{Re}(z_1\bar z_2)=3.$$
By symmetry, $|z_2-z_3|^2=|z_3-z_1|^2=3$. The three sides all have length $\sqrt3$: equilateral.

**Remark.** The converse holds too: the vertices of an equilateral triangle inscribed in the unit circle, centred at $0$, are $z,\ \omega z,\ \omega^2z$ with $|z|=1$.

**Lesson.** For points on the unit circle use $\bar z=1/z$: it turns geometric conditions (reality, perpendicularity, collinearity) into algebraic identities.

## Statement

Solve in real numbers:

1. $x+y+z=3,\quad x^2+y^2+z^2=5,\quad x^3+y^3+z^3=9$,
2. $x+y+z=3,\quad x^2+y^2+z^2=3$.

## Hint

For the first system, find $e_2$ and $e_3$ and solve the cubic whose roots are $x,y,z$. For the second, think of $(x-1)^2+(y-1)^2+(z-1)^2$.

## Solution

**1. Elementary symmetric polynomials.** $e_1=3$. From $e_1^2=p_2+2e_2$: $9=5+2e_2$, so $e_2=2$. Newton's identity $p_3=e_1p_2-e_2p_1+3e_3$ gives
$$9=3\cdot5-2\cdot3+3e_3=9+3e_3,\quad\text{so } e_3=0.$$
Therefore $x,y,z$ are the roots of $t^3-3t^2+2t=t(t-1)(t-2)$. The solutions are all permutations of $(0,1,2)$.

**Check.** $0+1+2=3$, $0+1+4=5$, $0+1+8=9$ ✓.

**2.** Expanding,
$$(x-1)^2+(y-1)^2+(z-1)^2=(x^2+y^2+z^2)-2(x+y+z)+3=3-6+3=0.$$
A sum of three real squares is zero only if each is zero: the only solution is $(1,1,1)$.

**Lesson.** A symmetric system is solved through $e_1,e_2,e_3$ and the cubic whose roots are the unknowns. A sum of squares that vanishes gives immediate equalities.

## Statement

Find all real polynomials $P$ such that $P(x^2)=P(x)^2$ for all real $x$.

## Hint

Compare the leading coefficients, then the **second** non-zero term of $P$ (the term of second-highest degree) on both sides.

## Solution

**Constants.** If $P$ is constant, $c=c^2$ gives $P=0$ or $P=1$.

**Non-constant case.** Let $P(x)=ax^n+cx^k+(\text{terms of degree}<k)$ with $a\ne0$, $n\ge1$, and $k<n$ the degree of the second non-zero term, if any.

*Leading coefficient.* Comparing the coefficients of $x^{2n}$: $a=a^2$, so $a=1$.

*Second term.* Suppose $P$ has a second non-zero term, $cx^k$ with $c\ne0$, $0\le k<n$. In $P(x)^2=x^{2n}+2cx^{n+k}+\dots$, the second-highest term is $2cx^{n+k}$ (all other products have degree $<n+k$). In $P(x^2)=x^{2n}+cx^{2k}+\dots$, the second-highest degree is $2k<n+k$ (since $k<n$). So the coefficient of $x^{n+k}$ is $2c\ne0$ on the left and $0$ on the right: contradiction.

Hence $P(x)=x^n$ (the only possibility), and indeed $P(x^2)=x^{2n}=(x^n)^2$ ✓.

**Answer.** $P=0$, $P=1$, or $P(x)=x^n$ for some integer $n\ge1$.

**Lesson.** In polynomial identities, compare leading terms first, then the next term; degrees often give an immediate contradiction.

## Statement

Let $n\ge2$ and $\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}$. Compute $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac1{1-\omega^k}$.

## Hint

Pair the term $k$ with the term $n-k$: $\omega^{n-k}=\overline{\omega^k}$.

## Solution

**A pair.** Put $\theta=\frac{2\pi k}n\in(0,2\pi)$ and $u=e^{i\theta}$. Then $\overline u=\omega^{n-k}$ and
$$\frac1{1-u}+\frac1{1-\bar u}=\frac{2-u-\bar u}{(1-u)(1-\bar u)}=\frac{2-2\cos\theta}{|1-u|^2}=\frac{2-2\cos\theta}{2-2\cos\theta}=1.$$

**Summing.** Let $S=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac1{1-\omega^k}$. Since $k\mapsto n-k$ permutes $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$, also $S=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac1{1-\omega^{n-k}}$. Adding the two expressions of $S$,
$$2S=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac1{1-\omega^k}+\frac1{1-\omega^{n-k}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}1=n-1.$$

**Answer.** $S=\dfrac{n-1}2$.

**Checks.** $n=2$: $\frac1{1-(-1)}=\frac12$ ✓. $n=3$: the two terms are conjugate and add up to $1$ ✓.

**Lesson.** Symmetry $k\leftrightarrow n-k$ (complex conjugation) pairs terms; the sum of the pair is often constant.

## Statement

Define $x_1=\dfrac12$ and $x_{n+1}=x_n^2+x_n$. Compute
$$\left\lfloor\sum_{k=1}^{2025}\frac1{1+x_k}\right\rfloor.$$

## Hint

From $x_{k+1}=x_k(x_k+1)$, express $\frac1{1+x_k}$ as a difference of two consecutive terms $\frac1{x_k}-\frac1{x_{k+1}}$.

## Solution

**Telescoping.** Since $x_{k+1}=x_k(1+x_k)$,
$$\frac1{x_k}-\frac1{x_{k+1}}=\frac{x_{k+1}-x_k}{x_kx_{k+1}}=\frac{x_k^2}{x_k\cdot x_k(1+x_k)}=\frac1{1+x_k}.$$
Hence
$$\sum_{k=1}^{2025}\frac1{1+x_k}=\frac1{x_1}-\frac1{x_{2026}}=2-\frac1{x_{2026}}.$$

**Size of $x_{2026}$.** The sequence is positive and increasing ($x_{n+1}-x_n=x_n^2>0$). Moreover $x_2=\frac34$ and $x_3=\frac34\cdot\frac74=\frac{21}{16}>1$. So $x_{2026}>x_3>1$, hence $0<\frac1{x_{2026}}<1$.

**Conclusion.** The sum lies in $(1,2)$, so its integer part is $\boxed1$.

**Check.** With only $6$ terms the sum is $2-\frac1{x_7}\approx1.99996$.

**Lesson.** When a recurrence has the form $x_{n+1}=x_n(x_n+c)$, the quantity $\frac1{x_n+c}$ telescopes. Then control the remaining term by monotonicity.

## Statement

Let $x^3+px^2+qx+r$ have three real roots $a,b,c$. Prove that $p^2\ge3q$, with equality iff $a=b=c$.

## Hint

Express $p$ and $q$ with Vieta's formulas, then relate $p^2-3q$ to a sum of squares of differences.

## Solution

**Vieta.** $p=-(a+b+c)$ and $q=ab+bc+ca$. Hence
$$p^2-3q=(a+b+c)^2-3(ab+bc+ca)=a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca.$$

**Sum of squares.**
$$a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca=\frac12\left[(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2\right]\ge0.$$

**Equality.** It requires $a=b$, $b=c$, $c=a$, i.e. $a=b=c$.

**Remark.** Geometrically, $p^2\ge3q$ says that the derivative $3x^2+2px+q$ has a real root: this is Rolle's theorem applied between the real roots of the cubic. The condition $p^2-3q\ge0$ is exactly the non-negativity of the discriminant of the derivative.

**Lesson.** A relation between coefficients of a polynomial with real roots usually comes from a sum of squares of differences of the roots, via Vieta's formulas.
