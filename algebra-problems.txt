## Statement

Let $a_i,x_i,u_i\ge0$ for $i=1,\dots,n$. Prove Hölder's inequality
$$\left(\sum a_i^3\right)\left(\sum x_i^3\right)\left(\sum u_i^3\right)\ge\left(\sum a_ix_iu_i\right)^3.$$
Deduce that $(a^3+b^3+c^3)\cdot3\cdot3\ge(a+b+c)^3$ for $a,b,c\ge0$.

## Hint

Divide each sum by its total so that the three sums become $1$. Then apply AM-GM to $a_i^3,x_i^3,u_i^3$ (after normalisation) for each $i$ and add.

## Solution

**Idea.** Normalise, then use AM-GM on each triple.

**Normalisation.** Let $A=\sum a_i^3$, $X=\sum x_i^3$, $U=\sum u_i^3$. If one of them is $0$, both sides vanish. Otherwise, for each $i$ AM-GM on three non-negative numbers gives
$$\frac{a_i^3}A+\frac{x_i^3}X+\frac{u_i^3}U\ \ge\ 3\,\frac{a_ix_iu_i}{(AXU)^{1/3}}.$$

**Summation.** Summing over $i$, the left side is $3$, so
$$3\ge3\,\frac{\sum a_ix_iu_i}{(AXU)^{1/3}}\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum a_ix_iu_i\le(AXU)^{1/3},$$
which is the claim after cubing.

**Consequence.** Take $x_i=u_i=1$ and $n=3$: $(a^3+b^3+c^3)\cdot3\cdot3\ge(a+b+c)^3$, i.e. $a^3+b^3+c^3\ge\frac{(a+b+c)^3}9$.

**Remark.** Equality holds iff the three sequences $(a_i^3),(x_i^3),(u_i^3)$ are proportional.

**Lesson.** To prove a product-of-sums inequality, normalise every factor to $1$ and let AM-GM do the work term by term.

## Statement

For $a,b,c>0$ prove that
$$\frac{a^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}+\frac{b^3}{b^2+bc+c^2}+\frac{c^3}{c^2+ca+a^2}\ \ge\ \frac{a+b+c}3.$$

## Hint

Compare the sum with the "twin" sum $\sum\frac{b^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}$ and look at the difference of the two.

## Solution

**Idea.** Let $S=\sum\frac{a^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}$ and $T=\sum\frac{b^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}$, both over the cyclic pairs $(a,b),(b,c),(c,a)$.

**The difference vanishes.** Since $a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)$,
$$S-T=\sum(a-b)=(a-b)+(b-c)+(c-a)=0.$$
So $S=T=\dfrac{S+T}2$ and
$$S=\frac12\sum_{\rm cyc}\frac{a^3+b^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}.$$

**A pair estimate.** For $a,b>0$,
$$\frac{a^3+b^3}{a^2+ab+b^2}\ge\frac{a+b}3\iff3(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)\ge(a+b)(a^2+ab+b^2)\iff2(a-b)^2\ge0.$$

**Conclusion.** Summing the three pair estimates, $S\ge\frac12\cdot\frac{2(a+b+c)}3=\frac{a+b+c}3$, with equality iff $a=b=c$.

**Lesson.** When a cyclic sum has numerators and denominators that differ by a nice factor, add the "mirror" sum: the difference telescopes and the symmetrised sum is easier to bound.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$. Prove that
$$a^5+b^5+c^5\ \ge\ abc\,(a^2+b^2+c^2).$$

## Hint

Write $a^3bc=(a^5)^{3/5}(b^5)^{1/5}(c^5)^{1/5}$ and use weighted AM-GM.

## Solution

**Idea.** The exponent vector $(5,0,0)$ majorises $(3,1,1)$: each term $a^3bc$ is a weighted geometric mean of $a^5,b^5,c^5$.

**Weighted AM-GM.** With weights $\frac35,\frac15,\frac15$,
$$a^3bc=(a^5)^{3/5}(b^5)^{1/5}(c^5)^{1/5}\le\frac{3a^5+b^5+c^5}5.$$
The same holds for $b^3ca$ and $c^3ab$.

**Summation.**
$$abc(a^2+b^2+c^2)=a^3bc+b^3ca+c^3ab\le\frac{5(a^5+b^5+c^5)}5=a^5+b^5+c^5.$$
Equality iff $a=b=c$.

**Remark.** Muirhead's theorem says that $\sum_{\rm sym}a^{\alpha_1}b^{\alpha_2}c^{\alpha_3}\ge\sum_{\rm sym}a^{\beta_1}b^{\beta_2}c^{\beta_3}$ whenever $\alpha$ majorises $\beta$. Each instance can be proved exactly as above.

**Lesson.** An inequality between symmetric sums of monomials of the same degree is an application of weighted AM-GM: find the weights that turn the smaller monomial into a geometric mean of the larger ones.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c\ge0$ with $a+b+c=3$. Prove that
$$a^2b+b^2c+c^2a+abc\le4.$$
When does equality hold?

## Hint

The expression is only cyclic, so you cannot assume $a\ge b\ge c$, but you can assume that $b$ is the middle value. Compare with $b(a+c)^2$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Choose the variable in the middle and merge the other two.

**Reduction.** The expression is invariant under the cyclic shift $(a,b,c)\to(b,c,a)$, and one of the three numbers is between the other two, so we may assume $b$ is between $a$ and $c$. Then $(a-b)(b-c)\ge0$.

**Key identity.** Expanding,
$$b(a+c)^2-\left(a^2b+b^2c+c^2a+abc\right)=abc+bc^2-b^2c-c^2a=c(a-b)(b-c)\ \ge0.$$
(Check: $(a-b)(b-c)=ab-ac-b^2+bc$, and multiplying by $c$ gives $abc-ac^2-b^2c+bc^2$. Wait — this gives $-ac^2$ not $-c^2a$, which is the same term. ✓)

**AM-GM.** Hence the expression is at most $b(a+c)^2=4\cdot b\cdot\frac{a+c}2\cdot\frac{a+c}2\le4\left(\frac{b+(a+c)}3\right)^3=4$, since $b+(a+c)=3$.

**Equality.** We need $c(a-b)(b-c)=0$ and $b=\frac{a+c}2=1$. This gives $a=b=c=1$ or $c=0$, $b=1$, $a=2$ (and the cyclic shifts of the latter).

**Lesson.** For cyclic inequalities, "middle variable" arguments replace the symmetry you do not have: the sign of $(a-b)(b-c)$ gives the one extra inequality you need.

## Statement

Let $x,y,z\ge0$ with $x+y+z=1$. Prove that
$$0\le xy+yz+zx-2xyz\le\frac7{27}.$$

## Hint

Expand $(1-2x)(1-2y)(1-2z)$ and compare with $xy+yz+zx-2xyz$.

## Solution

**Notation.** Let $q=xy+yz+zx$ and $r=xyz$.

**Lower bound.** Since $x,y,z\le1$,
$$q-2r=\bigl[xy(1-z)+yz(1-x)+zx(1-y)\bigr]+r\ge0,$$
because $xy(1-z)+yz(1-x)+zx(1-y)=q-3r$.

**Upper bound.** Expanding, using $x+y+z=1$,
$$(1-2x)(1-2y)(1-2z)=1-2+4q-8r=-1+4q-8r,$$
so $q-2r=\dfrac{1+P}4$ with $P=(1-2x)(1-2y)(1-2z)$. We must show $P\le\frac1{27}$.
- Two of the factors cannot be negative: $1-2x<0$ and $1-2y<0$ would give $x+y>1$, so $z<0$.
- If exactly one factor is negative, the others are non-negative and $P\le0$.
- If none is negative, their sum is $3-2=1$ and AM-GM gives $P\le\left(\frac13\right)^3=\frac1{27}$.

In all cases $P\le\frac1{27}$, hence $q-2r\le\frac{28}{27\cdot4}=\frac7{27}$.

**Equality.** Upper bound: $x=y=z=\frac13$. Lower bound: e.g. $(1,0,0)$.

**Lesson.** Rewriting a symmetric expression through $p=x+y+z$, $q$, $r$ (the pqr method) can turn it into a product whose sign analysis and AM-GM finish the job.

## Statement

1. Prove that for real numbers $p_1,q_1,p_2,q_2$,
$$\sqrt{p_1^2+q_1^2}+\sqrt{p_2^2+q_2^2}\ \ge\ \sqrt{(p_1+p_2)^2+(q_1+q_2)^2}.$$
2. Find the minimum over $x\in\mathbb{R}$ of $\sqrt{x^2+4}+\sqrt{(6-x)^2+9}$ and the $x$ where it is reached.

## Hint

For part 1, interpret $(p_i,q_i)$ as vectors and expand the square of the right-hand side. For part 2, apply part 1 with well-chosen $p_i,q_i$.

## Solution

**1.** Let $u=(p_1,q_1)$ and $v=(p_2,q_2)$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $u\cdot v\le|u||v|$, so
$$|u+v|^2=|u|^2+2u\cdot v+|v|^2\le|u|^2+2|u||v|+|v|^2=(|u|+|v|)^2.$$
Taking square roots gives the inequality. Equality iff $u,v$ are parallel with the same direction.

**2.** Write the expression as $\sqrt{x^2+2^2}+\sqrt{(6-x)^2+3^2}$ and apply part 1 with $(p_1,q_1)=(x,2)$ and $(p_2,q_2)=(6-x,3)$:
$$\ge\sqrt{6^2+5^2}=\sqrt{61}.$$

**Equality.** The vectors $(x,2)$ and $(6-x,3)$ must be parallel with the same direction: $3x=2(6-x)$, i.e. $x=\frac{12}5>0$. So the minimum is $\sqrt{61}$, reached at $x=\frac{12}5$.

**Remark.** Geometrically, the expression is the length of the broken line from $(0,2)$ to $(x,0)$ to $(6,-3)$; it is shortest when the line is straight, of length $\sqrt{6^2+5^2}$.

**Lesson.** A sum of square roots of sums of two squares is a sum of lengths: think "triangle inequality", and choose the vectors so that the unknown cancels in the sum.

## Statement

Let $A,B,C$ be the angles of a triangle. Prove that
$$\sin A\sin B\sin C\le\frac{3\sqrt3}8.$$

## Hint

Take logarithms and show that $\ln\sin$ is concave on $(0,\pi)$.

## Solution

**Concavity.** On $(0,\pi)$ the function $g=\ln\sin$ has $g'=\cot$ and $g''=-\dfrac1{\sin^2}<0$, so $g$ is strictly concave.

**Jensen.** For the three angles with equal weights,
$$\frac{g(A)+g(B)+g(C)}3\le g\!\left(\frac{A+B+C}3\right)=g\!\left(\frac\pi3\right)=\ln\frac{\sqrt3}2.$$
Hence $\ln(\sin A\sin B\sin C)\le3\ln\frac{\sqrt3}2$, i.e.
$$\sin A\sin B\sin C\le\left(\frac{\sqrt3}2\right)^3=\frac{3\sqrt3}8.$$

**Equality.** By strict concavity, iff $A=B=C=\frac\pi3$.

**Remark.** The same method gives $\sin\frac A2\sin\frac B2\sin\frac C2\le\frac18$.

**Lesson.** A product under a fixed-sum constraint is a sum after taking logarithms: check the concavity of $\ln f$ and use Jensen.

## Statement

1. If $x_1,\dots,x_n>0$ and $x_1x_2\cdots x_n=1$, then $x_1+\dots+x_n\ge n$.
2. If $x_1,\dots,x_n>-1$ all have the same sign, then $(1+x_1)\cdots(1+x_n)\ge1+x_1+\dots+x_n$.

Deduce AM-GM from part 1.

## Hint

For part 1, among $n+1$ numbers with product $1$, one is $\ge1$ and one is $\le1$: merge them. For part 2, multiply the inductive inequality by $1+x_{n+1}>0$.

## Solution

**1.** For $n=1$, $x_1=1$. Assume it holds for $n$ numbers and take $x_1,\dots,x_{n+1}$ with product $1$. Not all are $<1$ and not all $>1$; reorder so that $x_n\ge1\ge x_{n+1}$. The $n$ numbers $x_1,\dots,x_{n-1},x_nx_{n+1}$ have product $1$, so by induction
$$x_1+\dots+x_{n-1}+x_nx_{n+1}\ge n.$$
Therefore
$$\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}x_i\ \ge\ n+x_n+x_{n+1}-x_nx_{n+1}=n+1+(x_n-1)(1-x_{n+1})\ \ge\ n+1.$$

**2.** For $n=1$ it is an equality. Suppose it holds for $n$, and let $S_n=x_1+\dots+x_n$. Since $1+x_{n+1}>0$,
$$\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}(1+x_i)\ge(1+x_{n+1})(1+S_n)=1+S_{n+1}+x_{n+1}S_n\ge1+S_{n+1},$$
because $x_{n+1}$ and $S_n$ have the same sign, so $x_{n+1}S_n\ge0$.

**AM-GM.** For positive $a_1,\dots,a_n$ with geometric mean $g$, set $x_i=a_i/g$: the product of the $x_i$ is $1$, so $\sum a_i/g\ge n$, i.e. $\frac{a_1+\dots+a_n}n\ge g$.

**Lesson.** Induction on the number of variables works when two variables can be merged into one while keeping the constraint: choose the pair on opposite sides of $1$.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c>0$. Prove that
$$\frac a{b+c}+\frac b{c+a}+\frac c{a+b}\ge\frac32.$$

## Hint

Write $\frac a{b+c}=\frac{a^2}{a(b+c)}$ and use Titu's lemma (Cauchy-Schwarz in Engel form): $\sum\frac{x_i^2}{y_i}\ge\frac{(\sum x_i)^2}{\sum y_i}$ for $y_i>0$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Put every fraction in the form $\frac{x^2}{y}$.

**Titu.**
$$\sum\frac a{b+c}=\sum\frac{a^2}{a(b+c)}\ge\frac{(a+b+c)^2}{\sum a(b+c)}=\frac{(a+b+c)^2}{2(ab+bc+ca)}.$$

**Last step.** $(a+b+c)^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+2(ab+bc+ca)\ge3(ab+bc+ca)$ because $a^2+b^2+c^2\ge ab+bc+ca$ (it is $\frac12\sum(a-b)^2\ge0$). Therefore

## Statement

Let $P$ be a real polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ all of whose $n$ complex roots (counted with multiplicity) are real. Prove that $P'$ also has all its $n-1$ roots real.

## Hint

Let $r_1<\dots<r_k$ be the distinct roots of $P$, with multiplicities $m_1,\dots,m_k$. First find how many roots of $P'$ sit at the points $r_i$ themselves, then look for roots between consecutive $r_i$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Count the roots of $P'$ that we can *see* and show that there are already $n-1=\deg P'$ of them.

**Roots at the $r_i$.** Write $P(x)=(x-r_i)^{m_i}Q(x)$ with $Q(r_i)\ne0$. Then $P'(x)=(x-r_i)^{m_i-1}\bigl(m_iQ(x)+(x-r_i)Q'(x)\bigr)$ and the second factor equals $m_iQ(r_i)\ne0$ at $r_i$. So $r_i$ is a root of $P'$ of multiplicity exactly $m_i-1$ (no root if $m_i=1$). Together these give $\sum_i(m_i-1)=n-k$ roots of $P'$.

**Roots between the $r_i$.** For $1\le i\le k-1$, $P(r_i)=P(r_{i+1})=0$, so by Rolle's theorem $P'$ vanishes at some point of the open interval $(r_i,r_{i+1})$. These $k-1$ points are distinct from each other and from all the $r_j$.

**Conclusion.** $P'$ has at least $(n-k)+(k-1)=n-1$ real roots counted with multiplicity. Since $\deg P'=n-1$, these are all its roots, and they are all real. $\blacksquare$

**Example.** $P(x)=x^3-3x+2=(x-1)^2(x+2)$: $P'(x)=3(x-1)(x+1)$, with the roots $1$ (from the double root) and $-1\in(-2,1)$ (from Rolle).

**Lesson.** Rolle's theorem counts roots: between two roots of $P$ there is a root of $P'$, and a multiple root of $P$ stays a root of $P'$. Count both and compare with the degree.

## Statement

Let $P$ be a polynomial of degree $d$ with leading coefficient $a$ and let $n\ge0$ be an integer. Prove:
$$\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nkP(k)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }d<n,\\(-1)^n\,n!\,a&\text{if }d=n.\end{cases}$$
Deduce that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^4(-1)^k\binom4kk^m=0$ for $m=0,1,2,3$ and $=24$ for $m=4$.

## Hint

Define the difference operator $(\Delta P)(x)=P(x+1)-P(x)$. What happens to the degree of $P$ when we apply $\Delta$?

## Solution

**Step 1: $\Delta$ lowers the degree by $1$.** If $P(x)=ax^d+\cdots$ with $d\ge1$, then $(x+1)^d-x^d=dx^{d-1}+\cdots$, so $\Delta P$ has degree $d-1$ and leading coefficient $da$. **Step 2: a formula for $\Delta^n$.** Let $E$ be the shift $(EP)(x)=P(x+1)$ and $I$ the identity. Then $\Delta=E-I$, and $E$, $I$ commute, so the binomial theorem applies:
$$\Delta^n=(E-I)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^{n-k}\binom nkE^k,\qquad\text{so}\qquad(\Delta^nP)(0)=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^{n-k}\binom nkP(k).$$

**Step 3.** Multiplying by $(-1)^n$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nkP(k)=(-1)^n(\Delta^nP)(0).$$
By Step 1 this is $0$ if $d<n$ and $(-1)^n\,n!\,a$ if $d=n$. $\blacksquare$

**Check.** For $P(x)=x^m$ with $n=4$: the sums for $m=0,\dots,4$ are $0,0,0,0,24$.

**Lesson.** $\Delta$ is "differentiation for sequences": it lowers the degree by one, and $n$ applications expand into an alternating binomial sum.

## Statement

Let $P$ be a non-constant polynomial with integer coefficients. Prove that there are infinitely many integers $n$ for which $|P(n)|$ is not a prime number.

## Hint

Take $n_0$ with $m=P(n_0)$ and $|m|\ge2$. For which $k$ is $P(n_0+km)$ divisible by $m$? Why can $P(n_0+km)$ not equal $\pm m$ for most $k$?

## Solution

**Remark.** Famous examples such as $n^2+n+41$ are prime for many consecutive $n$ (here $n=0,\dots,39$), but must fail eventually (at $n=41$, $P(41)=41\cdot43$).

2. Do the same for $s=\sqrt2+\sqrt3+\sqrt5$, with a polynomial of degree $8$.
3. Prove that $s$ is irrational.

## Hint

Isolate one square root and square, repeatedly. For part 3 use the rational root theorem: a rational root of a monic integer polynomial is an integer.

## Solution

**1.** Let $t=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$. Then $t^2=5+2\sqrt6$, so $(t^2-5)^2=24$, i.e.
$$t^4-10t^2+1=0.$$

**2.** Let $x=\sqrt2+\sqrt3+\sqrt5=t+\sqrt5$, so $t=x-\sqrt5$. Substituting:
$$(x-\sqrt5)^4-10(x-\sqrt5)^2+1=0.$$
Expand: $(x-\sqrt5)^4=x^4-4\sqrt5x^3+30x^2-20\sqrt5x+25$ and $-10(x-\sqrt5)^2=-10x^2+20\sqrt5x-50$. The terms in $x$ cancel and
$$x^4+20x^2-24=4\sqrt5\,x^3.$$
Squaring: $(x^4+20x^2-24)^2=80x^6$, that is
$$x^8-40x^6+352x^4-960x^2+576=0.$$
So $s$ is a root of this monic integer polynomial of degree $8$.

**3.** By the rational root theorem, a rational root of this monic polynomial is an integer. But $5.37<s<5.40$: $s$ lies strictly between $5$ and $6$ and is not an integer. Hence $s$ is irrational. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The degree $8$ is the best possible: the $8$ numbers $\pm\sqrt2\pm\sqrt3\pm\sqrt5$ are the roots of this polynomial.

**Lesson.** To find a polynomial killing a sum of square roots, eliminate the radicals one at a time.

## Statement

Let $a,b,c$ be complex numbers with $|a|=|b|=|c|=1$.

1. Prove that $|ab+bc+ca|=|a+b+c|$.

**1.** Since $\bar a=1/a$, etc.,
$$\overline{a+b+c}=\frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c=\frac{ab+bc+ca}{abc}.$$

## Statement

$$\prod_{k=1}^n\cos\frac{k\pi}N=\frac1{2^n}.$$

## Hint

Multiply by $\prod\sin\frac{k\pi}N$ and use $\sin2x=2\sin x\cos x$. Then show that $\{\sin\frac{2k\pi}N\}_{k\le n}$ is a rearrangement of $\{\sin\frac{j\pi}N\}_{j\le n}$ using $\sin x=\sin(\pi-x)$.

## Solution

**Idea.** Doubling the angle turns the cosine product into a sine product that can be compared with the original sine product.

**Step 1.** Let $S=\prod_{k=1}^n\sin\frac{k\pi}N>0$ and $C=\prod_{k=1}^n\cos\frac{k\pi}N$. Then
$$\prod_{k=1}^n\sin\frac{2k\pi}N=2^nSC.$$

2. $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n\frac k{(k+1)!}$,

## Statement

Let $P$ be a real polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ all of whose roots are real. Prove that for every real $x$,
$$P'(x)^2\ge P(x)\,P''(x),$$
with strict inequality when $x$ is not a root of $P$.

## Hint

If $P(x)=c\prod(x-r_i)$ and $x$ is not a root, compute $\dfrac{P'}P$ and then its derivative.

## Solution

**Idea.** The logarithmic derivative $\frac{P'}P=\sum\frac1{x-r_i}$ is a sum of terms whose derivatives are all negative.

**Away from the roots.** Let $P(x)=c\prod_{i=1}^n(x-r_i)$ with real $r_i$ and $c\ne0$. For $x\notin\{r_i\}$,
$$\frac{P'(x)}{P(x)}=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{x-r_i}.$$
Differentiating,
$$\frac{P''(x)P(x)-P'(x)^2}{P(x)^2}=-\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{(x-r_i)^2}<0.$$
Multiplying by $P(x)^2>0$: $P'(x)^2-P(x)P''(x)=P(x)^2\sum_i\frac1{(x-r_i)^2}>0$.

**At a root.** If $P(x)=0$ then $P(x)P''(x)=0\le P'(x)^2$. $\blacksquare$

**Example.** For $P(x)=x^2-1$: $P'^2-PP''=2x^2+2>0$ ✓. For $P=x^2+1$ (no real roots): $4x^2-2(x^2+1)=2x^2-2<0$ at $x=0$, so the hypothesis cannot be dropped.

**Lesson.** The logarithmic derivative turns a product into a sum, and its derivative $-\sum\frac1{(x-r_i)^2}$ has a definite sign when the roots are real.
